### 32.7.5.1 Exact Cardinality of the Finite Projective Circle $C(\mathbb{QP}^1_{\le M})$, Extremal Farey Chord Gaps, and the Asymptotic Shadow of $\pi^2$ {#sec-ch39-35-5-1-farey-projective-gaps}

For any positive integer $M \ge 1$, take all primitive integer pairs $(p, q)$ satisfying $\max(\lvert p\rvert, \lvert q\rvert) \le M$ and $q > 0$, together with the single antipodal representative $(1, 0)$, as the canonical representative set of the bounded-horizon projective line $\mathbb{QP}^1_{\le M}$; write $\Phi(M) = \sum_{j=1}^M \varphi(j)$ (with $\varphi(1) = 1$) for the totient summatory function. The rational circle map $C([p : q]) = \bigl(\frac{q^2 - p^2}{p^2 + q^2},\, \frac{2pq}{p^2 + q^2}\bigr)$ sends $[1 : 0]$ to $(-1, 0)$ and, away from $(-1, 0)$, has exact inverse $[y : 1 + x]$, giving a bijection from the rational projective line onto the rational unit circle. For any two nonzero integer pairs with $C_i = C([p_i : q_i])$ and determinant $\Delta = p_2 q_1 - p_1 q_2$, direct expansion yields the exact rational squared chord and spread identities:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert C_1 - C_2 \rVert^2 &= \frac{4\Delta^2}{(p_1^2 + q_1^2)(p_2^2 + q_2^2)}, \\
s(C_1, C_2) &= \frac{1 - C_1 \cdot C_2}{2} = \frac{\Delta^2}{(p_1^2 + q_1^2)(p_2^2 + q_2^2)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

On the closed first-quadrant arc, reduced slopes form the Farey sequence of order $M$ in $[0, 1]$, containing $\Phi(M) + 1$ points; the symmetries $p \mapsto -p$ and $p \leftrightarrow q$ produce four closed arcs meeting only at the $4$ coordinate-axis intercepts, so the circle holds exactly $4(\Phi(M) + 1) - 4 = 4\Phi(M)$ points. For any reduced $p/q < 1$ on the first arc, since $\gcd(p, q) = 1$ and $q \le M$, the class $pb \equiv -1 \pmod q$ is nonempty in $\{1, \dots, M\}$; taking its largest positive integer $b \le M$ (for $0/1$, $b = M$) gives $a = (1 + pb)/q \le b$ with $aq - pb = 1$ (so $(a, b)$ is primitive) and $b + q > M$. For any strictly intermediate fraction $r/t$, the positive integers $qr - pt \ge 1$ and $at - br \ge 1$ satisfy $t = b(qr - pt) + q(at - br) \ge b + q > M$, so every one of the $4\Phi(M)$ cyclic edges obeys $\lvert\Delta\rvert = 1$.

On the first arc, $(0/1, 1/M)$ has $(p^2 + q^2)(a^2 + b^2)$ equal to $M^2 + 1$, whereas every other adjacent edge $(p/q, a/b)$ has positive numerators and $qb \ge q + b - 1 \ge M$, forcing $(p^2 + q^2)(a^2 + b^2) \ge (q^2 + 1)(b^2 + 1) > M^2 + 1$, so for all $M \ge 1$ the maximum adjacent squared chord gap is $\frac{4}{M^2 + 1}$ with multiplicity $4$ (at $[0 : 1], [\pm 1 : M]$ and $[1 : 0], [\pm M : 1]$). For $M \ge 3$, adjacent denominators cannot both equal $M$ (else $M \mid \Delta$); denominator $M$ gives squared norm at most $A = 2M^2 - 2M + 1$ (uniquely at $(M - 1)/M$), denominator $\le M - 1$ gives at most $B = 2M^2 - 6M + 5 < A$ (uniquely at $(M - 2)/(M - 1)$, covering $1/1$ since $2 < 5 \le B$), and when both denominators are $\le M - 1$ their product is at most $B^2 < AB$; because $(M - 2)/(M - 1)$ and $(M - 1)/M$ satisfy $(M - 1)^2 - M(M - 2) = 1$ with denominator sum $2M - 1 > M$, they are adjacent and with their three symmetry images uniquely realize the minimum adjacent squared chord gap with multiplicity $4$ (at $M = 1$ all four equal $2$, while at $M = 2$ the first-arc edges $(0/1, 1/2)$ and $(1/2, 1/1)$ give $\frac{4}{5}$ and $\frac{2}{5}$, so the minimum is $\frac{2}{5}$ with multiplicity $4$); writing $C_1 \sim C_2$ for cyclically adjacent pairs gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\max_{C_1 \sim C_2} \lVert C_1 - C_2 \rVert^2 &= \frac{4}{M^2 + 1} \quad (M \ge 1), \\
\min_{C_1 \sim C_2} \lVert C_1 - C_2 \rVert^2 &= \frac{4}{(2M^2 - 2M + 1)(2M^2 - 6M + 5)} \quad (M \ge 3).
\end{aligned}
$$

Accordingly, on the $M = 6$ circle grid drawn in the first card and at the default horizon $M = 12$ of the second card, the projective circle contains exactly $48$ and $184$ points respectively; at $M = 12$, the maximum and minimum cyclically adjacent squared chord gaps are $\frac{4}{145}$ and $\frac{4}{58565}$. The exact rational density of canonical representatives in $[-M, M]^2$ is $\frac{4\Phi(M)}{(2M + 1)^2}$ (at $M = 12$, $\frac{184}{625} = 0.2944$), which converges as $M \to \infty$ by the classical asymptotic $\Phi(M) \sim \frac{3M^2}{\pi^2}$ (T. M. Apostol, *Introduction to Analytic Number Theory*, 1976, Theorem 3.7, [10.1007/978-1-4757-5579-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4757-5579-4)) to $\frac{3}{\pi^2}$ (rounding from the archived enclosure to $0.303964$)—showing $\pi^2$ here purely as an asymptotic shadow of finite coprime counting.

For continued-fraction termination, with Fibonacci numbers $F_0 = 0, F_1 = 1$ and every $k \ge 2$, the relations $F_k / F_{k+1} \in (0, 1)$ and $F_{j+1}/F_j = 1 + F_{j-1}/F_j$ ($j = k, \dots, 3$, ending at $F_3/F_2 = 2$) give the standard expansion $[0; 1, \dots, 1, 2]$ ($k - 2$ copies of $1$), yielding exactly $k$ convergents (from $0/1$ through $F_k / F_{k+1}$); for example, at $k = 12$, $144/233$ has length $12$, whereas [@thm-ch39-math-omega-finite-rational-anchors] ([`convergents_length_log`](lean:D5/S1/Depth/ContinuedFractions/FiniteRationalConvergentsLength.convergents_length_log)) gives $2\lfloor\log_2 233\rfloor + 1 = 15$, a ratio of $\frac{4}{5}$.

### 32.7.5.2 Exact Cayley Composition Law, Two-Step Half-Turn Factorization, and the Taylor Polynomial Sphere-Preservation Barrier {#sec-ch39-35-5-2-cayley-composition-taylor-barrier}

Work with column vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, right-handed cross product $X\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol{\sigma} \times \mathbf{v}$, $u = \lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}\rVert^2$, and quaternion product $(s_1, \mathbf{v}_1)(s_2, \mathbf{v}_2) = (s_1 s_2 - \mathbf{v}_1\cdot\mathbf{v}_2,\, s_1\mathbf{v}_2 + s_2\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_1\times\mathbf{v}_2)$. For any nonzero real quaternion $q = (d, \mathbf{v}) \neq (0, \mathbf{0})$ and pure vector $x = (0, \mathbf{w})$, first $(d, \mathbf{v})(0, \mathbf{w}) = (-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w},\, d\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w})$, and multiplying on the right by $(d, -\mathbf{v})$ cancels the scalar part to $-d(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}) + d(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{v}) = 0$ while giving vector part $d^2\mathbf{w} + 2d(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}) + (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w})\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{v}\times(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w})$; substituting $\mathbf{v}\times(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{w}) = (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w})\mathbf{v} - \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2\mathbf{w}$ and dividing by $d^2 + \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2$ shows that conjugation $x \mapsto q x q^{-1}$ acts by the scale-invariant matrix $\Psi(d, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{(d^2 - \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2)\mathbf{I} + 2d[\mathbf{v}]_\times + 2\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^\top}{d^2 + \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2}$, which at $d = 1$ and $\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}$ equals $\mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})$ and satisfies $\Psi(q_1 q_2) = \Psi(q_1)\Psi(q_2)$. Apply $\Psi$ to $(1, \mathbf{a})(1, \mathbf{b}) = (1 - \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b},\, \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})$: when $1 - \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} \neq 0$, normalizing by the scalar part proves the **Rodrigues–Cayley rational composition law** (with exact cross-product sign $+1$; J. Solà, *Quaternion kinematics for the error-state Kalman filter*, 2017, Section 2.5, [arXiv:1711.02508v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.02508v1)), whereas when the quaternion product is purely imaginary, $(0, \mathbf{v})$, evaluating $\Psi(0, \mathbf{v})$ directly yields the **two-step rational half-turn factorization** for any rational axis:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{a})\,\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{b}) &= \mathbf{R}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}}{1 - \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}\right) \quad (1 - \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} \neq 0), \\
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{n})\,\mathbf{R}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{n}}{\lVert\mathbf{n}\rVert^2}\right) &= \frac{2\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^\top}{\lVert\mathbf{n}\rVert^2} - \mathbf{I} = \mathbf{R}_\pi(\mathbf{n}).
\end{aligned}
$$

For instance, the rational parameters $\mathbf{a} = (\frac{1}{2}, 0, 0)^\top$ and $\mathbf{b} = (0, \frac{1}{3}, 0)^\top$ of two non-commuting rotations compose directly to $(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{6})^\top$; and for any nonzero axis $\mathbf{n} \in \mathbb{Q}^3 \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$, the product $(1, \mathbf{n})(1, \mathbf{n}/\lVert\mathbf{n}\rVert^2) = \bigl(0,\, (1 + 1/\lVert\mathbf{n}\rVert^2)\mathbf{n}\bigr)$ has zero scalar part, so the resulting half-turn matrix satisfies $\mathbf{R}_\pi(\mathbf{n})^\top = \mathbf{R}_\pi(\mathbf{n})$, $\mathbf{R}_\pi(\mathbf{n})^2 = \mathbf{I}$, $\det\mathbf{R}_\pi(\mathbf{n}) = 1$, $\operatorname{Tr}\mathbf{R}_\pi(\mathbf{n}) = -1$, and $\det(\mathbf{R}_\pi(\mathbf{n}) + \mathbf{I}) = 0$ (e.g., the squared-norm-$5$ integer axis $\mathbf{n} = (1, 2, 0)^\top$ from the six-axis sphere cover factors cleanly as $\mathbf{R}(1, 2, 0)\,\mathbf{R}(\frac{1}{5}, \frac{2}{5}, 0)$).

By contrast, consider the $k$-th Taylor truncation $T_k(X) = \sum_{j=0}^k X^j / j!$ ($k \ge 1$; N. J. Higham, *Functions of Matrices: Theory and Computation*, 2008, Chapter 10, [10.1137/1.9780898717778](https://doi.org/10.1137/1.9780898717778)): at $\boldsymbol{\sigma} = \mathbf{0}$ one trivially has $T_k(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{I}$, whereas for any nonzero generator ($u = \lVert\boldsymbol{\sigma}\rVert^2 > 0$), on the two-dimensional invariant orthogonal plane $\boldsymbol{\sigma}^\perp$ we have $X^2 = -u\mathbf{I}$, so $T_k(X)$ reduces to $A_k(u)\mathbf{I} + B_k(u)X$ with $A_k(u) = \sum_{2j \le k} \frac{(-u)^j}{(2j)!}$ and $B_k(u) = \sum_{2j+1 \le k} \frac{(-u)^j}{(2j+1)!}$. Because $X^\top = -X$, cross terms cancel in $T_k(X)^\top T_k(X)$, multiplying the squared norm of every $\mathbf{v} \perp \boldsymbol{\sigma}$ by the exact rational polynomial $M_k(u) = A_k(u)^2 + u B_k(u)^2 = \bigl\lvert\sum_{j=0}^k \frac{(i\sqrt{u})^j}{j!}\bigr\rvert^2$ (while fixing the axis $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$). Writing $p_k(z) = \sum_{j=0}^k z^j / j!$, for every $1 \le m \le k$, $p_k(z)p_k(-z)$ has $z^m$ coefficient $\frac{(1 - 1)^m}{m!} = 0$; setting $z = i\sqrt{u}$ shows that $M_k(u) - 1 = p_k(i\sqrt{u})p_k(-i\sqrt{u}) - 1$ is divisible by $u^{\lfloor k/2 \rfloor + 1}$ with quotient of degree $\lfloor(k - 1)/2\rfloor$ and leading coefficient $\frac{1}{(k!)^2} > 0$, so on $u > 0$ it has at most $\lfloor(k - 1)/2\rfloor$ roots—hence **every positive-order Taylor truncation fails to preserve the Euclidean norm of all vectors on any open interval of positive generator radii**, standing in structural contrast to the rational Cayley map's exact identity $\bigl\lvert\frac{1 + i\sqrt{u}}{1 - i\sqrt{u}}\bigr\rvert^2 \equiv 1$. ### 32.7.5.3 Arbitrary-Order Rational Jet Recurrence, Four-Step Equatorial Scaling Law, and the Finite-Difference Secant-Error Hyperbola {#sec-ch39-35-5-3-integer-jets-secant-locus}

To extend the second-order jet ring $\mathbb{Q}[\eta]/(\eta^3)$ to arbitrary order $N \ge 0$ in $\mathbb{Q}(x)[\eta]/(\eta^{N+1})$, take the fidelity function $\kappa(x) = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}$ with $a = 1 + x^2$, solve the finite inversion congruence $(a + 2x\eta + \eta^2)\sum_{m=0}^N c_m \eta^m \equiv 1 \pmod{\eta^{N+1}}$ with $c_m = \frac{P_m(x)}{(1 + x^2)^{m+1}}$, and equate coefficients of $\eta^0, \dots, \eta^N$; this immediately yields an $N$-independent recurrence for polynomials with integer coefficients ($P_m \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$; upon specializing any $x \in \mathbb{Q}$, the fractions $c_m = \frac{P_m(x)}{(1 + x^2)^{m+1}}$ give the exact rational Taylor coefficients in $\mathbb{Q}[\eta]/(\eta^{N+1})$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_0(x) &= 1, \\
P_1(x) &= -2x, \\
P_{m+1}(x) &= -2x\,P_m(x) - (1 + x^2) P_{m-1}(x) \quad (m \ge 1).
\end{aligned}
$$

The next terms are $P_2(x) = 3x^2 - 1$, $P_3(x) = 4x(1 - x^2)$, and $P_4(x) = 5x^4 - 10x^2 + 1$, generating rational Taylor coefficients to arbitrary order without limits. At the Present equator $x = 1$, the characteristic polynomial $T^2 + 2T + 2$ divides $T^4 + 4$ (its two complex roots $t = -1 + i$ and $\bar{t} = -1 - i$ satisfy $t^4 = -4$), proving for every $m \ge 0$ the four-step scaling laws $P_{m+4}(1) = -4 P_m(1)$ and $c_{m+4}(1) = -\frac{1}{4} c_m(1)$; from the four initial values $P_0(1), P_1(1), P_2(1), P_3(1) = 1, -2, 2, 0$, it follows immediately that $P_m(1) = 0$ if and only if $m \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. Meanwhile, for any $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and $h \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$, subtracting the exact derivative $\kappa'(x) = -\frac{2x}{(1 + x^2)^2}$ from the forward finite-difference slope and combining over a common denominator gives the exact secant-error identity:

$$
E(x, h) \;=\; \frac{\kappa(x + h) - \kappa(x)}{h} - \kappa'(x) \;=\; \frac{h\bigl(3x^2 + 2xh - 1\bigr)}{(1 + x^2)^2\bigl(1 + (x + h)^2\bigr)}.
$$

For $x \neq 0$, setting $h_* = \frac{1 - 3x^2}{2x}$ gives error sign $\operatorname{sgn}\bigl(hx(h - h_*)\bigr)$ (and at $x = 0$, $-\operatorname{sgn}(h)$). Whenever $x > 0$, $h > 0$, and $3x^2 \ge 1$, the secant error is strictly positive, $E(x, h) > 0$ (accounting for the positive bias $\frac{5}{82}$ in [@ex-ch39-math-omega-quantitative-audit], rounding from the archived enclosure to $0.060976$; the positive-branch condition $x > 0$ cannot be omitted, as $E(-1, 2) = -\frac{1}{2}$ is negative). By contrast, on the hyperbola $3x^2 + 2xh = 1$ (with $h \neq 0$, forcing $x \neq 0$), letting $c = \operatorname{sgn}(x)/\sqrt{3}$ ($3c^2 = 1$) denote the same-sign inflection point and substituting $h = \frac{1 - 3x^2}{2x}$ yields $(x - c)(x + h - c) = -\frac{(x - c)^2(x + 3c)}{2x} < 0$ (since $x \neq c$ and $c/x > 0$), proving that every point on either branch puts $x$ and $x + h$ strictly on opposite sides of $c$; alongside $(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4})$ (see [@ex-ch39-math-omega-quantitative-audit]), the positive-branch pair $(\frac{1}{3}, 1)$ also gives $E(x, h) = 0$ at finite step size.

### 32.7.5.4 Coarse-Mesh Simplicial Vertex-Cone Homotopy and Obstruction-Free Potential Splitting {#sec-ch39-35-5-4-simplicial-cone-homotopy}

Finally, to see why a closed $2$-cochain on a full simplex admits an explicit coarse-scale potential without mesh refinement, take the full simplex $K_n$ (on ordered vertices $\{0, \dots, n - 1\}$) with alternating rational cochains $C^j(K_n; \mathbb{Q})$ indexed by increasing $(j + 1)$-tuples (C. A. Weibel, *An Introduction to Homological Algebra*, 1994, Section 1.4, [10.1017/CBO9781139644136](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139644136)). Define the coboundary $D_j : C^j \to C^{j+1}$ as the alternating sum $(D_j \omega)(v_0, \dots, v_{j+1}) = \sum_{i=0}^{j+1} (-1)^i \omega(v_0, \dots, \widehat{v_i}, \dots, v_{j+1})$ (deleting vertex pairs in opposite orders cancels, so $D_2 D_1 = 0$), and define the vertex-$0$ cone homotopy $H_j : C^j \to C^{j-1}$ ($j \ge 1$): on faces omitting $0$, set $(H_j \omega)(v_0, \dots, v_{j-1}) = \omega(0, v_0, \dots, v_{j-1})$, and $0$ otherwise. For $n \le 2$ there are no triangles ($C^2 = 0$), while for $n = 3$ we have $D_2 = 0$ and the single triangle $(0, 1, 2)$ directly obeys $F = D_1(H_2 F)$; for $n \ge 4$, expanding on any triangle $(a, b, c)$ omitting $0$ gives $(D_1 H_2 F)(a, b, c) = F(0, b, c) - F(0, a, c) + F(0, a, b)$ and $(H_3 D_2 F)(a, b, c) = F(a, b, c) - F(0, b, c) + F(0, a, c) - F(0, a, b)$, whereas on $(0, b, c)$ the first term equals $F(0, b, c)$ and the second vanishes—establishing directly for every $n \ge 4$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_1 H_2 + H_3 D_2 &= I, \\
\operatorname{rank} D_1 &= \binom{n - 1}{2}, \\
\operatorname{rank} D_2 &= \binom{n - 1}{3}, \\
\beta_2(K_n) &= 0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Accordingly, every closed $2$-cochain ($D_2 F = 0$) admits the explicit edge potential $H_2 F$ with $F = D_1(H_2 F)$ at zero subdivision, and every general $2$-cochain $F$ splits into an exact part $D_1 H_2 F$ (agreeing on triangles containing $0$ with $F$) plus a curvature-cone part $H_3 D_2 F$ (vanishing on triangles containing $0$, and applying $D_2$ to the split via $D_2 D_1 = 0$ shows its coboundary is $D_2 F$); this vertex-$0$-normalized split is unique, since any cocycle $C$ vanishing on triangles containing $0$ has $H_2 C = 0$ and hence $C = D_1 H_2 C + H_3 D_2 C = 0$. Likewise, extending arbitrary values on triangles containing $0$ to $G$ and taking $D_1 H_2 G$ yields a cocycle preserving those values, so restriction to triangles containing $0$ gives an isomorphism $\ker D_2 = \operatorname{im} D_1 \cong \mathbb{Q}^{\binom{n - 1}{2}}$, whence rank-nullity gives the rank formulas above and $\beta_2(K_n) = 0$.
