For $U \in \mathrm{SU}(2)$ the similarity part is already the real scalar $\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}U$ times the identity and no reading is needed; for general $U \in \mathrm{U}(2)$ and for $N \ge 3$ it is.
2. Let the eigen-angles of $U$ be $\theta_j \in (-\pi, \pi]$. From $\frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{24} \le 1 - \cos x \le \frac{x^2}{2}$, and $1 - \cos x \ge \frac{2x^2}{\pi^2}$ for $\lvert x\rvert \le \pi$,

   $$
   \frac{2}{\pi^2}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_j\theta_j^2 \;\le\; \varphi(v) \;\le\; \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_j\theta_j^2, \qquad \varphi(v) \;\ge\; \frac{1}{2N}\sum_j\theta_j^2 - \frac{1}{24N}\sum_j\theta_j^4.
   $$

3. The commutator holonomy $h = u_1u_2u_1^\dagger u_2^\dagger$ of two fair elements satisfies $h - \mathbf{1} = [u_1, u_2]\,(u_2u_1)^\dagger$ (take $U = u_1$, $f = u_2$, $\mathit{translated} = u_1u_2u_1^\dagger$ in [`covariant_commutator_formula`](lean:D5/S3/Quantum/Algebra/CovariantCommutator.covariant_commutator_formula), then multiply on the right by $(u_2u_1)^\dagger$), so $\varphi(v(h)) = \frac{1}{2}\varphi\bigl([u_1, u_2]^\dagger[u_1, u_2]\bigr)$: at finite aperture, the sagitta of the curvature is half the squared norm of the commutator.
4. Let the loop $C$ be the boundary of an $R \times T$ rectangular grid, traversed counterclockwise from the origin, with the link variables along a path multiplied from left to right in the order they are passed. Write $h_{i,j}$ and $w_{i,j}$ for the link variables from $(i, j)$ to $(i + 1, j)$ and to $(i, j + 1)$, $P_{i,j} = h_{i,j}w_{i+1,j}h_{i,j+1}^{-1}w_{i,j}^{-1}$ for the counterclockwise plaquette holonomy based at the lower-left corner, $b_i = h_{0,0}\cdots h_{i-1,0}$, $V_{i,J} = w_{i,0}\cdots w_{i,J-1}$ and $\ell_{i,j} = (b_iV_{i,j})P_{i,j}(b_iV_{i,j})^{-1}$. Induction on $J$ gives $\ell_{i,0}\ell_{i,1}\cdots\ell_{i,J-1} = b_i\,h_{i,0}V_{i+1,J}h_{i,J}^{-1}V_{i,J}^{-1}\,b_i^{-1}$ (at each step only $V_{i,J}^{-1}V_{i,J}$ and $h_{i,J}^{-1}h_{i,J}$ cancel); call this product $C_i$ when $J = T$. Since $b_ih_{i,0} = b_{i+1}$, the middle factor $V_{i,T}^{-1}b_i^{-1}b_iV_{i,T}$ of $C_iC_{i-1}$ cancels, and telescoping over the columns gives $W_C = C_{R-1}C_{R-2}\cdots C_0$: columns from right to left, and within a column from bottom to top. The factorization holds in any group; the order is part of the statement, and multiplying the columns from left to right in general gives a different element. On a general disc $W_C$ is likewise the product, in shelling order, of the $M$ face holonomies conjugated along anchor paths (the non-abelian surface Stokes identity of [§34.30.3](ch34-s30-arc-body-calculus-and-finite-stokes.md#sec-ch34-30-3), `Literature`). In both cases (with $M = RT$ for the grid), the norm $\lVert X\rVert_\varphi := \sqrt{\varphi(X^\dagger X)}$ is invariant under left and right multiplication by unitaries, and $ab - \mathbf{1} = (a - \mathbf{1})b + (b - \mathbf{1})$, so $\lVert W_C - \mathbf{1}\rVert_\varphi \le \sum_p\lVert U_p - \mathbf{1}\rVert_\varphi$, i.e. $\sqrt{\varphi(v(W_C))} \le \sum_p\sqrt{\varphi(v(U_p))}$; by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $1 - \frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}W_C \le M\sum_p\bigl(1 - \frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}U_p\bigr)$. A $\mathrm{U}(1)$ configuration with the same small flux through every plaquette makes the ratio of the two sides of each inequality tend to $1$, so the factor $M$ cannot be dropped.
5. For a smooth classical field, with link variables the path-ordered exponentials along the links, or $e^{iaA_\mu}$ at link midpoints, expanding about the centre of the plaquette gives $1 - \frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}U_p = \frac{a^4}{2N}\operatorname{Tr}F_{\mu\nu}^2 + O(a^6)$ with $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu + i[A_\mu, A_\nu]$ (no sum; $\mu\nu$ is the plane of the plaquette).

On a triangular face, third-point redundancy ([@def-appe-third-point-redundancy]) holds exactly when the face holonomy is $\mathbf{1}$, i.e. when its sagitta reading vanishes; carrying the same condition over to a plaquette (face holonomy $\mathbf{1}$), $S_W/\beta$ is the total sagitta of the failures of that condition. This is a kinematic rewriting only: the statistical weight, the measure, the values of the couplings and the quantum continuum limit are not supplied by it (for the last, see [@prob-ch34-rough-nonabelian-surface-calculus]).
:::

On the gravitational side the holonomy is read differently:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-regge-deficit-signed-angle title="The Regge deficit reads the signed angle, not a sum of sagittas (manual derivation and literature result)"}
**Manual derivation; the Regge action and its convergence theorem, Descartes' total-defect theorem and the polar-triangle theorem are literature results, not formalized in this book.** The holonomy around a hinge $h$ is a rotation $R_h$ by the deficit angle $\delta_h$ (Regge 1961, `Literature`). In three dimensions ($R_h \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$; in dimension $D$ replace $\operatorname{Tr}R_h - 1$ by $\operatorname{Tr}R_h - (D - 2)$), with $K_h$ the unit generator of the plane of rotation, $\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{Tr}R_h - 1) = \cos\delta_h$ and $-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(K_hR_h) = \sin\delta_h$. Reversing the loop leaves the first unchanged and flips the sign of the second.

1. If the gauge group acts irreducibly on $\mathbb{C}^N$ (such as $\mathrm{SU}(N)$ in its defining representation), the only single-loop reading that is real-linear in the holonomy matrix, gauge invariant and independent of the orientation of the loop is the sagitta, up to an affine change; the gauge term is of this kind.
2. The Regge action $\sum_hA_h\delta_h$ is of first order in the deficit: it reads the signed angle and needs a partner that changes sign with the loop, namely the plane dual to the hinge. It is not a sum of sagittas: when hinge measures scale as $a^{D-2}$ and deficits as $a^2$ ($D$ the dimension of the complex, the number of hinges scaling as $a^{-D}$), the sum $\sum_hA_h(1 - \cos\delta_h)$ tends to zero like $a^2$ under refinement.
3. On a closed convex polyhedron $\sum_v\delta_v = 4\pi$ whatever the shape (the $720^\circ$ statement; Descartes' total-defect theorem, see item 2 of [@rem-hairy-ball-adams-literature]), whereas $\sum_v(1 - \cos\delta_v)$ depends on the shape.
4. At a trihedral vertex the deficit equals the Girard excess of the spherical triangle spanned by the three outward face normals $n_1, n_2, n_3$ (the polar-triangle theorem), so the closed form of [@rem-appe-half-comparison-holonomy] gives $\delta_v = 2\operatorname{atan2}\bigl(\lvert[n_1, n_2, n_3]\rvert,\ 1 + n_1\cdot n_2 + n_2\cdot n_3 + n_3\cdot n_1\bigr)$; when the denominator is positive this is $\tan\frac{\delta_v}{2} = \frac{\lvert[n_1, n_2, n_3]\rvert}{1 + n_1\cdot n_2 + n_2\cdot n_3 + n_3\cdot n_1}$. Write $S = 1 + n_1\cdot n_2 + n_2\cdot n_3 + n_3\cdot n_1$ and $D = [n_1, n_2, n_3]$. When no two of the normals are antipodal, the identity $S^2 + D^2 = 2\prod_{i<j}(1 + n_i\cdot n_j)$ and the return phase $(S - iD)/\sqrt{S^2 + D^2}$ of the three rotor gates are frozen in the Pauli-matrix model ([@thm-appe-bloch-triangle-return-phase]). Identifying the argument of this phase with half the excess, and the excess with the vertex deficit, remains a literature result.

The precise content of "all forces in one arc" is therefore this: both kinds of force are described by how far a loop holonomy departs from the identity, and both obey the same Bianchi and Stokes identities; the gauge term reads the even channel, and the gravity term reads the signed angle (its sign fixed by the odd channel), weighted by the measure of the hinge. Treating this as the structural difference between gauge forces and gravity is a reading of this book (`MODEL`); it fixes neither the gauge group, nor the couplings, nor the dynamics.
:::

The hinge plane itself can also be read off the holonomy:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-hinge-plane-chord-and-sign title="The hinge plane, the chord and the sign of the deficit (manual derivation and literature result)"}
**Manual derivation; that the holonomy around a hinge of a piecewise flat manifold fixes the hinge pointwise (Regge 1961), and the total-deficit formula $\sum_v\delta_v = 2\pi\chi$ on closed polyhedral surfaces, are literature results, not formalized in this book.** Let $R \in \mathrm{SO}(D)$, $R \ne \mathbf{1}$, fix a $(D - 2)$-dimensional subspace pointwise (equivalently $\operatorname{rank}(R - \mathbf{1}) = 2$), with chord $\Delta = R - \mathbf{1}$.

1. $\Pi = \operatorname{im}\Delta$ is a 2-plane, and $R$ is the identity on $\Pi^\perp$. Choosing an orientation of $\Pi$ with unit generator $K$ ($K^2 = -P_\Pi$), there is a unique $\delta \in (-\pi, \pi]$ with $R = \mathbf{1} + \sin\delta\,K + (1 - \cos\delta)K^2$. The even channel $S = \frac{1}{2}(R + R^{\mathsf{T}}) = \mathbf{1} - (1 - \cos\delta)P_\Pi$ gives the unoriented plane $P_\Pi$ and $\cos\delta$; the odd channel $A = \frac{1}{2}(R - R^{\mathsf{T}}) = \sin\delta\,K$ gives the orientation of the plane and the sign of $\delta$, namely $-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}(KR) = \sin\delta$ of [@rem-ch39-regge-deficit-signed-angle]. Two rotations of the same plane with deficits $+90^\circ$ and $-90^\circ$ have the same even channel and the same chord length.
2. $\lVert\Delta\rVert_{\mathrm{op}} = 2\lvert\sin\frac{\delta}{2}\rvert$, so $\lvert\delta\rvert = 2\arcsin\frac{\lVert\Delta\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}{2} = \lVert\Delta\rVert_{\mathrm{op}} + \frac{1}{24}\lVert\Delta\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^3 + \cdots$: the size of the angle in the Regge term is a function of the chord length and is of the same order as the chord for small angles, while the Wilson term $1 - \frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}U = \frac{1}{2}\lVert U - \mathbf{1}\rVert_\varphi^2$ of [@rem-ch39-plaquette-sagitta-and-wilson-action] is exactly quadratic in the chord at every aperture. In terms of the sagitta $v_\Pi = 1 - \cos\delta$, $\lvert\delta\rvert = \arccos(1 - v_\Pi) = \sqrt{2v_\Pi}\,\bigl(1 + \frac{v_\Pi}{12} + \cdots\bigr)$, which has a square-root behaviour at $v_\Pi = 0$.
3. On a polyhedral surface embedded in $\mathbb{R}^3$ the hinges are the vertices. Taking the three outward face normals $n_1, n_2, n_3$ of a trihedral corner as the $a, b, c$ of [@rem-appe-half-comparison-holonomy], the rotation angle $\Omega$ of the half-comparison holonomy $\cos\frac{\Omega}{2} + \sin\frac{\Omega}{2}\,\mathbf{I}a$ is the vertex deficit (item 4 of [@rem-ch39-regge-deficit-signed-angle]), and its bivector part $\mathbf{I}a$ is the plane with normal $n_1$, the plane of the first face: here the hinge plane is supplied by comparison data on the normals.
4. For $D = 3$ every rotation fixes a line pointwise. For $D \ge 4$ a general rotation is a double rotation: $\operatorname{rank}\Delta = 4$, its rotor bivector is not simple, and there is no hinge plane, although the sagitta reading $1 - \frac{1}{D}\operatorname{Tr}R$ is still defined. For $D \ge 4$ the reading "deficit on a hinge" therefore needs one extra condition: the bivector of the holonomy around a hinge is simple. It holds automatically in piecewise flat geometry; in a general comparison structure it is a reading adopted in this book (`MODEL`).
5. The sign cannot be dropped: a vertex surrounded by three squares (deficit $+90^\circ$) and a saddle surrounded by five squares (deficit $-90^\circ$) both have sagitta $1$ and chord length $\sqrt{2}$. On a closed polyhedral surface $\sum_v\delta_v = 2\pi\chi$; on a torus this is $0$, and unless all deficits vanish both signs occur. Sums of sagittas and of chord lengths are positive and do not see this identity.
:::

The following example puts numbers on these three remarks:

::: {.example #ex-ch39-sagitta-versus-deficit-audit title="Deficits against sagittas: the five regular polyhedra, the sample hinge and one commutator holonomy (manual derivation)"}
1. **Regular polyhedra.** The number of vertices times the deficit is always $720^\circ$; the sums of sagittas differ:

   | Polyhedron | Vertices | Deficit per vertex | Sum of deficits | $\sum_v(1 - \cos\delta_v)$ |
   | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
   | Tetrahedron | $4$ | $180^\circ$ | $720^\circ$ | $8.0000$ |
   | Cube | $8$ | $90^\circ$ | $720^\circ$ | $8.0000$ |
   | Octahedron | $6$ | $120^\circ$ | $720^\circ$ | $9.0000$ |
   | Dodecahedron | $20$ | $36^\circ$ | $720^\circ$ | $3.8197$ |
   | Icosahedron | $12$ | $60^\circ$ | $720^\circ$ | $6.0000$ |

2. **Normal formula.** At a vertex of the cube the three normals are mutually orthogonal, $[n_1, n_2, n_3] = 1$ and the denominator is $1$, so $\delta_v = 2\arctan 1 = 90^\circ$. At a vertex of the regular tetrahedron $n_i\cdot n_j = -\frac{1}{3}$, the denominator is $0$, and $\delta_v = 180^\circ$.
3. **Sample hinge.** For four regular tetrahedra around an edge, $\delta_h = 360^\circ - 4\arccos\frac{1}{3} = 77.885^\circ = 1.3593\;\mathrm{rad}$, $\sin\delta_h = 0.9777$, $1 - \cos\delta_h = 0.7901$, $\frac{1}{2}\delta_h^2 = 0.9239$. These numbers have closed forms: $\cos\frac{\delta_h}{2} = -\cos\bigl(2\arccos\frac{1}{3}\bigr) = \frac{7}{9}$, hence $1 - \cos\delta_h = \frac{64}{81}$, $\sin\delta_h = \frac{56\sqrt{2}}{81}$, the chord length is $2\sin\frac{\delta_h}{2} = \frac{8\sqrt{2}}{9}$, and the rotor sagitta is $1 - \cos\frac{\delta_h}{2} = \frac{2}{9}$.
4. **Commutator holonomy.** For rotors turning by $60^\circ$ about two orthogonal axes, item 3 of [@rem-ch39-plaquette-sagitta-and-wilson-action] gives $[u_1, u_2] = -2i\sin 30^\circ\sin 30^\circ\,(n_1\times n_2)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}$, hence $v(h) = 2\sin^2 30^\circ\sin^2 30^\circ = \frac{1}{8}$, and the residual rotation is $2\arccos\frac{7}{8} = 57.91^\circ$, in agreement with the reading on the experiment card of [§32.5](ch32-s05-physical-fields-and-six-domains.md#sec-ch32-physical-fields).
:::

The sagitta of the commutator holonomy in item 4 above can be bounded from above by the sagittas of the two links themselves. The matrix inequality this needs is frozen:

::: {.theorem #thm-ch39-weighted-bottcher-wenzel title="The weighted Böttcher–Wenzel commutator inequality" lean="D5/S3/Quantum/Matrix/WeightedBotcherWenzel.result,D5/S3/Quantum/Matrix/WeightedBotcherWenzel.weighted_one"}
For $A, W \in M_n(\mathbb{C})$ write $\operatorname{weightedSq}(A, W) = \operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}(A^\dagger A W)$, $\operatorname{frobSq}(A) = \operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}(A^\dagger A)$ and $[A, B] = AB - BA$.

1. Let $n > 0$, let $W$ be positive definite, let $\mathrm{lo} > 0$ be real, and let $W$ be Hermitian with all eigenvalues in $[\mathrm{lo}, \mathrm{hi}]$, both $\mathrm{lo}$ and $\mathrm{hi}$ being eigenvalues of $W$. Then for all $A, B \in M_n(\mathbb{C})$,

   $$
   \operatorname{weightedSq}([A, B], W) \le \left(1 + \frac{\mathrm{hi}}{\mathrm{lo}}\right)\operatorname{weightedSq}(A, W)\,\operatorname{frobSq}(B).
   $$

2. For every $A \in M_n(\mathbb{C})$, $\operatorname{weightedSq}(A, \mathbf{1}) = \operatorname{frobSq}(A)$.
:::

::: proof
A general weight is first written as $W = \mathrm{lo}\sum_iw_i\bigl(\mathbf{1} + \bigl(\frac{\mathrm{hi}}{\mathrm{lo}} - 1\bigr)P_i\bigr)$ with $w_i \ge 0$, $\sum_iw_i = 1$ and each $P_i$ an orthogonal projection; both sides are linear in the weight, so only two-level weights $\mathbf{1} + sP$ remain, and that case is settled by a column estimate after projection and a completion of the square. Taking $n > 0$, $W = \mathbf{1}$ and $\mathrm{lo} = \mathrm{hi} = 1$, items 1 and 2 together give the unweighted Böttcher–Wenzel inequality $\lVert[A, B]\rVert_F^2 \le 2\lVert A\rVert_F^2\lVert B\rVert_F^2$ (with $\lVert X\rVert_F^2 = \operatorname{Tr}(X^\dagger X)$); equivalently, the commutator map $Y \mapsto [A, Y]$ has squared operator norm at most $2\lVert A\rVert_F^2$ with respect to the Frobenius norm. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

Applied to chords, it gives a product bound for the commutator sagitta:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-commutator-sagitta-product title="The sagitta of a commutator holonomy is at most four times the product of the link sagittas (manual derivation and literature result)"}
**Manual derivation; the commutator estimate behind the Zassenhaus neighbourhood is a literature result, not formalized in this book.** Item 1 uses only the case $W = \mathbf{1}$ of [@thm-ch39-weighted-bottcher-wenzel] (the unweighted inequality was first proved by A. Böttcher and D. Wenzel). For $U \in \mathrm{U}(N)$ let $\bar{v}(U) = N - \operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}U = \frac{1}{2}\lVert U - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F^2$ be the unnormalized sagitta, $N$ times the $\varphi(v)$ of [@rem-ch39-plaquette-sagitta-and-wilson-action]. Let $u_1, u_2 \in \mathrm{U}(N)$, $\Delta_k = u_k - \mathbf{1}$ and $h = u_1u_2u_1^{-1}u_2^{-1}$.

1. $\bar{v}(h) = \frac{1}{2}\lVert[\Delta_1, \Delta_2]\rVert_F^2 \le 4\,\bar{v}(u_1)\,\bar{v}(u_2)$; normalized, $\varphi(v(h)) \le 4N\,\varphi(v(u_1))\,\varphi(v(u_2))$. Proof: by item 3 of [@rem-ch39-plaquette-sagitta-and-wilson-action], $h - \mathbf{1} = [u_1, u_2]\,(u_2u_1)^\dagger$, and right multiplication by a unitary leaves $\lVert\cdot\rVert_F$ unchanged; moreover $[u_1, u_2] = [\Delta_1, \Delta_2]$. Hence by [@thm-ch39-weighted-bottcher-wenzel] with $W = \mathbf{1}$, $2\bar{v}(h) \le 2\lVert\Delta_1\rVert_F^2\lVert\Delta_2\rVert_F^2 = 8\,\bar{v}(u_1)\bar{v}(u_2)$. Only the normality of $\Delta_1$ is used, so there is also an elementary proof: in an eigenbasis of $u_1$, $\Delta_1 = \operatorname{diag}(x_i)$ with $x_i = e^{i\theta_i} - 1$, $[\Delta_1, Y]_{ij} = (x_i - x_j)Y_{ij}$, and for $i \ne j$, $\lvert x_i - x_j\rvert^2 \le 2(\lvert x_i\rvert^2 + \lvert x_j\rvert^2) \le 2\lVert\Delta_1\rVert_F^2$, so $\lVert[\Delta_1, Y]\rVert_F^2 \le 2\lVert\Delta_1\rVert_F^2\lVert Y\rVert_F^2$.
2. The constant $4$ cannot be lowered. For $u_k = \cos\frac{\theta_k}{2} - i\sin\frac{\theta_k}{2}\,n_k\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ in $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ one has exactly

   $$
   \bar{v}(h) = 4\,\bar{v}(u_1)\,\bar{v}(u_2)\cos^2\frac{\theta_1}{4}\cos^2\frac{\theta_2}{4}\,\lvert n_1\times n_2\rvert^2,
   $$

   since $[u_1, u_2] = -2i\sin\frac{\theta_1}{2}\sin\frac{\theta_2}{2}\,(n_1\times n_2)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}$, $\bar{v}(u_k) = 4\sin^2\frac{\theta_k}{4}$ and $\sin\frac{\theta}{2} = 2\sin\frac{\theta}{4}\cos\frac{\theta}{4}$. With orthogonal axes and angles tending to $0$ the ratio $\bar{v}(h)/(4\bar{v}(u_1)\bar{v}(u_2))$ tends to $1$; in $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ it is strictly below $1$ for nonzero angles, so $4$ is a supremum approached in the small-angle limit. Embedding this pair in a $2 \times 2$ block of $\mathrm{U}(N)$ makes the ratio in the normalized form tend to $4N$, so the factor $N$ cannot be dropped either.
3. For comparison: $\lVert u_1u_2 - u_2u_1\rVert_F^2 = \lVert h - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F^2 = 2\bar{v}(h)$, so by item 1 the two orderings of the product differ by at most $8\,\bar{v}(u_1)\bar{v}(u_2)$ in squared chord, which is fourth order in the angles; the sagitta of the product itself only obeys chord addition, $\sqrt{\bar{v}(u_1u_2)} \le \sqrt{\bar{v}(u_1)} + \sqrt{\bar{v}(u_2)}$, which is first order in the angles.
4. Iterated commutators contract: for any $h_0$ and $h_k = u_1h_{k-1}u_1^{-1}h_{k-1}^{-1}$, $\bar{v}(h_k) \le (4\bar{v}(u_1))^k\,\bar{v}(h_0)$, which tends to zero geometrically when $\bar{v}(u_1) < \frac{1}{4}$. The analogous operator-norm inequality $\lVert aba^{-1}b^{-1} - \mathbf{1}\rVert \le 2\lVert a - \mathbf{1}\rVert\,\lVert b - \mathbf{1}\rVert$ for unitary $a, b$ is the starting point of the Zassenhaus-neighbourhood argument (literature result).
5. A finite-aperture “$F = dA + [A, A]$”. The plaquette holonomy $P = h\,w\,h'^{-1}w'^{-1}$ ($h, h'$ one pair of opposite links, $w, w'$ the other) satisfies

   $$
   \lVert P - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F \le \sqrt{2}\,\lVert h - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F\,\lVert w - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F + \lVert h' - h\rVert_F + \lVert w' - w\rVert_F .
   $$

   Proof: let $C = hwh^{-1}w^{-1}$. Left multiplication by $(hw)^{-1}$ preserves distances, so $\lVert P - C\rVert_F = \lVert h'^{-1}w'^{-1} - h^{-1}w^{-1}\rVert_F \le \lVert h'^{-1} - h^{-1}\rVert_F + \lVert w'^{-1} - w^{-1}\rVert_F$, and $\lVert a^{-1} - b^{-1}\rVert_F = \lVert a^{-1}(b - a)b^{-1}\rVert_F = \lVert b - a\rVert_F$; by item 1, $\lVert C - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F = \sqrt{2\bar{v}(C)} \le \sqrt{2}\,\lVert h - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F\lVert w - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F$. When opposite links agree only the first term remains, and by item 2 its constant $\sqrt{2}$ cannot be lowered; in $\mathrm{U}(1)$, $C = \mathbf{1}$ and the first term can be dropped altogether. Reading the first term as $[A, A]$ and the differences of opposite links as $dA$ is an analogy with the continuum notation, not a derivation.

For the unweighted Böttcher–Wenzel inequality see A. Böttcher and D. Wenzel (*Linear Algebra Appl.* **429**, 1864–1885, 2008, DOI `10.1016/j.laa.2008.05.020`); for the Zassenhaus neighbourhood see H. Zassenhaus (*Abh. Math. Sem. Univ. Hamburg* **12**, 289–312, 1938).
:::

The Bianchi identity also has an exact finite form on the lattice. The smallest closed surface is the six faces of a cube:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-cube-bianchi-sagitta title="The Bianchi identity on a cube and the redistribution of sagitta (manual derivation and literature result)"}
**Manual derivation; the lattice Bianchi identity, the cube monopole number (DeGrand and Toussaint 1980) and the admissibility condition (Lüscher 1999) are literature results, not formalized in this book.** The unit cube has vertices $(x, y, z) \in \{0, 1\}^3$. Link variables take values in an arbitrary group: $X_{yz}$ runs from $(0, y, z)$ to $(1, y, z)$, $Y_{xz}$ from $(x, 0, z)$ to $(x, 1, z)$, $Z_{xy}$ from $(x, y, 0)$ to $(x, y, 1)$. Each face is traversed from its vertex nearest the origin, in the right-handed sense about the outward normal; the three faces that miss the origin are joined to it by one edge:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_1 &= Z_{00}Y_{01}Z_{01}^{-1}Y_{00}^{-1} && (x = 0),\\
\ell_2 &= Y_{00}\,(Z_{01}X_{11}Z_{11}^{-1}X_{10}^{-1})\,Y_{00}^{-1} && (y = 1),\\
\ell_3 &= Y_{00}X_{10}Y_{10}^{-1}X_{00}^{-1} && (z = 0),\\
\ell_4 &= X_{00}\,(Y_{10}Z_{11}Y_{11}^{-1}Z_{10}^{-1})\,X_{00}^{-1} && (x = 1),\\
\ell_5 &= X_{00}Z_{10}X_{01}^{-1}Z_{00}^{-1} && (y = 0),\\
\ell_6 &= Z_{00}\,(X_{01}Y_{11}X_{11}^{-1}Y_{01}^{-1})\,Z_{00}^{-1} && (z = 1).
\end{aligned}
$$

1. $\ell_1\ell_2\ell_3\ell_4\ell_5\ell_6 = \mathbf{1}$. Multiplying on the right one lasso at a time, each step cancels only one block of adjacent mutually inverse letters at the seam:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \ell_1\ell_2 &= Z_{00}Y_{01}X_{11}Z_{11}^{-1}X_{10}^{-1}Y_{00}^{-1},\\
   \ell_1\ell_2\ell_3 &= Z_{00}Y_{01}X_{11}Z_{11}^{-1}Y_{10}^{-1}X_{00}^{-1},\\
   \ell_1\cdots\ell_4 &= Z_{00}Y_{01}X_{11}Y_{11}^{-1}Z_{10}^{-1}X_{00}^{-1},\\
   \ell_1\cdots\ell_5 &= Z_{00}Y_{01}X_{11}Y_{11}^{-1}X_{01}^{-1}Z_{00}^{-1} = \ell_6^{-1}.
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Each partial product runs once, from the origin, around the boundary of the disc formed by the faces attached so far; the disc made of five faces has as its boundary the sixth face traversed backwards. This is an identity in the free group, so it holds for link variables in any group; a cyclic rotation of the product is a conjugate of it and also equals $\mathbf{1}$. The order is part of the statement.
2. Let $P_f$ be the holonomy of face $f$ from its starting vertex ($\ell_f$ is a conjugate of it). In $\mathrm{U}(N)$, item 1 and the two-sided unitary invariance of $\lVert\cdot\rVert_F$ give, for every face $f$,

   $$
   \lVert P_f - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F \le \sum_{g \ne f}\lVert P_g - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F, \qquad \bar{v}(P_f) \le \left(\sum_{g \ne f}\sqrt{\bar{v}(P_g)}\right)^2 \le 5\sum_{g \ne f}\bar{v}(P_g),
   $$

   with $\bar{v}$ the unnormalized sagitta of [@rem-ch39-commutator-sagitta-product]. Neither constant can be lowered: in $\mathrm{U}(1)$, give one face the outward flux $\vartheta$ and each of the other five $-\frac{\vartheta}{5}$; as $\vartheta \to 0$ both ratios tend to $1$. Sagitta on one face cannot appear alone; the other five faces must pay for it.
3. In $\mathrm{U}(1)$ conjugation does nothing and item 1 becomes $\sum_f\vartheta_f \equiv 0 \pmod{2\pi}$ for the outward fluxes $\vartheta_f$. With principal values $\vartheta_f \in (-\pi, \pi]$ the integer $n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_f\vartheta_f$ is the magnetic monopole number of the cube (it agrees with the DeGrand–Toussaint definition up to a sign convention). If $n \ne 0$ then $\sum_f\lvert\vartheta_f\rvert \ge 2\pi$, hence $\max_f\lvert\vartheta_f\rvert \ge \frac{\pi}{3}$, that is $\max_f(1 - \cos\vartheta_f) \ge \frac{1}{2}$: if all six face sagittas are below $\frac{1}{2}$ (all chords below $1$) there is no monopole. The threshold is attained: six fluxes equal to $\frac{\pi}{3}$ give $n = 1$ with every face sagitta exactly $\frac{1}{2}$. Using also $1 - \cos x \ge \frac{2x^2}{\pi^2}$ for $\lvert x\rvert \le \pi$ and $\sum_f\vartheta_f^2 \ge \frac{1}{6}\bigl(\sum_f\lvert\vartheta_f\rvert\bigr)^2$, one gets $\sum_f(1 - \cos\vartheta_f) \ge \frac{4}{3}$ whenever $n \ne 0$; this bound is not sharp.
4. In the non-abelian case the face holonomies do not commute, the principal-value sum of item 3 has no direct counterpart, and the inequalities of item 2 still hold. In the continuum limit the leading order of item 1 gives $D_{[\lambda}F_{\mu\nu]} = 0$ (literature result).

For the cube monopole number see T. A. DeGrand and D. Toussaint (*Phys. Rev. D* **22**, 2478–2489, 1980). M. Lüscher (*Nucl. Phys. B* **549**, 295–334, 1999, arXiv:`hep-lat/9811032`) restricts abelian chiral lattice gauge theory to admissible fields: the principal value of the plaquette field strength satisfies $\lvert F_{\mu\nu}\rvert < \epsilon$ everywhere, with a constant $\epsilon$ chosen in $(0, \frac{\pi}{3})$. By the arithmetic of item 3, such fields have $n = 0$ in every unit cube.
:::

On the gravitational side, in three dimensions the deficit is also the derivative of the Regge action with respect to edge length. The proof uses only pairwise comparisons of face normals:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-schlafli-regge-deficit-gradient title="Schläfli's formula: in three dimensions the gradient of the Regge action in the edge lengths is the deficit (manual derivation and literature result)"}
**Manual derivation; Schläfli's formula (Schläfli 1858) and its general-dimensional form, and the Regge equations (Regge 1961), are literature results, not formalized in this book.**

1. Let a convex polyhedron $P \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ deform smoothly without changing its combinatorial type, with edge lengths $\ell_e$ and interior dihedral angles $\theta_e \in (0, \pi)$. Then $\sum_e\ell_e\,d\theta_e = 0$. Proof: let $n_F$ be the outward unit normal of face $F$, and for an edge $e$ of $F$ let $m_{F,e}$ be the unit vector in the plane of $F$, perpendicular to $e$ and pointing out of $F$. The exterior angle $\alpha_e = \pi - \theta_e$ between the faces $F, G$ on either side of $e$ is given by comparing their normals: $\cos\alpha_e = n_F\cdot n_G$, and $n_G = \cos\alpha_e\,n_F + \sin\alpha_e\,m_{F,e}$ (likewise for $G$). Since $n_F\cdot dn_F = 0$,

   $$
   -\sin\alpha_e\,d\alpha_e = dn_F\cdot n_G + n_F\cdot dn_G = \sin\alpha_e\,\bigl(m_{F,e}\cdot dn_F + m_{G,e}\cdot dn_G\bigr).
   $$

   Cancelling $\sin\alpha_e \ne 0$, weighting by $\ell_e$, summing over edges and regrouping by faces gives $\sum_e\ell_e\,d\alpha_e = -\sum_F dn_F\cdot\left(\sum_{e \subset F}\ell_e\,m_{F,e}\right)$. Each $\ell_e\,m_{F,e}$ is an oriented edge vector of the boundary polygon of $F$ turned through $90^\circ$ within the face, and the edge vectors of a closed polygon sum to zero, so the right-hand side vanishes; and $d\theta_e = -d\alpha_e$.
2. Let every tetrahedron of a closed three-dimensional simplicial complex be a nondegenerate Euclidean tetrahedron, let $\delta_e = 2\pi - \sum_{t \ni e}\theta_e^{(t)}$ be the deficit at edge $e$, and let $S = \sum_e\ell_e\delta_e$ be the Regge action. Then $\frac{\partial S}{\partial\ell_k} = \delta_k$. Proof: $\frac{\partial S}{\partial\ell_k} = \delta_k - \sum_t\sum_{e \subset t}\ell_e\,\frac{\partial\theta_e^{(t)}}{\partial\ell_k}$, and the second term vanishes by item 1 applied to each tetrahedron. So the vacuum Regge equations in three dimensions say exactly that every edge has zero deficit, that is, flatness edge by edge.
3. Numerical check: on the boundary of a 4-simplex (five tetrahedra, ten edges, each edge in three tetrahedra), with edge lengths drawn at random in $1 \pm 0.15$, the central-difference $\frac{\partial S}{\partial\ell_k}$ differs from $\delta_k$ by at most $10^{-8}$; with all lengths $1$, $\delta_k = 2\pi - 3\arccos\frac{1}{3} \approx 2.5903$.

The proof of item 1 uses only two things: the comparison $n_F\cdot n_G$ of two face normals, and the closure of each face boundary; reading the former as the similarity channel of Clause II of [@def-ch32-axiom-of-comparison] is a reading of this book (`MODEL`). Item 2 says that in three dimensions the quantity conjugate to a hinge length is precisely the arc of its deficit. In four dimensions, $\frac{\partial S}{\partial\ell_k} = \sum_h\delta_h\,\frac{\partial A_h}{\partial\ell_k}$ rests on the general-dimensional Schläfli formula (literature result; see J. Milnor, “The Schläfli differential equality”, in *Collected Papers of John Milnor*, vol. 1, 1994). Lorentzian signature, the coupling constant and quantization do not follow from this ([@prob-ch34-gravity-dynamics-and-coupling]).
:::

On the gauge side the action can also be varied to first order. Rotating one link changes the Wilson action only through the plaquettes that contain that link:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-wilson-staple-equation-of-motion title="The lattice Yang–Mills equation: the odd channel of a link times its staple sum (manual derivation and literature result)"}
**Manual derivation; the Wilson action (Wilson 1974), the staple form and the usual form of the lattice equation of motion, and their continuum counterparts, are literature results, not formalized in this book.** Let a $d$-dimensional periodic hypercubic lattice have at least two sites in each direction, and let the link variable $U_\mu(x) \in G$ point from $x$ to $x + \hat{\mu}$, with $G = \mathrm{U}(N)$ or $\mathrm{SU}(N)$. Plaquette holonomies follow the order of item 4 of [@rem-ch39-plaquette-sagitta-and-wilson-action], $U_{\mu\nu}(x) = U_\mu(x)U_\nu(x + \hat{\mu})U_\mu(x + \hat{\nu})^{-1}U_\nu(x)^{-1}$, and the action is $S_W = \beta\sum_{x}\sum_{\mu < \nu}\left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}U_{\mu\nu}(x)\right)$. For a link $\ell = (x, \mu)$ define the staple sum

$$
\Sigma_\mu(x) = \sum_{\nu \ne \mu}\left(U_\nu(x + \hat{\mu})\,U_\mu(x + \hat{\nu})^{-1}\,U_\nu(x)^{-1} + U_\nu(x + \hat{\mu} - \hat{\nu})^{-1}\,U_\mu(x - \hat{\nu})^{-1}\,U_\nu(x - \hat{\nu})\right),
$$

and write $M_\ell = U_\mu(x)\Sigma_\mu(x)$ (the sum of the $2(d - 1)$ plaquette loops starting and ending at $x$), its odd channel $A(M) = \frac{1}{2}(M - M^\dagger)$, and the traceless odd channel $A_0(M) = A(M) - \frac{1}{N}\operatorname{Tr}A(M)\,\mathbf{1}$.

1. **Linear in a single link.** $S_W = C_\ell - \frac{\beta}{N}\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(U_\mu(x)\Sigma_\mu(x)\bigr)$, where $C_\ell$ does not contain $U_\mu(x)$. Proof: exactly $2(d - 1)$ plaquettes contain the link $\ell$, two for each $\nu \ne \mu$, based at $x$ and at $x - \hat{\nu}$; by $\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}U^{-1} = \operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}U$ and cyclicity of the trace, the $\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}$ of each equals $\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}(U_\mu(x)\,\cdot\,\text{its staple})$.
2. **Exact change under a finite rotation.** Replacing $U_\mu(x)$ by $VU_\mu(x)$ ($V \in G$) gives $\Delta S_W = -\frac{\beta}{N}\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((V - \mathbf{1})M_\ell\bigr)$: the change in the action is the pairing of the chord $V - \mathbf{1}$ of the rotation with $M_\ell$, exactly, for rotations of any size.
3. **First variation.** For anti-Hermitian $X$ (with $\operatorname{Tr}X = 0$ as well when $G = \mathrm{SU}(N)$),

   $$
   \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\,S_W\bigl(e^{\varepsilon X}U_\mu(x)\bigr)\bigr\rvert_{\varepsilon = 0} = -\frac{\beta}{N}\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}(XM_\ell) = -\frac{\beta}{N}\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(X\,A(M_\ell)\bigr),
   $$

   because $\overline{\operatorname{Tr}(XM)} = \operatorname{Tr}(M^\dagger X^\dagger) = -\operatorname{Tr}(XM^\dagger)$ for anti-Hermitian $X$. When $\operatorname{Tr}X = 0$, $A$ may be replaced by $A_0$.
4. **Equation of motion.** On $\mathfrak{su}(N)$, $\langle X, Y\rangle = -\operatorname{Tr}(XY)$ is a positive definite inner product ($-\operatorname{Tr}(XX) = \operatorname{Tr}(X^\dagger X)$), and item 3 says that the gradient of $S_W$ in this link is $\frac{\beta}{N}A_0(M_\ell)$. Hence $S_W$ is stationary under all variations of the link $\ell$ within $\mathrm{SU}(N)$ if and only if $A_0\bigl(U_\mu(x)\Sigma_\mu(x)\bigr) = 0$; for $\mathrm{U}(N)$, if and only if $A\bigl(U_\mu(x)\Sigma_\mu(x)\bigr) = 0$, that is, $U_\mu(x)\Sigma_\mu(x)$ is Hermitian. The action reads the even channel (the sagitta) of each plaquette; its derivative in a link reads the odd channel of the link times its staple sum.
5. **An identity at each vertex.** For every vertex $x$,

   $$
   \sum_{\mu}\left(A_0\bigl(U_\mu(x)\Sigma_\mu(x)\bigr) - A_0\bigl(\Sigma_\mu(x - \hat{\mu})\,U_\mu(x - \hat{\mu})\bigr)\right) = 0 .
   $$

   Both terms are loops based at $x$. Proof: make a gauge transformation $g = e^{\varepsilon X}$ at $x$ only; links leaving $x$ become $gU$, links entering $x$ become $Ug^{-1}$, and $S_W$ is unchanged. Differentiating in $\varepsilon$ with item 3, a leaving link contributes $-\frac{\beta}{N}\operatorname{Tr}(XA_0(U\Sigma))$ and an entering link $+\frac{\beta}{N}\operatorname{Tr}(XA_0(\Sigma U))$; the total vanishes for every $X \in \mathfrak{su}(N)$, and choosing $X$ equal to the left-hand side above gives the claim. It can also be checked pair by pair: each plaquette with a corner at $x$ contributes one loop based at $x$ on each of its two links at $x$; when both links leave $x$ or both enter it, the two loops are mutually inverse and carry the same sign, and when one leaves and one enters, the two loops are equal and carry opposite signs; since $A(L^{-1}) = -A(L)$, the contributions cancel in pairs. Because $\operatorname{Tr}(U\Sigma) = \operatorname{Tr}(\Sigma U)$ term by term, the identity also holds with $A$ in place of $A_0$. This identity holds for every configuration, whether or not it satisfies the equation of motion.
6. **The $\mathrm{U}(1)$ form.** Let $\theta_p$ be the flux of plaquette $p$ and $\epsilon(\ell, p) = \pm 1$ according to whether the boundary of $p$ runs along the link $\ell$ in its direction or against it. Then $A\bigl(U_\ell\Sigma_\ell\bigr) = i\sum_{p \ni \ell}\epsilon(\ell, p)\sin\theta_p$, and the equation of motion is $\sum_{p \ni \ell}\epsilon(\ell, p)\sin\theta_p = 0$. Item 5 becomes: at every vertex, summing this expression with sign $+$ for leaving and $-$ for entering links gives zero identically, because each plaquette with a corner at $x$ appears once on each of its two links at $x$, with opposite signs. For a single plaquette, $S = \beta(1 - \cos\theta)$ has $\theta$-derivative $\beta\sin\theta$: the derivative of the even channel is the odd channel.
7. **Numerical check.** On a four-dimensional periodic lattice of side $3$ with random $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ and $\mathrm{U}(3)$ configurations ($\beta = 2.3$), the finite-rotation formula of item 2 agrees with a direct recomputation of the action to within $10^{-12}$, item 3 agrees with central differences of step $10^{-5}$ to a relative deviation below $10^{-7}$, and item 5 holds at the vertices to within $2 \times 10^{-15}$; items 1 and 5 also hold on a lattice of side $2$. In $\mathrm{SU}(3)$, gradient flow of a single link along $-A_0$ lowers the action monotonically and stops at $A_0 = 0$.

Set next to the Bianchi identity on a cube ([@rem-ch39-cube-bianchi-sagitta]): the Bianchi identity and item 5 hold for every configuration, whereas the equation of motion holds only at stationary points of the action. The former comes from each edge appearing exactly twice on the boundary of the cube, with opposite orientations; the latter comes from gauge invariance at the vertex, that is, from the pairwise cancellation in item 5. No claim is made here that either one does or does not follow from the other. In $\mathrm{U}(1)$ the three are $\sum_{f \subset \partial c}\theta_f \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, $\sum_{p \ni \ell}\epsilon(\ell, p)\sin\theta_p = 0$, and the identical vanishing of the divergence of the latter at each vertex. In the continuum limit of smooth fields they correspond to $D_{[\lambda}F_{\mu\nu]} = 0$, $D_\mu F_{\mu\nu} = 0$ and $D_\nu D_\mu F_{\mu\nu} = 0$ (literature result). Read together with [@rem-ch39-schlafli-regge-deficit-gradient]: the derivative of the three-dimensional Regge action in an edge length is the deficit itself, whereas the derivative of the Wilson action in a link is an odd channel, which in $\mathrm{U}(1)$ is $\sin\theta_p$ rather than $\theta_p$. Treating this difference as a structural difference between the two kinds of force is a reading of this book (`MODEL`); the gauge group, the coupling constants and the quantum dynamics do not follow from it.

For the Wilson action see K. G. Wilson (*Phys. Rev. D* **10**, 2445–2459, 1974); for the linear dependence of the action on a single link and the staple form see the textbook by C. Gattringer and C. B. Lang, *Quantum Chromodynamics on the Lattice* (Lecture Notes in Physics **788**, Springer, 2010, DOI `10.1007/978-3-642-01850-3`).
:::

The plaquette sagitta also splits exactly into an even and an odd channel, and admits a second-order expansion in link chords:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-plaquette-even-odd-expansion title="The even–odd Pythagorean split of the plaquette sagitta and the remainder of the second-order expansion (manual derivation)"}
**Manual derivation.** Let $P \in \mathrm{U}(N)$ be a plaquette holonomy with eigenangles $\theta_j \in (-\pi, \pi]$, $\bar{v}(P) = N - \operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}P$, even-channel sagitta matrix $v_P = \mathbf{1} - \frac{1}{2}(P + P^\dagger)$ and odd channel $A_P = \frac{1}{2}(P - P^\dagger)$.

1. **Pythagorean split.** $\bar{v}(P) = \frac{1}{2}\lVert A_P\rVert_F^2 + \frac{1}{2}\lVert v_P\rVert_F^2$; in eigenangles, $\sum_j(1 - \cos\theta_j) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_j\sin^2\theta_j + \frac{1}{2}\sum_j(1 - \cos\theta_j)^2$. Proof: $P - \mathbf{1} = -v_P + A_P$ with $v_P$ Hermitian and $A_P$ anti-Hermitian, so $\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((P - \mathbf{1})^\dagger(P - \mathbf{1})\bigr) = \operatorname{Tr}(v_P^2) - \operatorname{Tr}(A_P^2)$, the cross terms $\operatorname{Tr}(v_PA_P)$ and $\operatorname{Tr}(A_Pv_P)$ cancelling; then use $\bar{v}(P) = \frac{1}{2}\lVert P - \mathbf{1}\rVert_F^2$.
2. **Sagitta against the squared odd channel.** Since $v_P \ge 0$, $\lVert v_P\rVert_F^2 \le \lVert v_P\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\operatorname{Tr}v_P = \lVert v_P\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\,\bar{v}(P)$, so

   $$
   \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\lVert v_P\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\right)\bar{v}(P) \le \frac{1}{2}\lVert A_P\rVert_F^2 \le \bar{v}(P),
   $$

   with equality on the left if and only if the nonzero eigenvalues of $v_P$ are all equal (for instance in $\mathrm{U}(1)$ and $\mathrm{SU}(2)$). $\frac{1}{2}\lVert A_P\rVert_F^2 = \frac{1}{2}\sum_j\sin^2\theta_j$ is another plaquette term, written with the odd channel; the relative difference between the two is at most $\frac{1}{2}\lVert v_P\rVert_{\mathrm{op}} = \frac{1}{4}\lVert P - \mathbf{1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}^2$, which is gauge invariant and tends to zero like $a^4$ for smooth fields. By two-sided unitary invariance and the triangle inequality, $\lVert P - \mathbf{1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}} \le \sum_{\ell \in \partial p}\lVert U_\ell - \mathbf{1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}$, so the relative difference is also at most $\frac{1}{4}$ of the square of the sum of the four link chords. In $\mathrm{U}(1)$ with $h = w = e^{i\phi}$ and $h' = w' = e^{-i\phi}$, the ratio of the two sides of this last step tends to $1$ as $\phi \to 0$.
3. **Second-order expansion and remainder.** Write $x_1 = h - \mathbf{1}$, $x_2 = w - \mathbf{1}$, $x_3 = h'^{-1} - \mathbf{1}$, $x_4 = w'^{-1} - \mathbf{1}$ and $c_i = \lVert x_i\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}$ (note $\lVert h'^{-1} - \mathbf{1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}} = \lVert h' - \mathbf{1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}$). Expanding $P = (\mathbf{1} + x_1)(\mathbf{1} + x_2)(\mathbf{1} + x_3)(\mathbf{1} + x_4)$ gives

   $$
   P - \mathbf{1} = \sum_i x_i + \sum_{i < j}x_ix_j + R, \qquad \lVert R\rVert_{\mathrm{op}} \le e_3(c) + e_4(c),
   $$

   where $e_k$ is the $k$-th elementary symmetric polynomial in $c_1, \dots, c_4$: $R$ is the sum of all ordered triple and quadruple products, and the operator norm is submultiplicative. The constant cannot be lowered: for four equal, commuting chords $R = 4x^3 + x^4$, and the ratio tends to $1$ as $x \to 0$.
4. **How far this goes.** The remainder in item 3 controls $\lVert P - \mathbf{1}\rVert$ to third order in the link chords. Link chords are gauge dependent; for smooth fields, in a gauge in which they are $O(a)$, combining item 3 with the Taylor expansion of the link variables gives the leading term of $\sqrt{\bar{v}(P)}$ with an error $O(a^3)$, that is, the leading term $\frac{a^4}{2}\operatorname{Tr}F_{\mu\nu}^2$ of $\bar{v}(P)$ with an error $O(a^5)$. The $O(a^6)$ of item 5 of [@rem-ch39-plaquette-sagitta-and-wilson-action] (labelled there as a literature result) comes from the cancellation of odd orders when expanding about the centre of the plaquette; the bound of item 3 does not produce it by itself.
5. **Numerical check.** For random elements of $\mathrm{U}(1)$ to $\mathrm{U}(5)$, item 1 holds to within $2 \times 10^{-15}$; the inequalities of items 2 and 3 hold on 3000 random four-link plaquettes, with largest ratios $1.0000$ and $0.9999$, in line with the limiting cases above. For a smooth $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ potential with path-ordered exponentials as link variables, the difference between $\bar{v}(P)$ and $\frac{a^4}{2}\operatorname{Tr}F_{\mu\nu}^2$ evaluated at the centre of the plaquette shrinks by a factor of about $2^{-6}$ when $a$ is halved, and by about $2^{-5}$ when $F$ is evaluated at a corner instead.
:::

Zero action does not always mean pure gauge. On a lattice with non-contractible loops, such as a torus, the following frozen theorem gives an instance with $\mathbb{Z}/2$ coefficients:

::: {.theorem #thm-ch39-periodic-grid-flat-not-exact title="Flat Z/2 edge labels on a periodic grid: the gradient criterion and a one-seam counterexample" lean="D5/S3/Fourier/CharacterSelection/PeriodicGridHolonomy.flat_exact_iff,D5/S3/Fourier/CharacterSelection/PeriodicGridHolonomy.horizontal_seam_counterexample"}
Let $M, N$ be nonzero natural numbers, and let an edge label $y$ consist of two families $y.\mathrm{horizontal},\ y.\mathrm{vertical} : \operatorname{Fin} M \to \operatorname{Fin} N \to \mathbb{Z}/2$, with indices added modulo $M$ and $N$. Put $\operatorname{plaquette}(y, i, j) = y.\mathrm{horizontal}(i, j) + y.\mathrm{vertical}(i, j + 1) + y.\mathrm{horizontal}(i + 1, j) + y.\mathrm{vertical}(i, j)$; let $\operatorname{Flat}(y)$ mean that every $\operatorname{plaquette}(y, i, j)$ is $0$; let $\operatorname{rowHolonomy}(y, i) = \sum_j y.\mathrm{horizontal}(i, j)$ and $\operatorname{columnHolonomy}(y, j) = \sum_i y.\mathrm{vertical}(i, j)$. For a vertex label $x : \operatorname{Fin} M \to \operatorname{Fin} N \to \mathbb{Z}/2$ let $\operatorname{gradient}(x).\mathrm{horizontal}(i, j) = x(i, j + 1) + x(i, j)$ and $\operatorname{gradient}(x).\mathrm{vertical}(i, j) = x(i + 1, j) + x(i, j)$; and let $\operatorname{seam}(h, v)$ take the value $h$ on the horizontal edges with $j = N - 1$, the value $v$ on the vertical edges with $i = M - 1$, and $0$ elsewhere. Then:

1. if $\operatorname{Flat}(y)$, then $\bigl(\exists x,\ \operatorname{gradient}(x) = y\bigr) \iff \operatorname{rowHolonomy}(y, 0) = 0 \land \operatorname{columnHolonomy}(y, 0) = 0$;
2. if $3 \le M$ and $3 \le N$, then $\operatorname{Flat}(\operatorname{seam}(1, 0))$, $\operatorname{rowHolonomy}(\operatorname{seam}(1, 0), 0) = 1$, $\operatorname{columnHolonomy}(\operatorname{seam}(1, 0), 0) = 0$, and there is no $x$ with $\operatorname{gradient}(x) = \operatorname{seam}(1, 0)$.
:::

::: proof
Necessity in item 1 holds because the terms of a gradient cancel in pairs when summed along any row or column. Sufficiency uses the decomposition of flat labels: a flat $y$ is the gradient of some vertex label plus $\operatorname{seam}(\operatorname{rowHolonomy}(y, 0), \operatorname{columnHolonomy}(y, 0))$, and the seam term vanishes when both holonomies are zero. Item 2 first checks that the seam label is flat and computes its two holonomies, then applies item 1: the row holonomy is $1 \ne 0$, so the label is not a gradient. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

The zero-action counterexample above shows that on a torus a flat field need not be a pure gauge. On a planar region the situation is the opposite: the gauge freedom can be absorbed along a spanning tree, the remaining plaquette holonomies are independent, and the two-dimensional Wilson loop obeys an exact area law:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-tree-gauge-plaquette-independence title="Spanning-tree gauge: plaquette holonomies on a planar region are independent, and the two-dimensional area law is exact (manual derivation and literature result)"}
**Manual derivation; the exact solvability of two-dimensional lattice gauge theory, its area law and the continuum correspondence are literature results, not formalized in this book.** Let $G$ be a compact group with normalized Haar measure $\mathrm{d}U$, and $F$ an irreducible unitary representation of $G$ (the defining one for $\mathrm{U}(N)$ and $\mathrm{SU}(N)$) with character $\chi_F$ and dimension $d_F$. The plaquette weight $\phi : G \to (0, \infty)$ is a continuous class function with $\phi(U^{-1}) = \phi(U)$; the Wilson action corresponds to $\phi(U) = \exp\left(\frac{\beta}{d_F}\operatorname{Re}\chi_F(U)\right)$, $\beta \ge 0$, which differs from $e^{-S_W}$ of item 1 of [@rem-ch39-plaquette-sagitta-and-wilson-action] only by a constant factor $e^{-\beta}$ per plaquette. Write $\nu(\mathrm{d}U) = \phi(U)\,\mathrm{d}U / \int_G\phi\,\mathrm{d}U$ for the law of a single plaquette and

$$
w = \frac{\int_G \phi(U)\,\chi_F(U)\,\mathrm{d}U}{d_F\int_G \phi(U)\,\mathrm{d}U}.
$$

Since $\phi(U^{-1}) = \phi(U)$ and $\chi_F(U^{-1}) = \overline{\chi_F(U)}$, $w$ is real. For $\mathrm{U}(1)$, $w = I_1(\beta)/I_0(\beta)$; for $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ (with $\phi = e^{\frac{\beta}{2}\operatorname{Tr}U}$), $w = I_2(\beta)/I_1(\beta)$; for $\mathbb{Z}_2$, $w = \tanh\beta$. Here $I_k$ is the modified Bessel function of the first kind.

1. **The existence of the tree gauge is the frozen gain-potential theorem applied to a tree.** Let $T$ be a spanning tree of a finite lattice graph $\Gamma$ (a spanning forest if $\Gamma$ is disconnected), and let the link variables satisfy $U_{yx} = U_{xy}^{-1}$. Having no cycles, $T$ is chordal, and it has no triangles; applying [`exists_gain_potential`](lean:D5/S3/Combinatorics/Graph/Chordal/GainPotential.SimpleGraph.IsChordal.exists_gain_potential) ([@thm-appe-chordal-gain-potential]) to $T$ with $H = G$ and $g(i, j) = U_{ij}$, condition 1 is $U_{ji} = U_{ij}^{-1}$ and condition 2 holds vacuously, which gives $\varphi : V \to G$ with $U_{xy} = \varphi(x)^{-1}\varphi(y)$ on tree edges; the gauge transformation $g_x = \varphi(x)$ turns every tree link into $\mathbf{1}$. The frozen theorem gives existence only. With an explicit potential (a root $r$ in each component, $\varphi(r) = \mathbf{1}$, and $\varphi(y) = \varphi(x)U_{xy}$ along tree edges), a chord $c$ from $s$ to $t$ becomes $\varphi(s)U_c\varphi(t)^{-1}$, a two-sided translation once the tree links are fixed; so under the product Haar measure the gauge-fixed chord variables are independent and Haar distributed (for $\mathrm{U}(1)$ see [@thm-tree-phase-transport]). The full square-lattice graph is not chordal (every plaquette is a chordless four-cycle), and a potential representation in general does not exist (the four-cycle remark after [@thm-appe-chordal-gain-potential]); what remains after the tree gauge is exactly the set of plaquette holonomies.
2. **Central convolution lemma.** Let $X_1, \dots, X_m$ be independent $G$-valued random elements, each with a conjugation-invariant law. Then for every $g \in G$, $\mathbb{E}[\chi_F(gX_1\cdots X_m)] = \chi_F(g)\prod_k\frac{\mathbb{E}[\chi_F(X_k)]}{d_F}$. Proof: $M_k = \mathbb{E}[F(X_k)]$ commutes with every $F(h)$, so by Schur's lemma $M_k = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\chi_F(X_k)]}{d_F}\mathbf{1}$; integrate over $X_m$ first and induct. The order of the factors does not matter, and if some $X_k$ is Haar distributed and $F$ is nontrivial, the expectation vanishes.
3. **Planar regions and simple loops.** Let $P$ be a finite set of closed unit squares in $\mathbb{Z}^2$ and $\Gamma$ the plane graph of their vertices and edges (assumed connected; otherwise treat each component), with measure $Z^{-1}\prod_{p \in P}\phi(U_p)\prod_\ell\mathrm{d}U_\ell$ and free boundary (no constraint on boundary links). Let $C$ be a simple closed curve made of edges of $\Gamma$, $D$ the set of unit squares inside $C$, and $A = \lvert D\rvert$. If $D \subseteq P$, then $\frac{1}{d_F}\mathbb{E}[\chi_F(W_C)] = w^A$; if $D$ contains a square not in $P$, then $\mathbb{E}[\chi_\rho(W_C)] = 0$ for every nontrivial irreducible $\rho$; and $Z = \left(\int_G\phi\,\mathrm{d}U\right)^{\lvert P\rvert}$. The proof has four steps. (a) The dual edges of the chords (edges not in a spanning tree $T$) form a spanning tree $T^*$ of the dual graph (vertices: all faces of $\Gamma$, the unbounded one included): the two sides of a chord are different faces (otherwise it would be a bridge, and bridges lie in every spanning tree); the number of chords $E - V + 1$ equals the number of bounded faces (Euler's formula); and a cycle of dual chord edges would be a closed curve separating the two ends of a crossed chord, which $T$ joins without meeting the curve. (b) Root $T^*$ at the unbounded face and let $c(f)$ be the chord dual to the edge from a bounded face $f$ to its parent. The chords on the boundary of $f$ are $c(f)$ and the $c(f')$ of its children, each once, so in the tree gauge the lasso of $f$ is $y_f = a_fh_{c(f)}^{\pm 1}b_f$, where $h_c$ is the variable on the chord $c$ and $a_f, b_f$ involve only the children's chord variables. (c) Integrate over the $h_{c(f)}$ by depth in $T^*$ from the top; at each step $h_{c(f)}$ occurs only in $y_f$, and the substitution preserves Haar measure, so the lassos of all bounded faces are independent and Haar distributed. The weight of $p \in P$ is $\phi(U_p) = \phi(y_p)$ ($y_p$ is conjugate to $U_p$ or to $U_p^{-1}$), so $y_p$ has law $\nu$, while a bounded face not in $P$ (a hole) has a Haar-distributed lasso. (d) Choose $T \supseteq C \setminus \{e_0\}$ and a base point on $C$; then $\mathrm{hol}(C) = h_{e_0}^{\pm 1}$. The faces inside and outside $C$ are separated only by edges of $C$, so the faces inside $C$ form the subtree of $T^*$ below the inside face at $e_0$; solving (b) from the leaves up writes $h_{e_0}^{\pm 1}$ as a product of the lassos of all bounded faces inside $C$, each exactly once (possibly inverted). Item 2 and the reality of $w$ finish the proof. This is a non-abelian Stokes identity in the tree gauge and needs no shelling order (compare [§34.30.3](ch34-s30-arc-body-calculus-and-finite-stokes.md#sec-ch34-30-3)); for a rectangle with the comb tree (the bottom-row horizontal links and all vertical links), the $b_i$ and $V_{i,J}$ of item 4 of [@rem-ch39-plaquette-sagitta-and-wilson-action] are all $\mathbf{1}$, the lassos are the $P_{i,j}$, and the factorization there is a special case. The region need not be convex and $C$ may run along its boundary; only $D \subseteq P$ matters.
4. **String tension.** With $\sigma = -\log w$, $D \subseteq P$ gives $\frac{1}{d_F}\mathbb{E}[\chi_F(W_C)] = e^{-\sigma A}$. Let $F$ be nontrivial and take the Wilson weight. Then $z(\beta) = \int_G\phi\,\mathrm{d}U$ is an entire function of $\beta$, positive on the real axis, $w = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\log z$, $w(0) = 0$, and $w'(\beta) = \operatorname{Var}_\nu(\operatorname{Re}\chi_F)/d_F^2 > 0$: if $\operatorname{Re}\chi_F$ were $\nu$-a.e. constant, then by continuity and because $\nu$ has full support it would equal its value $d_F$ at $\mathbf{1}$ everywhere, forcing $F(U) = \mathbf{1}$ for all $U$. Together with $\operatorname{Re}\chi_F \le d_F$, for $\beta > 0$ this gives $0 < w(\beta) < 1$, and $\sigma(\beta)$ is positive, strictly decreasing and real analytic; since $Z = z^{\lvert P\rvert}$, the free energy per plaquette $-\log z$ is real analytic too. So for a fixed compact group $G$ and nontrivial $F$ (for instance fixed $N$), the two-dimensional free-boundary theory has an exact area law with $\sigma > 0$ at every $\beta > 0$, and its free energy has no singularity. For small $\beta$, $w = \frac{\beta}{2d_F^2}\left(1 + \int_G\chi_F^2\,\mathrm{d}U\right) + O(\beta^2)$, where $\int_G\chi_F^2\,\mathrm{d}U$ is $1$ if $F$ is equivalent to its conjugate representation and $0$ otherwise: $\frac{\beta}{2}$ for $\mathrm{U}(1)$, $\frac{\beta}{4}$ for $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, $\frac{\beta}{2N^2}$ for $\mathrm{SU}(N)$ with $N \ge 3$, $\beta$ for $\mathbb{Z}_2$. Exact expansions:

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   \mathrm{U}(1):&\quad \sigma = -\log\frac{\beta}{2} + \frac{\beta^2}{8} - \frac{5\beta^4}{384} + O(\beta^6), &\quad \sigma &= \frac{1}{2\beta} + \frac{1}{4\beta^2} + O(\beta^{-3})\ (\beta \to \infty),\\
   \mathrm{SU}(2):&\quad \sigma = -\log\frac{\beta}{4} + \frac{\beta^2}{24} - \frac{\beta^4}{576} + O(\beta^6), &\quad \sigma &= \frac{3}{2\beta} + \frac{3}{4\beta^2} + O(\beta^{-3})\ (\beta \to \infty).
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   With $\beta = 1/(g^2a^2)$ for $\mathrm{U}(1)$ and $\beta = 4/(g^2a^2)$ for $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, the leading large-$\beta$ term gives $\sigma/a^2 \to \frac{1}{2}g^2C_2(F)$ with $C_2(F) = 1$ and $\frac{3}{4}$ respectively, in agreement with $\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}g^2C_2(F)\cdot\text{area}\right)$ for a simple loop in continuum two-dimensional Yang–Mills theory (literature result).

With periodic boundary conditions the plaquette variables are no longer independent. With $\mathbb{Z}/2$ coefficients there is exactly one linear relation among them, and this is frozen:

::: {.theorem #thm-ch39-periodic-grid-plaquette-relation title="The unique linear relation among Z/2 plaquette values on a periodic grid, and the counts of flat labels" lean="D5/S3/Fourier/CharacterSelection/PeriodicGridHolonomy.plaquette_relation_rank_one,D5/S3/Fourier/CharacterSelection/PeriodicGridHolonomy.periodic_grid_linear_statistics"}
Use the notation of [@thm-ch39-periodic-grid-flat-not-exact], with $M, N$ nonzero natural numbers. For coefficients $\mathit{coeff} : \operatorname{Fin} M \to \operatorname{Fin} N \to \mathbb{Z}/2$, let $\operatorname{PlaquetteRelation}(\mathit{coeff})$ mean: for every edge label $y$, $\sum_i\sum_j\mathit{coeff}(i, j)\cdot\operatorname{plaquette}(y, i, j) = 0$. Regard edge labels as elements of the $\mathbb{Z}/2$-vector space $\operatorname{EdgeSpace}(M, N) = (\operatorname{Fin} M \to \operatorname{Fin} N \to \mathbb{Z}/2)^2$, let $\operatorname{plaquetteLinear}$ be the linear map $y \mapsto \operatorname{plaquette}(y, \cdot, \cdot)$ and $\operatorname{flatSubspace}$ its kernel; write $\mathrm{Exact} = \{y \mid \exists x,\ \operatorname{gradient}(x) = y\}$, $\mathrm{Flat} = \{y \mid \operatorname{Flat}(y)\}$, and $\mathrm{All}$ for the set of all edge labels. Then:

1. $\operatorname{PlaquetteRelation}(\mathit{coeff}) \Longleftrightarrow \forall i\, j,\ \mathit{coeff}(i, j) = \mathit{coeff}(0, 0)$;
2. $\dim_{\mathbb{Z}/2}\operatorname{range}(\operatorname{plaquetteLinear}) = MN - 1$, $\dim_{\mathbb{Z}/2}\operatorname{flatSubspace} = MN + 1$, $\lvert\mathrm{Flat}\rvert > 0$, $\lvert\mathrm{All}\rvert > 0$, and, as rational numbers,

   $$
   \frac{\lvert\mathrm{Exact}\rvert}{\lvert\mathrm{Flat}\rvert} = \frac{1}{4}, \qquad \frac{\lvert\mathrm{Flat}\rvert}{\lvert\mathrm{All}\rvert} = \frac{1}{2^{MN - 1}}, \qquad \frac{\lvert\mathrm{Exact}\rvert}{\lvert\mathrm{All}\rvert} = \frac{1}{2^{MN + 1}}.
   $$
:::

::: proof
Item 1, "$\Leftarrow$": every edge lies in exactly two plaquettes, so the sum of all plaquette values is zero. "$\Rightarrow$": applying the relation to the label that is $1$ on a single edge shows that the coefficients of the two plaquettes on either side of that edge are equal; going around the rows and columns, every coefficient equals $\mathit{coeff}(0, 0)$. Item 2 follows from the number of flat labels, $2^{MN + 1}$ ($2^{MN - 1}$ gradient parts times two choices for each seam holonomy), the number $2^{2MN}$ of all labels, and rank–nullity; the three ratios are ratios of these counts. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

By item 2 the image of the plaquette map has dimension $MN - 1$; by item 1 it lies in the hyperplane $\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{plaquette}(y, i, j) = 0$, which also has dimension $MN - 1$; so the image is exactly that hyperplane. This gives the exact finite-volume correction to the area law on a torus:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-torus-area-law-correction title="The area law on a torus: the areas on both sides of the loop contribute (manual derivation and literature result)"}
**Manual derivation; the torus formula for non-abelian groups is a literature result, not formalized in this book.** Let $G$ be a compact abelian group (finite abelian or $\mathrm{U}(1)$) with character group $\hat{G}$ and trivial character $\psi_0$, on the $M \times N$ periodic grid ($M, N \ge 2$) with $n = MN$ plaquettes and the weights of [@rem-ch39-tree-gauge-plaquette-independence]. Let $C$ be the counterclockwise boundary of an $R \times T$ rectangle ($1 \le R \le M - 1$, $1 \le T \le N - 1$), $A = RT$, and $\chi \in \hat{G}$. With $f_\psi = \int_G\phi\,\bar{\psi}\,\mathrm{d}U$,

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\chi(W_C)\right] = \frac{\sum_{\psi \in \hat{G}} f_{\chi\psi}^{\,A}\, f_\psi^{\,n - A}}{\sum_{\psi \in \hat{G}} f_\psi^{\,n}}.
$$

1. **Proof.** Use additive notation, with $h_{i,j}$ and $v_{i,j}$ the links from $(i, j)$ to $(i + 1, j)$ and to $(i, j + 1)$, indices modulo $M$ and $N$, and plaquette values $\theta_{i,j} = h_{i,j} + v_{i+1,j} - h_{i,j+1} - v_{i,j}$. (a) The rectangle is a planar disk ($R < M$, $T < N$), and the abelian Stokes identity gives $\theta_C = \sum_{p \in D}\theta_p$, $D$ being the plaquettes of the rectangle. (b) The image of the plaquette map $G^E \to G^n$ is exactly $H = \{\sum_p\theta_p = 0\}$: each link occurs in two plaquettes with opposite signs, so the image lies in $H$; conversely, given $\eta \in H$, put $r_j = \sum_i\eta_{i,j}$, choose $h_{0,j}$ with $h_{0,j} - h_{0,j+1} = r_j$ (solvable because $\sum_jr_j = 0$) and all other $h$ equal to $0$; then for each $j$ the remaining equations sum to zero over $i$ and are solved for $v_{i,j}$ along $i$. For $G = \mathbb{Z}/2$ this is a consequence of the frozen theorem above. (c) A continuous surjective homomorphism of compact groups pushes Haar measure forward to Haar measure on the image. (d) For finite $G$, $\mathbf{1}_H(\theta) = \lvert G\rvert^{-1}\sum_\psi\psi\left(\sum_p\theta_p\right)$; for $\mathrm{U}(1)$ the same expansion holds for functions with absolutely summable Fourier coefficients, and the Fourier coefficients of $e^{\beta\cos\theta}$ are $I_k(\beta)$. Substituting, factorizing over plaquettes and using $f_\psi = f_{\bar{\psi}}$ gives the formula.
2. **Two special cases.** $\mathrm{U}(1)$: $\mathbb{E}[e^{i\theta_C}] = \left(\sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}}I_{k+1}(\beta)^AI_k(\beta)^{n-A}\right)/\left(\sum_kI_k(\beta)^n\right)$. $\mathbb{Z}_2$, $t = \tanh\beta$:

   $$
   \mathbb{E}[W_C] = \frac{t^A + t^{\,n - A}}{1 + t^{\,n}}.
   $$

   On the torus the loop bounds both the $A$ plaquettes and the other $n - A$, and both sides enter the answer; a planar region with free boundary has no second side, and the answer is $w^A$. As $M, N \to \infty$ with $A$ fixed, the torus value tends to $w^A$, because $\lvert f_\psi\rvert < f_{\psi_0}$ for $\psi \ne \psi_0$ and $\sum_\psi\lvert f_\psi/f_{\psi_0}\rvert^m \to 0$.
3. **Numerical checks (`NUM`).** Exact enumeration for $\mathbb{Z}_2$ on the $3 \times 3$ torus ($A = 1, 2, 4$) and for $\mathbb{Z}_3$ on the $2 \times 3$ and $3 \times 3$ tori agrees with the formula to within $10^{-15}$; for example, for $\mathbb{Z}_2$ at $\beta = 1.3$ and $A = 4$ the torus value is $0.813439$ while the planar value is $0.551405$.
4. **Non-abelian groups (literature result).** For a general compact group, gluing a disk, whose boundary holonomy has density $\sum_rd_rf_r^A\chi_r$, to a torus with one hole gives

   $$
   \frac{1}{d_F}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\chi_F(W)\right] = \frac{\sum_{r,s}N_{rF}^{\,s}\,\frac{d_r}{d_Fd_s}\,f_r^{\,A}f_s^{\,n-A}}{\sum_rf_r^{\,n}},
   $$

   where $r, s$ run over the irreducible representations, $f_r = \frac{1}{d_r}\int_G\phi\,\bar{\chi}_r\,\mathrm{d}U$, and $N_{rF}^{\,s}$ is the multiplicity of $s$ in $r \otimes F$; the term with $s$ trivial is the planar value $w^A$. The book does not give a lattice proof for general compact groups; exact enumeration for the two-dimensional representation of the symmetric group $S_3$ on the $2 \times 2$ torus (without gauge fixing, $6^8$ configurations, $A = 1$) and on the $2 \times 3$ torus (spanning-tree gauge, $A = 1, 2$) agrees with the formula to within $10^{-15}$ (`NUM`).

For the non-abelian torus formula see Migdal (1975), B. Rusakov (*Mod. Phys. Lett. A* **5**, 693–703, 1990, DOI `10.1142/S0217732390000780`) and E. Witten (*Commun. Math. Phys.* **141**, 153–209, 1991, DOI `10.1007/BF02100009`).
:::

The exact area law is a statement about closed loops. Open comparisons cannot be read at all:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-elitzur-closed-comparisons title="Only closed comparisons are readable: a finite form of Elitzur's theorem and the completeness of Wilson loops (manual derivation and literature result)"}
**Manual derivation; that local gauge symmetry cannot break spontaneously in infinite volume (Elitzur 1975), and that non-abelian gauge invariants are determined by Wilson loops, are literature results, not formalized in this book.** Let $\Gamma$ be a finite graph, $G$ a compact group, and $\mu = Z^{-1}e^{-S(U)}\prod_\ell\mathrm{d}U_\ell$ with $S$ gauge invariant: $S(U^g) = S(U)$, where $(U^g)_{xy} = g_xU_{xy}g_y^{-1}$. For a vertex $x$ and $h \in G$, let $\tau_h^x$ be the gauge transformation equal to $h$ at $x$ and to $\mathbf{1}$ elsewhere, and $P_xF(U) = \int_GF(\tau_h^xU)\,\mathrm{d}h$.

1. **Finite form.** For every bounded measurable $F$ and every matrix entry $\rho_{ij}$ of a nontrivial irreducible unitary representation $\rho$,

   $$
   \mathbb{E}_\mu\left[\int_GF(\tau_h^xU)\,\overline{\rho_{ij}(h)}\,\mathrm{d}h\right] = 0, \qquad \mathbb{E}_\mu[F] = \mathbb{E}_\mu[P_xF].
   $$

   Proof: product Haar measure is invariant under $U_\ell \mapsto hU_\ell$ and $U_\ell \mapsto U_\ell h^{-1}$, and so is $S$; hence $\mathbb{E}_\mu[F \circ \tau_h^x] = \mathbb{E}_\mu[F]$ for every $h$, and Schur orthogonality gives $\int_G\overline{\rho_{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}h = 0$. In particular, $\mathbb{E}_\mu[\rho(U_\ell)] = 0$ (as a matrix) for every link $\ell$ and every nontrivial irreducible $\rho$; $\mathbb{E}_\mu[\rho(\mathrm{hol}\,\gamma)] = 0$ for every open path $\gamma$ with distinct endpoints; and $\mathbb{E}_\mu[F]$ equals the expectation of the average of $F$ over the whole gauge group.
2. **A volume-independent bound.** Add a bounded term that breaks the gauge symmetry: $\mu_J \propto e^{-S(U) + J\Psi(U)}\prod_\ell\mathrm{d}U_\ell$ with $J \in \mathbb{R}$, $\Psi = \sum_\ell\psi(U_\ell)$, $\psi$ real and $\lvert\psi\rvert \le K$ (for example $\psi = \operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}$ and $K = N$ when $G \subseteq \mathrm{U}(N)$). Let $\deg(x)$ be the number of links at $x$. If $P_xF = 0$, then

   $$
   \left\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\mu_J}[F]\right\rvert \le \left(e^{2K\lvert J\rvert\deg(x)} - 1\right)\lVert F\rVert_\infty ,
   $$

   and the right-hand side does not depend on the size of the lattice. Proof: $\tau_h^x$ changes only the $\deg(x)$ link terms at $x$, so $\lvert\Psi(\tau_h^xU) - \Psi(U)\rvert \le 2K\deg(x)$; a change of variables gives $\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\mu_J}[F \circ \tau_h^x] - \mathbb{E}_{\mu_J}[F]\rvert \le (e^{2K\lvert J\rvert\deg(x)} - 1)\lVert F\rVert_\infty$; then average over $h$. If $F$ depends only on finitely many links with endpoints $x_1, \dots, x_k$ and $P$ is the average over the whole gauge group, then $F - PF = \sum_j(1 - P_{x_j})P_{x_{j+1}}\cdots P_{x_k}F$, each term satisfies the condition above and has sup norm at most $2\lVert F\rVert_\infty$, so $PF = 0$ implies $\lvert\mathbb{E}_{\mu_J}[F]\rvert \le 2\sum_j(e^{2K\lvert J\rvert\deg(x_j)} - 1)\lVert F\rVert_\infty$. For continuous $F$ of this kind the bound passes to every weak limit point of finite-volume measures, so in every such infinite-volume limit the expectation of a local observable whose gauge-invariant part vanishes tends to zero as $J \to 0$. This is Elitzur's argument written out at finite volume; for a global symmetry the transformation acts on every site, and the corresponding bound grows with the volume.
3. **$\mathrm{U}(1)$: gauge invariants are spanned by Wilson loops on simple cycles.** Orient the links, let $\theta \in \mathbb{T}^E$, with gauge transformations $\theta_{xy} \mapsto \theta_{xy} + \alpha_x - \alpha_y$. A trigonometric polynomial $F = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}^E}c_ne^{in\cdot\theta}$ is gauge invariant if and only if $c_n = 0$ whenever $\partial n \ne 0$, where $(\partial n)_x$ is the outgoing minus the incoming integer current at $x$. If $\partial n = 0$ and $n \ne 0$, walk along edges with $n_e \ne 0$ in the direction of $n$; since inflow equals outflow at each vertex the walk never stops and closes a simple directed cycle $\gamma$; subtracting its indicator current keeps $\partial n = 0$ and strictly lowers $\lvert n\rvert_1$. By induction $n = \sum_kz_{\gamma_k}$ with $\gamma_k$ simple closed walks that follow the direction of $n$, so $e^{in\cdot\theta} = \prod_kW_{\gamma_k}$: every gauge-invariant trigonometric polynomial is a linear combination of products of Wilson loops on simple cycles, and every continuous gauge-invariant function is a uniform limit of such (Fejér means commute with the gauge average). In two dimensions the area law is exact at every $\beta$; in three and four dimensions what can be proved is the strong-coupling end:

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-strong-coupling-higher-dimensions title="Three and more dimensions: the strong-coupling leading term and the known phase structure (manual derivation and literature result)"}
**Manual derivation (three-dimensional $\mathbb{Z}_2$ on finite boxes); the rest are literature results, not formalized in this book.**

1. **Three-dimensional $\mathbb{Z}_2$ on a finite box.** Let the box consist of $L_x \times L_y \times L_z$ unit cubes, with free boundary, the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ Wilson action, and $t = \tanh\beta$. Let $C$ be the boundary of a planar set $S_0$ of $A$ plaquettes in a coordinate plane. Then, as $t \to 0$,

   $$
   \mathbb{E}[W_C] = t^A\left(1 + c_1t^4 + O(t^6)\right),
   $$

   where $c_1$ is the number of unit cubes with exactly one face in $S_0$ ($c_1 = 2A$ when the plane of $S_0$ is not a face of the box, $c_1 = A$ when it is). Proof: expanding $e^{\beta\sigma} = \cosh\beta\,(1 + t\sigma)$ and summing over the links gives $\mathbb{E}[W_C] = \left(\sum_{\partial S = C}t^{\lvert S\rvert}\right)/\left(\sum_{\partial S = 0}t^{\lvert S\rvert}\right)$, with $S$ ranging over sets of plaquettes ($\mathbb{Z}/2$ coefficients). The box is contractible, so the closed $S$ are exactly the boundaries $\partial Q$ of unique sets $Q$ of cubes, the $S$ with boundary $C$ are exactly the $S_0 \triangle \partial Q$, and $\lvert S_0 \triangle \partial Q\rvert = A + \lvert\partial Q\rvert - 2\lvert S_0 \cap \partial Q\rvert$. Take $S_0$ horizontal. Along each vertical column of cubes, $\partial Q$ contains an even number of horizontal faces, at most one of which lies in $S_0$, so each column contributes non-negatively to $\lvert\partial Q \setminus S_0\rvert - \lvert\partial Q \cap S_0\rvert$ through its horizontal faces; vertical faces never lie in $S_0$, and for $Q \ne \varnothing$ some horizontal layer of $Q$ is nonempty and has perimeter at least $4$, with equality only for a single cube. Hence for $Q \ne \varnothing$, $\lvert\partial Q\rvert - 2\lvert S_0 \cap \partial Q\rvert \ge 4$, with equality exactly when $Q$ is a single cube with one face in $S_0$; this quantity is always even, so in all other cases it is at least $6$, and the terms with $Q \ne \varnothing$ in the denominator are $O(t^6)$. In particular $S_0$ is the unique minimal surface bounded by $C$, the leading term is the minimal-area tiling term $t^A = w^A$, and the correction is of relative order $t^4$.
2. **Numerical checks (`NUM`).** Exact polynomials: on a single cube $\mathbb{E}[W] = \frac{t + t^5}{1 + t^6}$; on the surface of the $2 \times 2 \times 1$ box, $A = 4$ gives $t^4 + 4t^8 + 7t^{12} + \cdots$; in the middle plane of the $2 \times 2 \times 2$ box, $A = 1, 2, 4$ give $t + 2t^5 - 2t^7 + \cdots$, $t^2 + 4t^6 - 2t^8 + \cdots$, $t^4 + 8t^8 + 26t^{12} + \cdots$; on interior planes of the $3 \times 3 \times 2$ and $2 \times 3 \times 3$ boxes, $c_1$ for $A = 1, 2, 3$ also agrees with item 1.
3. **General dimension and general groups (literature result).** K. Osterwalder and E. Seiler (*Ann. Phys.* **110**, 440–471, 1978, DOI `10.1016/0003-4916(78)90039-8`) proved, for lattice gauge theories with compact gauge group, convergence of the strong-coupling (small-$\beta$) expansion and an area-law upper bound for Wilson loops at small $\beta$ in every dimension; for the exact hypotheses (representation, form of the bound) see the paper, which the book does not restate. A systematic account of strong-coupling expansions: J.-M. Drouffe and J.-B. Zuber (*Phys. Rep.* **102**, 1–119, 1983, DOI `10.1016/0370-1573(83)90034-0`).
4. **The small-$\beta$ area law need not persist to all $\beta$ (literature result).** Four-dimensional $\mathrm{U}(1)$ has a non-confining phase at large $\beta$: A. H. Guth (*Phys. Rev. D* **21**, 2291–2307, 1980, DOI `10.1103/PhysRevD.21.2291`); J. Fröhlich and T. Spencer (*Commun. Math. Phys.* **83**, 411–454, 1982, DOI `10.1007/BF01213610`). Three-dimensional $\mathrm{U}(1)$ confines at all $\beta$: M. Göpfert and G. Mack (*Commun. Math. Phys.* **82**, 545–606, 1982, DOI `10.1007/BF01961240`). The three-dimensional $\mathbb{Z}_2$ gauge theory is dual to the three-dimensional Ising model and has a phase transition: F. J. Wegner (*J. Math. Phys.* **12**, 2259–2272, 1971, DOI `10.1063/1.1665530`).
5. **Where the argument stops.** The two-dimensional exactness ([@rem-ch39-tree-gauge-plaquette-independence]) comes from the independence of plaquette variables in the plane; in three or more dimensions the plaquettes of every cube satisfy a Bianchi relation ([@rem-ch39-cube-bianchi-sagitta]), independence fails, and item 1 only gives the leading strong-coupling term. The area law at all $\beta$ and the mass gap for four-dimensional non-abelian theories are the open problem below (`OPEN`).

::: {.problem #prob-ch39-four-dimensional-area-law-and-mass-gap title="Area law at all couplings for four-dimensional non-abelian lattice gauge theory, and the continuum mass gap (open problem)"}
**`OPEN`.** Let $G = \mathrm{SU}(N)$, $N \ge 2$, with the Wilson action on the four-dimensional hypercubic lattice.

1. Prove or disprove: for every $\beta > 0$ there are $\sigma(\beta) > 0$ and a constant $c$ such that, in every infinite-volume limit (weak limit point of finite-volume measures), rectangular $R \times T$ Wilson loops in the defining representation satisfy $\frac{1}{N}\lvert\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Tr}W_{R \times T}]\rvert \le ce^{-\sigma(\beta)RT}$ for all $R, T$.
2. Construct a nontrivial four-dimensional continuum quantum Yang–Mills theory and prove that its mass gap is positive; for general compact simple groups this is one of the Millennium Problems of the Clay Mathematics Institute (problem description by A. Jaffe and E. Witten).

Known: in two dimensions the area law is exact at every $\beta$ ([@rem-ch39-tree-gauge-plaquette-independence]); at small $\beta$ it holds in every dimension (Osterwalder and Seiler 1978, under the hypotheses stated there); for four-dimensional $\mathrm{U}(1)$ it fails at large $\beta$ (Guth 1980; Fröhlich and Spencer 1982). No result in this book implies items 1 or 2.
