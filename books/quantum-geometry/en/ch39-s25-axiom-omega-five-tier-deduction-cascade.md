**From zeros to Schur pivots.** Take $N$ distinct nodes $s_j$ in the half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>1/2$ and set $G_{jk}=K_\epsilon(s_j,s_k)$. In the [zero expansion of §31.3](ch34-s31-arc-calculus-riemann-and-hermite-biehler.md#sec-ch34-31-3), let $m_\rho$ be the multiplicity of each distinct zero $\rho$. For any coefficients $c_j\in\mathbb C$,

$$
c^*Gc=\frac12\sum_\rho m_\rho\,|R(\rho)|^2,\qquad
R(z)=\sum_{j=1}^N\frac{c_j\,\xi(s_j)}{s_j-z}.
$$

RH gives $\xi(s_j)\ne0$ for every node. If the quadratic form vanishes, the rational function $R$ vanishes at infinitely many distinct zeros. Clearing the denominators gives a polynomial of degree at most $N-1$, so its numerator vanishes identically. Taking the residue at each $s_j$ gives $c_j=0$. Thus $G$ is positive definite. For any partition into nonempty sets $I,P$ and any $y\ne0$, the quadratic form of the Schur complement is the minimum of $(x,y)^*G(x,y)$ over $x$. It is attained at $x=-G_{II}^{-1}G_{IP}y$ and hence is strictly positive. This also supplies the witness $M_L\equiv1$ for the existence question in [@prob-ch34-schur-hb-kernel-positivity-bridge]. The multiplicative subtrees in [@prob-ch34-arithmetic-pivot-area-law] specify a separate family of matrices. Relating them to $G$ and bounding the norm across subtrees are additional steps.

**How many nodes can one quaternionic cell represent?** Take real nodes $s_j>1/2$. Conjugate zeros occur in pairs, so $G$ is real. In §39.1 the single-cell reading satisfies $2\kappa(q_j,q_k)-1=\langle q_j,q_k\rangle_{\mathbb R^4}$ for unit quaternions $q_j$. **Approximating all spectral readings on one integer shell.** For finitely many distinct real nodes $s_j>1/2$, put $u_j=s_j-1/2$. Pair the zeros $\tfrac12\pm i\gamma$ for $\gamma>0$, with multiplicity $m_\gamma$. This pair contributes two real coordinates at node $j$,

$$

$$

Their dot product across two nodes is exactly the pair's contribution to $G$. Since $\xi(1)>0$ and the real half-line $s>1/2$ is zero-free, the coordinates are nonnegative. Divide the full coordinate vector for each node by $\sqrt{G_{jj}}$ to obtain a nonnegative unit vector $x_j$ with $\langle x_j,x_k\rangle=H_{jk}$.

Choosing $D$ and then $Q$ makes the bound smaller than $\eta$. Positive definiteness gives $H_{jk}<1$, so the $A_j$ are distinct. The Tier 1 half-angle readings have half this error. **Can scale inversion select the mode weights on a rotor circle? (Model condition and hand calculation.)** Choose a circle in a three-dimensional rotor. Mode $n\in\mathbb Z$ winds $n$ times as the phase completes one turn. Prescribe quadratic heat damping and allow detector weights $w_n=w_{-n}\ge0$, with the zero mode calibrated by $w_0=1$. Assume the reading $\Theta_w(t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}w_n e^{-\pi t n^2}$ is finite for each $t>0$. Impose the following model condition on these readings.

$$
\Theta_w(t)=t^{-1/2}\Theta_w(1/t)\qquad(t>0).
$$

This condition selects $w_n=1$ uniquely. To see how readings at one position can determine the weights, introduce two solutions of the heat equation at position $x$.

$$
U(t,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt t}\sum_n w_n e^{-\pi(x-n)^2/t},\qquad
V(t,x)=\sum_n w_n e^{-\pi t n^2}\cos(2\pi n x).
$$

Both solve the heat equation $\partial_x^2 u=4\pi\partial_t u$. At the origin, scale inversion gives $U(t,0)=V(t,0)$. Successive time derivatives determine all even spatial derivatives there, and evenness makes the odd spatial derivatives vanish. At fixed $t>0$ both functions are entire in $x$. A Gaussian series with slightly slower decay bounds the terms and the required derivatives on compact sets. It follows that $U=V$ everywhere. The integer frequencies make $V$ periodic with period one, so $U(t,k)=U(t,0)$ at every integer $k$. Multiply by $\sqrt t$ and let $t\downarrow0$. The Gaussian sum centered at $k$ tends to $w_k$ and the right side tends to $w_0$. Conversely, Poisson summation gives scale inversion for unit weights. [Jacobi transformation formula](https://dlmf.nist.gov/20.7.E32)

If the condition is tested at only $m$ scales, perturb the weights of $m+1$ pairs of nonzero modes. Preserving the $m$ equalities places $m$ homogeneous linear constraints on the changes. A nonzero solution always exists and can be scaled to preserve strict positivity. Both sides of reciprocity are holomorphic for $\operatorname{Re}t>0$. Hence exact test scales with an accumulation point on the positive real axis already determine all weights by the identity theorem. \mathbb E[2\kappa(1,q_W(y))-1]
=\sum_{\gamma>0}p_\gamma\int_0^\infty\gamma e^{-\gamma w}\cos(wy)\,dw
=\sum_{\gamma>0}p_\gamma\frac{\gamma^2}{\gamma^2+y^2}=F(y^2).
$$

The exact curve determines the probability law on nonnegative frequencies uniquely. Assign $W$ an independent fair sign. The resulting symmetric distribution has the cosine average above as its characteristic function. A characteristic function determines its distribution, and taking the absolute value recovers $W$. Its density on $w>0$ is $\sum_{\gamma>0}p_\gamma\gamma e^{-\gamma w}$. The arithmetic spectrum specifies the mixing weights and rates. Each individual reading uses a single fixed-axis circle in $S^3$. Interpreting the sampling law and the scale of $y$ as a physical field evolution requires independent physical input.

Two offsets suffice to test the single-frequency double-angle law. Write $A(y)=F(y^2)$. The identity $\cos(2x)=2\cos^2 x-1$ gives $A(2y)-2A(y)^2+1=2\operatorname{Var}(\cos(Wy))$. A single frequency gives zero on the right. The mixture density above is positive for $w>0$, so the variance is strictly positive for $y\ne0$. At $y=4$, interval evaluation on the real axis gives $F(16)=0.975746642870670$ and $F(64)=0.916843302283509$, each within $10^{-15}$. Substitution yields $0.01268<F(64)-2F(16)^2+1<0.01269$. [Interval certificate for the readings](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/786#issuecomment-7068457253)

For a finite set of frequencies $w_j\ge0$ with probability weights $\alpha_j$, the uniform error on the whole half-line is at least $1$. Indeed, the phase vectors $(e^{i2^n w_j})_j$ have a convergent subsequence in the compact torus. The differences $y_\ell$ between successive times along that subsequence tend to infinity, and all $e^{iy_\ell w_j}$ tend to $1$. Thus $\sum_j\alpha_j\cos(w_jy_\ell)\to1$, whereas dominated convergence gives $F(y_\ell^2)\to0$.

There is also a finite-dimensional obstruction for the full two-node reading $h(s,t)$. Since $h(s,t)\to0$ for each fixed $s>1/2$, choose $s_j>s_{j-1}$ recursively so that $0<h(s_i,s_j)<1/j$ for every $i<j$. Map the nodes in any way to a fixed finite-dimensional real unit sphere. Their images have a convergent subsequence. Inner products of consecutive images along that subsequence approach $1$, while the corresponding values of $h$ approach $0$. Thus the uniform absolute error over all node pairs is at least $1$. By [@thm-ch39-quaternion-product-feature-rank], the product reading of any fixed number $N$ of rotors has such a unit feature representation, so the bound also holds for every nonlinear assignment of rotors to nodes. For any $m$ distinct offsets $0=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_{m-1}$, this construction takes exactly $\lceil m/2\rceil$ modes. Place weight $p_\gamma$ at $\lambda=\gamma^{-2}$ to form the measure $\mu$. At offset $x$ a single mode gives the planar vector $v_\lambda(x)=(1,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x)/(1+\lambda x^2)$. Its angle is $\arctan(\sqrt{\lambda}\,x)$ and its length is the cosine of that angle. $$
v_\lambda(x)\cdot v_\lambda(y)=\frac{x f_x(\lambda)+y f_y(\lambda)}{x+y}.
$$

At $x=y=0$ the dot product is $1$. Averaging over the shared mode gives the unnormalized kernel $K(x,y)$. Dividing by $\sqrt{K(x,x)K(y,y)}$ recovers the two-node overlap. It therefore suffices to integrate the $m$ functions exactly with one quadrature rule. Set $D(\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(1+\lambda x_i^2)$. Each $D f_{x_i}$ is a polynomial of degree at most $m-1$. Gaussian quadrature for the positive measure $d\mu/D$ with $n=\lceil m/2\rceil$ nodes has positive weights $c_j$, is exact through degree $2n-1$, and has nodes $\lambda_j$ inside the support interval. The required mode weights are $c_jD(\lambda_j)$ and sum to one because $f_0=1$. The four-point values above can also be obtained by interpolating $F(u)$ at $u=16,64,144$ with $(1+a u)/(1+b u+c u^2)$ and factoring the denominator. The overlap matrix also gives the minimum. The same zero ordinate $\gamma$ can supply the input to two phase encodings. Write $z=2^{i\gamma}$ and $w=3^{i\gamma}$. Recording the constant and the two individual phases gives the unit vector $(1,z,w)/\sqrt3$. Recording their product $zw=6^{i\gamma}$ as well gives $(1,z,w,zw)/2$. Sampling ordinates from a common probability distribution produces a mixed state for each encoding. The mean squared overlap of two independent draws equals the purity $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)$ of the mixture. The following bound compares the purity losses of the two encodings.

::: {.theorem #thm-ch39-phase-ensemble-purity title="Sharp coefficient for the product phase encoding" lean="D5/S3/Quantum/PureState/PhaseProductFidelity.mixed_coefficient_iff"}
Fix an integer $n\ge1$ and a real number $C$. For a vector $u=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$ of complex coordinates, define

$$
A(u)=\frac{|1+\sum_k u_k|^2}{(n+1)^2},\qquad
B(u)=\prod_k\frac{1+\Re u_k}{2}.
$$

Take any finite list of complex rows $v_i$ with real weights $p_i$. Assume every product $p_i p_j$ is nonnegative and every coordinate of a row of nonzero weight lies in the closed unit disk. Put $u_{ij,k}=\overline{v_{ik}}v_{jk}$ and $L(F)=\sum_{i,j}p_i p_j(1-F(u_{ij}))$. The inequality $L(B)\le C L(A)$ holds for every such finite ensemble if and only if $C\ge(n+1)^2/4$.
:::

::: {.proof}
Omit rows of weight zero. For every remaining pair of inputs, each factor in the product lies in $[0,1]$, so one minus the product is at most the sum of the individual losses. Expanding $A$ and using $|\sum_k u_k|\le n$ gives the bound for each pair of inputs. Summing with the nonnegative pair weights preserves it. For sharpness take two equally weighted rows, one consisting of ones and the other consisting of a single unit phase repeated in every coordinate. As that phase approaches one, the loss ratio tends to $(n+1)^2/4$. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

The two-phase encodings above have $n=2$, giving the sharp coefficient $9/4$. For probability weights and unit phases, $L(A)$ and $L(B)$ are each one minus the purity of the corresponding mixture. Sharpness holds over all finite unit-phase ensembles. Any particular choice of zeros and weights inherits the inequality.

If each complex coordinate may range over the disk of radius $R$, the same formulas define $L(A)$ and $L(B)$ as algebraic deficits. The zero-ordinate encoding has unit-modulus phases, so its probability ensembles satisfy the purity bound for $R=1$.

Consider one row $(t,t)$ of weight one, with $t$ real and nonnegative. Both relative coordinates equal $x=t^2$. The two losses are $1-(1+2x)^2/9$ and $1-(1+x)^2/4$. For $x\ne1$ the ratio of the second to the first is $9(3+x)/[16(2+x)]$. Both losses change sign as $t$ passes through one. The choice of disk radius therefore affects the allowable lower coefficient.

::: {.theorem #thm-ch39-phase-pair-loss-floor title="Sharp lower coefficient for two coordinates on a disk" lean="D5/S3/Quantum/PureState/PhasePairLossFloor.mixed_lower_coefficient_iff"}
Use the two-coordinate functions $A,B,L$ above, and fix real numbers $R\ge0$ and $C$. Consider every finite ensemble with real weights satisfying $p_i p_j\ge0$ and with both coordinates of every nonzero-weight row bounded in modulus by $R$. The inequality $C L(A)\le L(B)$ holds for all these ensembles if and only if the following condition holds.

$$
\begin{cases}
C\le 9(3+R^2)/[16(2+R^2)],&0\le R\le1,\\
C=3/4,&R>1.
\end{cases}
$$

:::

::: {.proof}
For a pair of nonzero-weight rows, let $u_1,u_2$ denote the relative coordinates and put $x=(\Re u_1+\Re u_2)/2$. When $y=R^2\le1$ we have $x\le y$. At the endpoint coefficient $c=9(3+y)/[16(2+y)]$, direct expansion gives

$$
(1-B)-c(1-A)=
\frac{(1-x)(y-x)}{4(2+y)}
+\frac{(\Re u_1-\Re u_2)^2}{16}
+\frac{c(\Im u_1+\Im u_2)^2}{9}.
$$

All three terms are nonnegative. The relative coordinates lie in the unit disk, so $A\le1$ and every smaller coefficient also works. For relative coordinates of arbitrary modulus, the same expansion at $c=3/4$ gives $(1-x)^2/12$ as the first term, with the two remaining squares still nonnegative. Summing with weights $p_i p_j$ gives the ensemble inequality. In the single-row example above, $t=R$ attains the endpoint ratio when $R<1$. At $R=1$, taking $t$ to one from below forces $C\le3/4$. For $R>1$ we can also approach from above, where the change of sign forces $C\ge3/4$. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-jacobi-field-chain title="A nearest-neighbour field chain for the full arithmetic response"}
Place real scalar fields $\phi_0,\phi_1,\ldots$ at every point of the same three-dimensional space. Apply an external source only to $\phi_0$. The local coefficients to determine are the kinetic coefficients of individual fields and the derivative couplings between neighbours. Start with one field and write $u=s/M^2$, where $s=q_E^2$ is Euclidean momentum squared and $M>0$. A unit mass term and kinetic coefficient $a_0$ give the endpoint response $1/(1+a_0u)$. Matching the slope $F'(0)$ forces $a_0=-F'(0)$. Adding the neighbouring field with coupling $b_1$ changes the quadratic coefficient to $a_0^2+b_1^2$. Thus the curvature determines the strength of the first link, $b_1^2=F''(0)/2-a_0^2$.

**From the spectrum to the couplings (manual derivation).** Set $t_\gamma=\gamma^{-2}$ for each distinct positive zero ordinate, and let $\mu=\sum_\gamma p_\gamma\delta_{t_\gamma}$ with $p_\gamma=m_\gamma/(S\gamma^2)$ and $S=\sum_\gamma m_\gamma/\gamma^2$. This probability measure has support in $[0,t_{\gamma_1}]$ and moments $\mu_k=\int t^k\,d\mu=(-1)^kF^{(k)}(0)/k!$. Orthonormalize $1,t,t^2,\ldots$ with positive leading coefficients, writing $P_n$ for the resulting polynomials and $P_{-1}=0$, $P_0=1$. The support is compact and infinite, so polynomials are dense in $L^2(\mu)$ and every normalization step has positive norm. Multiplication $T:f(t)\mapsto tf(t)$ is self-adjoint, and $tP_n$ is orthogonal to every polynomial of degree less than $n-1$. Its matrix $J$ is therefore tridiagonal, with the Jacobi recurrence

$$
tP_n=b_{n+1}P_{n+1}+a_nP_n+b_nP_{n-1},\qquad b_0=0,\quad b_{n+1}>0 .
$$

These conventions make the chain unique for the given $\mu$. Changing basis-vector signs can change the signs of adjacent links. The chain describes the subspace generated by the endpoint, and additional orthogonal fields can be invisible there. The operator $J$ is positive, injective and trace class. Its distinct nonzero eigenvalues are the $t_\gamma$, each occurring once in the cyclic space, so $\operatorname{Tr}J=\sum_\gamma t_\gamma\le S$. The spectral accumulation point $0$ has no eigenvector since $\mu(\{0\})=0$. [Three-term recurrence for orthogonal polynomials, DLMF §18.2](https://dlmf.nist.gov/18.2)

**Normalizing the fields (free-field model).** At Euclidean four-momentum take the dimensionless inverse propagator $I+(s/M^2)J$. The identity supplies the mass term, and the kinetic matrix $J$ couples only neighbouring field labels. $$

D(s)=\frac{F(s/M^2)}{M^2}=\sum_\gamma\frac{p_\gamma B_\gamma/M^2}{s+B_\gamma}.
$$

The pole residue of $F(s/M^2)$ is $p_\gamma B_\gamma$. The normalized endpoint field $\phi_0/M$ has propagator $D$ with residue $m_\gamma/S$. In the cyclic chain each distinct ordinate gives one canonically normalized mode $\psi_\gamma$, coupled to the endpoint source with strength $\sqrt{m_\gamma/S}$. Equivalently, enlarge the field space by assigning an independent free field $\varphi_{\gamma,j}$ to each multiplicity copy, and set $\psi_\gamma=m_\gamma^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{m_\gamma}\varphi_{\gamma,j}$. The endpoint source then couples to $S^{-1/2}\sum_{\gamma,j}\varphi_{\gamma,j}$. Modes orthogonal to the symmetric combination are invisible to this source. Its two-point function has positive spectral measure and its commutator vanishes at spacelike separation. Polynomial growth of the zero count makes the sum a generalized free field after spacetime smearing. The total residue $\sum_\gamma m_\gamma/S$ diverges, so the formal equal-time canonical commutator coefficient is infinite for this source normalization. All fields in this model live on the same three-dimensional space and their labels distinguish internal modes. The entire zero spectrum and scale $M$ enter as inputs. ::: {.theorem #thm-ch39-fifteen-setting-gap title="Error bounds for five unit vectors in four dimensions and fifteen settings" lean="D5/S3/Quantum/Rotors/FifteenSettingGap.fifteen_setting_gap,D5/S3/Quantum/Rotors/FifteenSettingGap.fixed_mixture_probability_gap"}
Assign unit vectors $q_i\in\mathbb R^4$ to five settings and unit vectors $q_{ij}\in\mathbb R^4$ to the ten pair settings ($i<j$). Then $\sum_{i<j}(q_i\cdot q_j)^2\ge5/8$ and $\sum_{i<j}\|q_i+q_j-\sqrt2\,q_{ij}\|^2\ge5/[4(1+\sqrt2)^2]$.

Take a finite collection of such assignments. For each pair of settings, average the half-angle probabilities $(1+q\cdot q')/2$ with the same nonnegative weights summing to one. For every $i<j$, suppose the probability for the two basis settings differs from $1/2$ by at most $\delta$, and the probability for either endpoint with the pair setting differs from $(1+1/\sqrt2)/2$ by at most $\delta$. Then $\delta\ge5/[80(1+\sqrt2)^2(2+4\sqrt2)]$.
:::

::: proof
Write the five vectors as the rows of a matrix with four columns. Its column Gram matrix has trace five. Cauchy–Schwarz on the four diagonal entries and cyclicity of trace give the bound $5/8$ for the ten squared inner products of the rows. The reverse triangle inequality at each pair setting then bounds the sum of squared norms. Expanding each square gives an affine function of three inner products, so the common weights preserve the bound on averaging. Converting the inner products to half-angle probabilities bounds the total change from the thirty comparisons by $20(2+4\sqrt2)\delta$. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-isotropic-scale-response title="Measuring transverse and longitudinal static response in one three-dimensional ensemble"}
Take a rotation-invariant random vector $X\in\mathbb R^3$ in equilibrium at zero field. The model specifies the scale density $f(u)=W(u)/\xi(1/2)$ for its component along any unit axis. For $u\ge0$, set $y_n=\pi n^2e^{2u}$. The circle heat trace gives $W(u)=e^{u/2}\sum_{n\ge1}(4y_n^2-6y_n)e^{-y_n}$. Even extension gives the smooth density $f$. Differentiating its heat series gives $f'(u)<0$ for every $u>0$. Its moment-generating function is $\int_{\mathbb R}e^{hu}f(u)\,du=\xi(1/2+h)/\xi(1/2)$. [Integral representation of the scale density](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/925#issuecomment-7075783754) · [Proof of strict decrease from the heat series](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/943#issuecomment-7076657847)

This specification determines the law of the radius $R=|X|$ uniquely. Conditional on $R=r>0$, a uniformly distributed direction gives an axial component uniform on $[-r,r]$. A radial density $g$ thus gives $f(u)=\int_{|u|}^\infty g(r)/(2r)\,dr$. Inverting the relation yields $g(r)=-2rf'(r)$. The double-exponential tail of the scale density removes the boundary term. The radial density is nonnegative, and integration by parts gives $\int_0^\infty g(r)\,dr=2\int_0^\infty f(r)\,dr=1$. For a profile right-continuous at zero, integrable and differentiable on the positive half-line with $f'\le0$, [`shell_projection_mass`](lean:D5/S3/Quantum/StaticExchange/IsotropicShellInversion.shell_projection_mass) gives nonnegativity of the recovered shell density, its marginal and its total mass. If $g$ is continuous on the positive half-line and $g(r)/(2r)$ is integrable on every positive tail, [`shell_density_unique`](lean:D5/S3/Quantum/StaticExchange/IsotropicShellInversion.shell_density_unique) gives pointwise uniqueness of the shell density for that marginal. For a general radius law the same tail integral determines the measure on the positive half-line, and any atom at the origin would give an atom in the marginal. Sampling this radius and an independent uniform direction constructs the ensemble.

The construction above specifies three internal components. If one fixes only the axial marginal and requires Euclidean rotation invariance, the admissible integer dimensions are exactly $1\le d\le9$. The nine-dimensional spatial density $[-(2\pi r)^{-1}\,d/dr]^4 f(r)$ is positive, and its orthogonal projections realize each lower dimension. The candidate ten-dimensional density is negative at the origin by Fourier inversion, ruling out dimension ten and above. This classification uses a manual derivation and interval arithmetic (python-flint 0.9.0, FLINT 3.6.0). [Nine-dimensional construction, ten-dimensional obstruction and interval certificate](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/943#issuecomment-7076692591)

Six and nine dimensions can also be built from two and three groups of three components, respectively. The groups share a total radius. Split its square in proportion to the squared lengths of the groups of a normalized Gaussian vector. Apply a joint field at $45^\circ$ between axes in two different groups. Sampling the groups independently raises the predicted partition function at $h=16$ by about $36.1\%$. [Three-component construction and joint-field test](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/943#issuecomment-7076750829)

Apply a dimensionless static source $\mathbf h$ by multiplying the weight by $e^{\mathbf h\cdot X}$ and normalizing. By rotation symmetry, the partition function at $h=|\mathbf h|$ depends only on $h$. The partition function is $Z(\mathbf h)=\xi(1/2+h)/\xi(1/2)$. With the background fixed, apply a small source increment first perpendicular and then parallel to it. In each case measure the slope of the mean component in the direction of the increment. Differentiating the partition function gives the mean vector $\nabla\log Z$, so this slope equals the variance of that component in the tilted ensemble. Write $m(h)=\partial_h\log\xi(1/2+h)$ and let $\chi_0=\xi''(1/2)/\xi(1/2)$ be the zero-field variance of one component. The paired product over zeros obtained in this section using the Riemann hypothesis gives $\chi_0=2L(0)$. The Hessian of the radial function $\log Z$ gives

$$
\frac{\chi_\perp(h)}{\chi_0}=\frac{m(h)}{h\chi_0}=F(h^2),\qquad
\frac{\chi_\parallel(h)}{\chi_0}=\frac{m'(h)}{\chi_0}=F(h^2)+2h^2F'(h^2).
$$

Both expressions extend continuously to $1$ at zero field. At $h=16$, the predicted normalized transverse and longitudinal readings are $0.780327$ and $0.527664$, respectively (numerical check). At positive real $s=h^2$ both readings are positive. Locating the zeros by the Riemann hypothesis and using the positive spectral expansion above, the transverse reading has a simple pole at each $s=-\gamma^2$ and the longitudinal reading has a double pole there. When the same axis is used to perturb and read the system at angle $\theta$ to the background, the longitudinal weight is $\cos^2\theta$ and the transverse weight is $\sin^2\theta$. Every nonzero longitudinal weight retains the double pole. A positive-spectrum autocorrelation propagator with no contact term is the Stieltjes transform of a positive measure. Approaching a finite negative real point vertically, it grows at most as $1/|\operatorname{Im}s|$. Only the purely transverse reading is compatible with this propagator class. Here the static source calibrates $h$. ::: {.remark #rem-ch39-projected-diffusion-memory title="Do the three observed components determine their own next-step law?"}
Take the rotation-invariant zero-field equilibrium law in six or nine dimensions from [@rem-ch39-isotropic-scale-response] and fix a three-dimensional orthogonal projection $Y_t=PX_t$. First suppose that the full state follows a conservative, time-homogeneous, stationary and reversible Markov process. Observe three equally spaced times $0,t,2t$. For a real square-integrable reading $g(Y)$, write $T_t$ for the full transition semigroup, $Q$ for conditional expectation given the current three components, and $K_tg=QT_tg$. Reversibility in equilibrium gives

$$
\Delta_t(g)=\mathbb E[g(Y_0)g(Y_{2t})]-\mathbb E[(K_tg(Y_t))^2]
=\|(I-Q)T_tg\|_2^2.
$$

The [orthogonal projection identity for two successive steps](lean:D5/S3/HilbertOperators/VisibleTwoStepDefect.visible_two_step_defect) verifies the operator identity used here in a real inner product space, together with the equivalence between vanishing for every visible vector and invariance of the visible subspace. Here the projection is conditional expectation in equilibrium.

The first term compares the first and last readings directly. The second uses the conditional mean for one time step of the same length. If the projected process is Markov in equilibrium, semigroup composition requires $\Delta_t(g)=0$ for every $t>0$ and every $g$. Conversely, if all these differences vanish, the identity makes each $T_tg$ a function of the current three components. Conditioning the full Markov prediction on the observed history then gives $K_tg(Y_s)$ as the conditional mean of the next reading from time $s$, so the projection is Markov in equilibrium. A strictly positive value detects the influence of the hidden components on prediction. A zero value for one lag and one reading checks only that combination. If the absolute errors of the two estimated terms are at most $\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2$, an observed difference $\widehat\Delta_t(g)>\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2$ certifies a positive true difference.

**Fourth-moment test (manual derivation).** Suppose further that the full state follows an everywhere nondegenerate Itô diffusion with continuous drift and diffusion covariance and dynamics invariant under all internal orthogonal transformations. Set $r=|x|$. Rotation invariance requires a drift of the form $c(r)x$ and a diffusion covariance $a(r)I+b(r)xx^{\mathsf T}$. For a Markov projection the identity above makes $T_t$ preserve functions of $Px$ at every time. Applying the generator to smooth observable tests then makes the projected drift and covariance functions of $y=Px$. Assume that smooth compactly supported functions of the visible coordinates lift to cylinder functions in the equilibrium $L^2$ generator domain, with generator given by the local Itô formula. The equilibrium law in this section has an everywhere positive spatial density, so continuity of the coefficients extends this comparison along each fiber. At any fixed nonzero $y$, varying the hidden coordinates makes $r$ range over $[|y|,\infty)$. Reading the drift parallel to $y$ and the covariance perpendicular and parallel to it shows that $a,b,c$ are constant. Nondegeneracy and positivity on the whole space require $a>0$ and $b\ge0$.

Let $\mu_2,\mu_4$ be the second and fourth moments of one observable coordinate. Multiply its square and fourth power by smooth compactly supported cutoffs on the three-dimensional visible space and apply stationarity. All moments of the visible coordinates are finite, allowing the cutoffs to be removed. Eliminating $c$ gives

$$
a(\mu_4-3\mu_2^2)=2b\mu_2\mu_4\ge0.
$$

For the arithmetic coordinate, $\mu_4/\mu_2^2=3(1-A/S^2)\approx2.791103$, where $A=\sum_{\gamma>0}m_\gamma/\gamma^4$. This follows from the quadratic and quartic terms of the paired zero product. Under the stated diffusion conditions the three projected components therefore cannot evolve autonomously as a Markov process. [Fourth cumulant of the arithmetic coordinate](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/943#issuecomment-7076750829)

**Can the same coordinate carry two energies?** The test also applies to jump processes. Suppose the equilibrium law has an everywhere positive density and the transfer operators $T_t=e^{-tH}$ form a strongly continuous self-adjoint contraction semigroup on equilibrium $L^2$, commuting with the full group $O(d)$ of internal rotations and reflections. Take a visible coordinate $g(x)=x_1$. Averaging over the orthogonal group with Haar probability gives an equivariant representative of the vector $(T_t x_i)_{i=1}^{d}$. The orthogonal transformations fixing $x$ jointly fix precisely the scalar multiples of $x$, so this representative has the form $h_t(|x|)x$. If $\Delta_t(g)=0$ at some $t>0$, then $h_t(|x|)x_1$ depends only on $y=Px$. On almost every fiber with $y_1\ne0$, the conditional law of the radius is equivalent to Lebesgue measure on $(|y|,\infty)$, forcing $h_t$ to be constant almost everywhere on that interval. The intervals overlap and $|y|$ can be arbitrarily small, giving $T_tg=c_tg$. By the spectral theorem and injectivity of $E\mapsto e^{-tE}$, the spectral measure of $g$ is concentrated at a single $E\ge0$. Conversely, a single energy makes $T_tg$ proportional to the visible $g$, giving zero difference. Requiring positive spectral weight for the same coordinate at two distinct arithmetic energies thus forces a strictly positive three-time difference at every positive lag. This assumes symmetry under rotations mixing all internal coordinates, a stronger requirement than ordinary three-dimensional spatial rotations. [Spectrum of one coordinate and memory of the projection](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/981#issuecomment-7079837895)

**What if each three-dimensional field rotates separately?** Impose $O(3)^k$ symmetry for $k=2,3$ groups and keep the same joint equilibrium law. Redraw the full configuration from that law at rate $a>0$ and, between redraws, hold each group length $s$ fixed and evolve its direction independently with generator $(E(s)-a)\Delta_{S^2}/2$, where $E(s)>a$. Each group predicts its own future from its present reading even when the equilibrium groups are correlated, and for a coordinate in that group $T_t x_i=e^{-tE(|Y|)}x_i$. Given energies $E_j$ and normalized positive weights $p_j$, choose $0<a<\inf_j E_j$ and partition the atomless visible-radius law weighted by $x_i^2/\mathbb E[x_i^2]$ into intervals of probabilities $p_j$, taking $E(s)=E_j$ on each corresponding interval to obtain the prescribed spectral lines for an autonomous three-dimensional reading. The six- and nine-dimensional equilibria have the same three-dimensional marginal, so using the same rates gives identical single-group laws at any number of observation times. [Construction under separate three-dimensional rotations](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/981#issuecomment-7079956001)

**One extra scalar suffices.** Write $U_t=|X_t|^2-|Y_t|^2$ for the sum of the squared hidden components. Any two full states with the same $(Y,U)$ differ by an orthogonal transformation of the hidden subspace. Back in the full $O(d)$ model, for any time-homogeneous equivariant transition, they have the same probability of reaching any specified $(Y,U)$ set. Thus $(Y_t,U_t)$ is a Markov state for predicting all later times. Allowing jumps gives another dynamics. At the ticks of a state-independent Poisson clock of rate $\lambda>0$, redraw the whole configuration from equilibrium. The centered linear coordinate has just one correlation decay rate $\lambda$. ::: {.remark #rem-ch39-equal-time-variance title="Can one finite-variance probe reproduce the full frequency response?"}
**Equal-time measurement (manual derivation).** The ensemble in [@rem-ch39-isotropic-scale-response] gives $F$ at different static backgrounds. Now fix one equilibrium state and one centered Hermitian probe. Take its connected Euclidean **time** correlation function at zero spatial momentum as the target, with the elastic zero-frequency and contact terms set to zero. Use one calibration $D_0>0$ and scale $M>0$ for all frequencies. The zero expansion in this section gives

$$
D(\omega^2)=D_0 F(\omega^2/M^2)=\frac{D_0M^2}{S}\sum_{\gamma>0}\frac{m_\gamma}{\omega^2+M^2\gamma^2}.
$$

At zero temperature, integrating over all real frequencies with Fourier measure $d\omega/(2\pi)$ gives the equal-time variance. Keep the full-tower normalization when retaining only terms with $\gamma\le H$. Integrating each term using $\int_{\mathbb R}d\omega/[2\pi(\omega^2+E^2)]=1/(2E)$ gives

$$
V_H=\frac{D_0M}{2S}\sum_{0<\gamma\le H}\frac{m_\gamma}{\gamma}
=\frac{D_0M}{8\pi S}\log^2(H/(2\pi))+O(1)\qquad(H\to\infty).
$$

Here $N(H)$ counts positive ordinates with multiplicity. Integration by parts in $\int_{0^+}^H t^{-1}dN(t)$ and the Riemann–von Mangoldt counting law $N(t)=t\log(t/(2\pi e))/(2\pi)+O(\log t)$ give the displayed coefficient. The counting law is unconditional. This section uses the Riemann hypothesis to identify the arithmetic response with the positive pole sum. The integral for a single positive-mass oscillator over the positive frequency axis is given by [`oscillator_integral`](lean:D5/S3/Quantum/StaticExchange/OscillatorInverseMass.oscillator_integral). For any countable tower with masses $E_n>0$ and residues $a_n\ge0$, the positive-frequency integral is finite exactly when $\sum_n a_n/E_n$ converges, by [`tower_integral_criterion`](lean:D5/S3/Quantum/StaticExchange/OscillatorInverseMass.tower_integral_criterion). All terms are nonnegative, so monotone convergence gives infinite variance for the full tower. At inverse temperature $\beta>0$, take bosonic Matsubara frequencies $\omega_n=2\pi n/\beta$. In the equal-time sum $\beta^{-1}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}D(\omega_n^2)$, each zero-temperature contribution acquires a factor $\coth(\beta M\gamma/2)\ge1$. A single probe with finite equal-time variance therefore also fails to match all discrete frequencies at any fixed positive temperature.

A finite-frequency measurement gives a necessary condition. In a zero-temperature ground state, let $\mu$ be the probe's positive-energy transition measure, of total mass at most the measured equal-time variance $V$. Its spectral representation $D(\omega^2)=\int 2E/(\omega^2+E^2)\,d\mu(E)$ and $2|\omega|E\le\omega^2+E^2$ give $|\omega|D(\omega^2)\le V$. At $|\omega|/M=16$, for instance, matching the target requires $V/(D_0M)\ge16F(256)\approx12.4852241$. The variance and response require independent calibration in the same state. A finite bandwidth tests the frequencies it covers. The preceding zero-field coordinate has variance $2S$ and falls under the same bound. [Equal-time sum, finite-frequency bound and numerical intervals](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/967#issuecomment-7078068828)

**A probe with finite time resolution (model).** A finite-variance example retains each pole while attenuating the high-energy residues. Take a dimensionless parameter $L>0$, write $u=\omega^2/M^2$, and multiply each spectral residue by $w_L(\gamma)=L^2/(\gamma^2+L^2)$. Partial fractions give

$$
K_L(u)=\frac{L^2[F(u)-F(L^2)]}{L^2-u},\qquad K_L(L^2)=-L^2F'(L^2).
$$

After normalization at zero frequency, $L=16$ gives $K_L(256)/K_L(0)\approx0.575087$, compared with $F(256)\approx0.780327$. The weighted inverse-mass sum $\sum_\gamma m_\gamma w_L(\gamma)/\gamma$ converges. Retaining the entire equal-time joint law of the three coordinates fixes each variance at $2S$. For given $M,L$, the zero-frequency gain must therefore satisfy $D_0M=4S^2K_L(0)/(\sum_\gamma m_\gamma w_L(\gamma)/\gamma)$. On the $L^2$ space of that law, take the constant function as vacuum and coordinate multiplication as the probe. Choose a positive, rotation-invariant Hamiltonian for which at each energy $M\gamma$ the spectral weight of a coordinate excitation is proportional to $m_\gamma w_L(\gamma)/\gamma$. This realizes the normalized frequency response. A separate instrument model starts with the ideal unfiltered tower and averages its probe exponentially in time. A measured decay rate $\Lambda$ weights the squared transition amplitude by $\Lambda^2/(\Lambda^2+E^2)$, giving $L=\Lambda/M$. The filter, frequency scale and gain can all be calibrated independently. [Positive-energy realization and filtered prediction](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/967#issuecomment-7078075499) · [Calibrating the probe time constant](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/967#issuecomment-7078108994)

If one further specifies a single three-dimensional internal space, a current-free ground state and the flat Schrödinger operator reconstructed from its density, the coordinate probe sees too few radial levels to supply the complete arithmetic frequencies. The square-root dispersion has a related counting obstruction. [Radial counts for the two dispersions](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/967#issuecomment-7078151889)
:::

**Which propagators can reproduce this response? (Scalar exchange model.)** Let $s=q_E^2>0$ be the squared Euclidean momentum and introduce a free mass scale $M>0$. Set $b_\gamma=M^2\gamma^2$ and retain the weights $p_\gamma=m_\gamma/(S\gamma^2)$. The dimensionless response $A(s)=F(s/M^2)=\sum_\gamma p_\gamma b_\gamma/(s+b_\gamma)$ decreases strictly from $1$ to $0$. If a scalar diagonal propagator without contact terms has a positive spectral representation $D(s)=\int_{[0,\infty)}(s+b)^{-1}\,d\rho(b)$, then $sD(s)$ is nondecreasing because each $s/(s+b)$ is nondecreasing. This excludes $A(s)/s$ as such an autocorrelation propagator. The partial fractions locate the obstruction:

$$
\frac{A(s)}s=\frac1s-\sum_\gamma\frac{p_\gamma}{s+b_\gamma}.
$$

Take two distinguishable real scalar sources. Describe the spectral weight at each mass by a positive semidefinite matrix, with total weight normalized to the identity $I$. Place the matrix $Z$ at zero mass. At squared mass $b_\gamma$, place $p_\gamma(I-Z)$, where

$$
 Z=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&r\\r&1/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 D(s)=\frac{Z}{s}+(I-Z)\sum_\gamma\frac{p_\gamma}{s+b_\gamma}.
$$

Both $Z$ and $I-Z$ have eigenvalues $1/2\pm r$. Thus all these weights are positive semidefinite exactly when $|r|\le1/2$. The mixed response is $sD_{12}(s)=rA(s)$, while the two diagonal responses obey $sD_{11}(s)=sD_{22}(s)=1-A(s)/2$. For $r\ne0$, division by the massless mixed residue $r=\lim_{s\downarrow0}sD_{12}(s)$ recovers the preceding half-value prediction. Doubling the momentum magnitude multiplies $s$ by $4$.

::: {.theorem #thm-ch39-two-source-massless-residue title="Mixed residue for two normalized sources" lean="D5/S3/Quantum/TwoSource/MasslessResidue.sharp_mixed_residue"}
Fix $r\in\mathbb R$. A real $2\times2$ matrix $Z_0$ satisfying $0\preceq Z_0\preceq I$ and $(Z_0)_{12}=r$ exists exactly when $|r|\le1/2$. If $|r|=1/2$, every realization has both diagonal entries equal to $1/2$ and both off-diagonal entries equal to $r$.
:::

::: proof
Consider any two-source model with total spectral weight $I$. Its zero-mass spectral matrix $Z_0$ satisfies $0\preceq Z_0\preceq I$. Its expectations on the unit vectors $e_\pm=(1,\pm1)/\sqrt2$ both lie in $[0,1]$, and their difference is $2(Z_0)_{12}$. If $|(Z_0)_{12}|=1/2$, the two expectations must be $0$ and $1$ in some order. A vector on which a positive semidefinite quadratic form vanishes belongs to the kernel of its matrix. Applying this to $Z_0$ and $I-Z_0$ shows that $e_\pm$ are eigendirections and both diagonal entries must equal $1/2$. Conversely, the preceding construction of $Z$ realizes every $r$ in the interval. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

Positivity also constrains how the two sources couple to massive modes. Let $v,w$ denote the two source directions, choose the sign so that $r>0$, and write $H(s)=D(s)-Z_0/s$ for the positive semidefinite bilinear form contributed by the massive spectrum. If the mixed response satisfies $sD(v,w)=rA(s)$ and $Z_0(v,w)=r$, then

$$
 H(v-w,v-w)=H(v+w,v+w)+\frac{4r[1-A(s)]}{s}.
$$

The second term on the right is strictly positive for every $s>0$. The sources must therefore differ in their couplings to the massive spectrum. In the construction with $r=1/2$ the symmetric combination has no massive component, attaining the least possible difference and making the coefficient $4$ sharp.

The tree-level neutral electroweak propagator gives a single-pole comparison. For conserved sources take the transverse propagator. The gauge basis $B,W^3$ is an orthogonal mixture of the massless photon and the $Z$ boson of mass $m_Z$. Dividing $sD_{BW^3}(s)$ by the massless mixed residue $\sin\theta_W\cos\theta_W$ leaves the two-pole expression $A_Z(s)=m_Z^2/(s+m_Z^2)$. At $s=m_Z^2$ the response takes the value $1/2$. Doubling the momentum magnitude gives $A_Z(4m_Z^2)=1/5$. The arithmetic spectrum predicts approximately $0.330835577358412$ under the same half-value calibration. For a specified pair of conserved currents, denote the products of their photon and $Z$ couplings at the two vertices by $C_{\mathrm{ph}}$ and $C_Z$. If $C_{\mathrm{ph}}\ne0$, the normalized response is $1+(C_Z/C_{\mathrm{ph}})s/(s+m_Z^2)$. The single-pole shape above requires $C_Z=-C_{\mathrm{ph}}$, a condition on the source couplings at both poles. [PDG 2024, Eqs. (10.3a) and (10.3c)](https://pdg.lbl.gov/2024/reviews/rpp2024-rev-standard-model.pdf)

At the endpoint $r=1/2$ of the construction above, the symmetric combination of sources couples only to the massless mode, and the antisymmetric combination couples only to the gapped spectrum. For static point sources at separation $R>0$ in three spatial dimensions, the Green function for each mass $M\gamma$ is $e^{-M\gamma R}/(4\pi R)$. Summing with the mixed spectral residues gives the exchange kernel

$$
\frac{r}{4\pi R}\left(1-\sum_\gamma p_\gamma e^{-M\gamma R}\right).
$$

::: {.remark #rem-ch39-dipole-source-identification title="How many separations identify the derivative coupling of a spin dipole source?"}

::: {.theorem #thm-ch39-dipole-two-distance title="A two-by-two dipole kernel minor and separation for finite spectra" lean="D5/S3/Quantum/StaticExchange/DipoleTwoDistance.profile_minor,D5/S3/Quantum/StaticExchange/DipoleTwoDistance.finite_spectral_separation"}
Put $q(s)=(1+s+s^2/3)e^{-s}$. If $0<x<y$ and $0<a<b$, then $q(xa)q(yb)<q(xb)q(ya)$. Take a finite family of positive scales $g_i$ with nonnegative weights $p_i$, with positive weight at two distinct scales. Set $U(t)=\sum_i p_i g_i^2 q(tg_i)$ and $V(t)=\sum_i p_i g_i^4 q(tg_i)$. For $0<x<y$, one has $U(x)V(y)<V(x)U(y)$, and the map

$$
(h,k)\longmapsto\big(hU(x)+kV(x),\,hU(y)+kV(y)\big)
$$

is injective on $\mathbb R^2$.
:::

::: proof
Differentiate $q(tb)/q(ta)$ on the positive half-line. Its denominator is positive, and its numerator is $-(b-a)$ times positive factors, so the ratio is strictly decreasing. Expand the cross products of the two finite moments and group terms by pairs of scales. Equal scales and zero weights contribute zero. Unequal scales with positive weights contribute with the same sign by the kernel inequality. The support hypothesis gives at least one strict contribution. The two-row coefficient matrix therefore has nonzero determinant, so equal readings force equal coefficients. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
