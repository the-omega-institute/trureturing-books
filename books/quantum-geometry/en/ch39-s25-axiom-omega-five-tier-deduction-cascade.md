**From zeros to Schur pivots.** Take $N$ distinct nodes $s_j$ in the half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>1/2$ and set $G_{jk}=K_\epsilon(s_j,s_k)$. In the [zero expansion of §31.3](ch34-s31-arc-calculus-riemann-and-hermite-biehler.md#sec-ch34-31-3), let $m_\rho$ be the multiplicity of each distinct zero $\rho$. For any coefficients $c_j\in\mathbb C$,

$$
c^*Gc=\frac12\sum_\rho m_\rho\,|R(\rho)|^2,\qquad
R(z)=\sum_{j=1}^N\frac{c_j\,\xi(s_j)}{s_j-z}.
$$

RH gives $\xi(s_j)\ne0$ for every node. If the quadratic form vanishes, the rational function $R$ vanishes at infinitely many distinct zeros. Clearing the denominators gives a polynomial of degree at most $N-1$, so its numerator vanishes identically. Taking the residue at each $s_j$ gives $c_j=0$. Thus $G$ is positive definite. For any partition into nonempty sets $I,P$ and any $y\ne0$, the quadratic form of the Schur complement is the minimum of $(x,y)^*G(x,y)$ over $x$. It is attained at $x=-G_{II}^{-1}G_{IP}y$ and hence is strictly positive. This also supplies the witness $M_L\equiv1$ for the existence question in [@prob-ch34-schur-hb-kernel-positivity-bridge]. The multiplicative subtrees in [@prob-ch34-arithmetic-pivot-area-law] specify a separate family of matrices. Relating them to $G$ and bounding the norm across subtrees are additional steps.

**How many nodes can one quaternionic cell represent?** Take real nodes $s_j>1/2$. Conjugate zeros occur in pairs, so $G$ is real. Normalizing it to unit diagonal by $H_{jk}=G_{jk}/\sqrt{G_{jj}G_{kk}}$ preserves its rank $N$. **Approximating all spectral readings on one integer shell.** For finitely many distinct real nodes $s_j>1/2$, put $u_j=s_j-1/2$. Pair the zeros $\tfrac12\pm i\gamma$ for $\gamma>0$, with multiplicity $m_\gamma$. This pair contributes two real coordinates at node $j$,

$$
\sqrt{m_\gamma}\,\xi(s_j)\frac{(u_j,\gamma)}{u_j^2+\gamma^2}.
$$

Their dot product across two nodes is exactly the pair's contribution to $G$. Since $\xi(1)>0$ and the real half-line $s>1/2$ is zero-free, the coordinates are nonnegative. Divide the full coordinate vector for each node by $\sqrt{G_{jj}}$ to obtain a nonnegative unit vector $x_j$ with $\langle x_j,x_k\rangle=H_{jk}$.

Given $\eta>0$, keep the first $D$ real coordinates so that every $x_j$ has squared tail norm at most $\delta<\eta/2$. For a positive integer $Q$, round the retained coordinates down to $a_{j\ell}=\lfloor Qx_{j\ell}\rfloor$. Give each $j$ a private set of $Q^2-\sum_\ell a_{j\ell}^2$ additional coordinates. Set those entries of $A_j$ to $1$ and those of every other vector to $0$. The resulting nonnegative integer vectors lie on one shell of squared radius $Q^2$. The diagonal readings equal $1$ exactly. For $j\ne k$, the discarded inner product is at most $\delta$, each scaled rounding error has norm at most $\sqrt D/Q$, and the private coordinates contribute zero to the cross term. Consequently

$$
0\le H_{jk}-\frac{A_j\cdot A_k}{Q^2}\le\delta+\frac{2\sqrt D}{Q}.
$$

Choosing $D$ and then $Q$ makes the bound smaller than $\eta$. Positive definiteness gives $H_{jk}<1$, so the $A_j$ are distinct. The Tier 1 half-angle readings have half this error. The shell excludes nonconstant three-term arithmetic progressions exactly at every finite scale. Its dimension and radius may grow with the number of nodes and the requested precision.

**What can one central reference point reveal about the zeros? (Manual derivation.)** Fix the reference at $s=1/2$ and move only the other real node. Write $s=1/2+x$ and $t=1/2+y$, initially with $x,y>0$. Cancel the common factor $\xi$ at each node in the paired coordinates above. At offset $y$ the squared norm is $L(y)=\sum_{\gamma>0}m_\gamma/(\gamma^2+y^2)$. A partial fraction identity gives the dot product term by term. Writing $h(s,t)$ for the normalized inner product gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{xy+\gamma^2}{(x^2+\gamma^2)(y^2+\gamma^2)}
&=\frac{1}{x+y}\left(\frac{x}{x^2+\gamma^2}+\frac{y}{y^2+\gamma^2}\right),\\
h(s,t)&=\frac{xL(x)+yL(y)}{(x+y)\sqrt{L(x)L(y)}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The zero-counting estimate for positive ordinates gives $0<L(0)=\sum_{\gamma>0}m_\gamma/\gamma^2<\infty$. By [@prop-zeta-real-axis-sign] and [@prop-xi-zeros-strip-zeros], $\xi(1/2)\ne0$, so the central vector also admits normalization. Each term is bounded by $m_\gamma/\gamma^2$, so taking $x\downarrow0$ gives $h(1/2,1/2+y)^2=L(y)/L(0)$. Under RH, pairing the factors in the Hadamard product of $\xi$ gives factors $1+y^2/\gamma^2$. Logarithmic differentiation then yields $2yL(y)=\xi'(1/2+y)/\xi(1/2+y)$. [Paired product and zero expansion in §31.3](ch34-s31-arc-calculus-riemann-and-hermite-biehler.md#sec-ch34-31-3)

Regard the squared reading as a function of $u=y^2$. Its meromorphic continuation is $\sum_{\gamma>0}m_\gamma/[L(0)(u+\gamma^2)]$. The series converges uniformly on compact sets away from its poles since its tail terms are bounded in modulus by $2m_\gamma/[L(0)\gamma^2]$. By the identity theorem, the **exact curve** on any open interval of the positive real axis determines this continuation uniquely. Each pole $u=-\gamma^2$ gives a positive ordinate, and ratios of the residues $m_\gamma/L(0)$ give the relative multiplicities. Multiplying every multiplicity by the same positive integer preserves the normalized curve. For the Riemann zeros, the Riemann–von Mangoldt formula gives $N(T)\sim T\log T/(2\pi)$ for the number of positive ordinates counted with multiplicity. **How do finitely many readings bracket the first positive zero ordinate?** Put $F(u)=L(\sqrt u)/S$ with $S=L(0)$. Denote the smallest positive ordinate by $\gamma_1$ and write $a=\gamma_1^2$. The weights $p_\gamma=m_\gamma/(S\gamma^2)$ satisfy $\sum p_\gamma=1$, and $F(u)=\sum p_\gamma\gamma^2/(\gamma^2+u)$. Take a spacing $\delta>0$. With just one reading $F(\delta)$, the identity $F(0)=1$ gives $(1-F(\delta))/\delta=\sum p_\gamma/(\gamma^2+\delta)$. Every denominator is at least $a+\delta$, and the smallest ordinate itself carries weight at least $1/(Sa)$. These observations give upper and lower bounds, respectively, on the first difference with its sign reversed.

For an integer $k\ge1$, put $\Delta_\delta f(u)=f(u+\delta)-f(u)$ and $P_k(x)=\prod_{j=1}^k(x+j\delta)$. Each successive difference introduces another denominator. The same two bounds give

$$
D_k=\frac{(-1)^k\Delta_\delta^k F(0)}{k!\delta^k}
  =\sum_{\gamma>0}\frac{p_\gamma}{P_k(\gamma^2)},
\qquad
\frac{1}{SaP_k(a)}\le D_k\le\frac{1}{P_k(a)}.
$$

Suppose the normalized reading $F(0)=1$ is exact and the absolute error in each remaining $F(j\delta)$ is at most $\varepsilon$. Compute $\widehat D_k$ from these readings. The absolute binomial coefficients on the terms carrying error sum to $2^k-1$, giving $|\widehat D_k-D_k|\le\eta_k=(2^k-1)\varepsilon/(k!\delta^k)$. For this normalized positive spectrum the noisy readings satisfy $1\le SaP_k(a)(\widehat D_k+\eta_k)$ and $P_k(a)(\widehat D_k-\eta_k)\le1$. The same bounds hold for nonnegative discrete weights summing to one when the origin reading is exact and the first-atom weight satisfies $Sa p_0\ge1$. Here $Sa p_0=m_{\gamma_1}\ge1$. See [`noisy_first_atom_enclosure`](lean:D5/S3/Analytic/Stieltjes/NoisyFirstAtom.noisy_first_atom_enclosure).

To compute the endpoints by hand, take a certified upper bound $S_+\ge S$. If the readings are compatible with the error bound and $\widehat D_k>\eta_k$, solve for the two positive roots

$$
S_+\ell P_k(\ell)(\widehat D_k+\eta_k)=1,\qquad P_k(r)(\widehat D_k-\eta_k)=1.
$$

Both $xP_k(x)$ and $P_k(x)$ are strictly increasing on the positive half-line. It follows that $\ell\le a\le r$, so $\sqrt{\ell}\le\gamma_1\le\sqrt r$. When $\widehat D_k\le\eta_k$, the difference can still supply a lower bound. This order of difference and its error bound alone then give no finite upper bound.

Two successive differences can tighten the upper endpoint. Put $D_0=1$ and $\eta_0=0$. Then $D_{k-1}-(a+k\delta)D_k=\sum p_\gamma(\gamma^2-a)/P_k(\gamma^2)\ge0$, so for $\widehat D_k>\eta_k$,

$$
a\le\frac{\widehat D_{k-1}+\eta_{k-1}}{\widehat D_k-\eta_k}-k\delta.
$$

Take $\delta=16$ and $k=10$. Values of $\xi$ and its derivative on the real axis directly give the ten readings $F(j\delta)$. At the centre, symmetry gives $S=\xi''(1/2)/(2\xi(1/2))$. Use $S_+=0.023105$ for the lower endpoint and the successive-difference ratio for the upper endpoint. Rational substitution into the two inequalities gives $14.1338<\gamma_1<14.1432$. **Which shell angles occur exactly between two real spectral nodes?** Fix any real node $s>1/2$ and let a second real node $t$ move to the right from $s$. Write $x(t)$ for its normalized spectral vector and $h(s,t)$ for its inner product with $x(s)$. The kernel formula gives continuity and $h(s,s)=1$. For $t>s$, the coordinates from each conjugate pair are positive and the two-node Gram matrix is positive definite, so $0<h(s,t)<1$. We can also determine the limit of this inner product as $t\to\infty$.

Put $v=t-1/2$. In the coordinate formula above, cancel the common factor $\xi(t)$ and multiply every coordinate by $v$. Before normalization, the sum of their squares is

$$
S(v)=\sum_{\gamma>0}m_\gamma\,\frac{v^2}{v^2+\gamma^2}.
$$

Each summand increases to $m_\gamma$. There are infinitely many positive ordinates, so $S(v)\to\infty$. After normalization, the two coordinates of any fixed conjugate pair have squared norm at most $m_\gamma/S(v)\to0$. Hence the inner product of any finite truncation of $x(s)$ with $x(t)$ tends to zero. The remaining contribution is bounded in absolute value by the norm of the tail of $x(s)$, since $\|x(t)\|=1$. That tail can be made arbitrarily small, proving $h(s,t)\to0$.

By the intermediate value theorem, for fixed $s$ the range as $t>s$ varies is exactly $(0,1)$. Every acute angle therefore occurs between a pair of real spectral nodes. With $s=1$, the shortest arc above has target inner product $16/17$ and the companion book's example has target $11/17$, giving half-angle readings $33/34$ and $14/17$, respectively. Numerical solutions using the kernel formula of §31.3 give one node at approximately $t=10.964391$ for the first target and $t=62.587918$ for the second. Matching all nine points in the companion book imposes a further rank constraint. The three cyclic permutations of $(0,1,4)$ form a matrix whose determinant has absolute value $65$. The normalized Gram matrix of the nine three-dimensional vectors thus has rank three. Nine distinct real spectral nodes give rank nine, ruling out an exact simultaneous match for all nine points.

**Reading the central curve with unit-quaternion rotors (manual derivation).** Write $S=L(0)$ and $F(y^2)=L(y)/S$. Under RH the nontrivial zeros form conjugate pairs $1/2\pm i\gamma$. Assign probability $p_\gamma=m_\gamma/(S\gamma^2)$ to each distinct positive ordinate, where $m_\gamma$ is its multiplicity. Draw $\gamma$ with these probabilities, then draw an angular frequency $W\ge 0$ with conditional density $\gamma e^{-\gamma w}$ for $w>0$. Fix a unit imaginary quaternion $i$ and take the rotor $q_W(y)=\cos(Wy)+i\sin(Wy)$ in $S^3$. The model reading in [@thm-ch39-axiom-omega-rank-five-unit-quaternion-embedding] gives $2\kappa(1,q_W(y))-1=\cos(Wy)$. Integration gives, for every real $y$,

$$
\mathbb E[2\kappa(1,q_W(y))-1]
=\sum_{\gamma>0}p_\gamma\int_0^\infty\gamma e^{-\gamma w}\cos(wy)\,dw
=\sum_{\gamma>0}p_\gamma\frac{\gamma^2}{\gamma^2+y^2}=F(y^2).
$$

The exact curve determines the probability law on nonnegative frequencies uniquely. Assign $W$ an independent fair sign. The resulting symmetric distribution has the cosine average above as its characteristic function. A characteristic function determines its distribution, and taking the absolute value recovers $W$. Its density on $w>0$ is $\sum_{\gamma>0}p_\gamma\gamma e^{-\gamma w}$. The arithmetic spectrum specifies the mixing weights and rates. Each individual reading uses a single fixed-axis circle in $S^3$. Interpreting the sampling law and the scale of $y$ as a physical field evolution requires independent physical input.

Two offsets suffice to test the single-frequency double-angle law. Write $A(y)=F(y^2)$. The identity $\cos(2x)=2\cos^2 x-1$ gives $A(2y)-2A(y)^2+1=2\operatorname{Var}(\cos(Wy))$. A single frequency gives zero on the right. The mixture density above is positive for $w>0$, so the variance is strictly positive for $y\ne0$. At $y=4$, interval evaluation on the real axis gives $F(16)=0.975746642870670$ and $F(64)=0.916843302283509$, each within $10^{-15}$. Substitution yields $0.01268<F(64)-2F(16)^2+1<0.01269$. [Interval certificate for the readings](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/786#issuecomment-7068457253)

For a finite set of frequencies $w_j\ge0$ with probability weights $\alpha_j$, the uniform error on the whole half-line is at least $1$. Indeed, the phase vectors $(e^{i2^n w_j})_j$ have a convergent subsequence in the compact torus. The differences $y_\ell$ between successive times along that subsequence tend to infinity, and all $e^{iy_\ell w_j}$ tend to $1$. Thus $\sum_j\alpha_j\cos(w_jy_\ell)\to1$, whereas dominated convergence gives $F(y_\ell^2)\to0$.

There is also a finite-dimensional obstruction for the full two-node reading $h(s,t)$. Since $h(s,t)\to0$ for each fixed $s>1/2$, choose $s_j>s_{j-1}$ recursively so that $0<h(s_i,s_j)<1/j$ for every $i<j$. Map the nodes in any way to a fixed finite-dimensional real unit sphere. Their images have a convergent subsequence. Inner products of consecutive images along that subsequence approach $1$, while the corresponding values of $h$ approach $0$. Thus the uniform absolute error over all node pairs is at least $1$. By [@thm-ch39-quaternion-product-feature-rank], the product reading of any fixed number $N$ of rotors has such a unit feature representation, so the bound also holds for every nonlinear assignment of rotors to nodes. [Shared random rotors for two nodes and finite-sample error](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/786#issuecomment-7068672243)

**How many planar rotor modes do four observations require? (Hand calculation.)** Take the four offsets $x=0,4,8,12$, including the center. Allow a positive effective height for each mode, with the same heights and weights at every offset. Two modes reproduce all overlaps among these four observations exactly. Their heights are approximately $14.930912$ and $53.051497$, with respective probability weights $0.303376$ and $0.696624$. The effective heights depend on the chosen observations and can lie between arithmetic zero ordinates.

For any $m$ distinct offsets $0=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_{m-1}$, this construction takes exactly $\lceil m/2\rceil$ modes. Place weight $p_\gamma$ at $\lambda=\gamma^{-2}$ to form the measure $\mu$. At offset $x$ a single mode gives the planar vector $v_\lambda(x)=(1,\sqrt{\lambda}\,x)/(1+\lambda x^2)$. Its angle is $\arctan(\sqrt{\lambda}\,x)$ and its length is the cosine of that angle. With $f_x(\lambda)=(1+\lambda x^2)^{-1}$ we have $\int f_x\,d\mu=F(x^2)$. Taking the dot product for $x+y>0$ gives

$$
v_\lambda(x)\cdot v_\lambda(y)=\frac{x f_x(\lambda)+y f_y(\lambda)}{x+y}.
$$

At $x=y=0$ the dot product is $1$. Averaging over the shared mode gives the unnormalized kernel $K(x,y)$. Dividing by $\sqrt{K(x,x)K(y,y)}$ recovers the two-node overlap. It therefore suffices to integrate the $m$ functions exactly with one quadrature rule. Set $D(\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(1+\lambda x_i^2)$. Each $D f_{x_i}$ is a polynomial of degree at most $m-1$. Gaussian quadrature for the positive measure $d\mu/D$ with $n=\lceil m/2\rceil$ nodes has positive weights $c_j$, is exact through degree $2n-1$, and has nodes $\lambda_j$ inside the support interval. The required mode weights are $c_jD(\lambda_j)$ and sum to one because $f_0=1$. The four-point values above can also be obtained by interpolating $F(u)$ at $u=16,64,144$ with $(1+a u)/(1+b u+c u^2)$ and factoring the denominator. Keeping those two modes fixed, the squared center overlap at a fifth offset $x=16$ is about $0.779757394$, while the arithmetic curve gives $F(256)\approx0.780326506$. [Gaussian quadrature](https://dlmf.nist.gov/3.5#v)

The overlap matrix also gives the minimum. A linear relation among the $m$ node vectors would make the rational function $\sum_i a_i f_{x_i}$ from their first coordinates vanish at the infinitely many atoms of $\mu$. Multiplication by $D$ gives an identically zero polynomial. The distinct negative poles force $a_i=0$ for each $i>0$, and the remaining constant vanishes as well. The kernel matrix thus has rank $m$, which normalization preserves. Each planar mode contributes a matrix of rank at most two, so fewer than $\lceil m/2\rceil$ modes cannot suffice. This count applies to the specified observations and the amplitude-weighted planar readings above.

For a finite observation window $0\le y\le Y$, choose a cutoff $T>0$ and move the probability mass of $W>T$ to $T$. Take a grid on $[0,T]$ that includes both endpoints and has maximum spacing $\Delta$. Distribute the mass at each interior frequency between its two surrounding grid points using the weights of linear interpolation. The cosine average $C(y)$ of this finite probability distribution satisfies

$$
\sup_{0\le y\le Y}|F(y^2)-C(y)|\le 2e^{-aT}+\frac{Y^2\Delta^2}{8}
\qquad (0<a\le\gamma_1).
$$

Here $\gamma_1$ is the least positive ordinate. The first term follows from $\Pr(W>T)=\sum_\gamma p_\gamma e^{-\gamma T}\le e^{-aT}$ and the bound $2$ on the difference of two cosine values. The second follows from the linear interpolation remainder for $w\mapsto\cos(wy)$, whose second derivative has absolute value at most $Y^2$.

**Reading the whole shell from one set of zeros (derivation and interval computation).** Choose a finite spectral measure for which all nine shell points share four phase functions. Index the positive ordinates $\gamma_j$ of the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function in increasing order. At ordinate $\gamma$, a positive integer $n$ supplies the phase $e_n(\gamma)=\exp(i\gamma\log n)$. Use the labels $n=1,2,3,6$. For $j=4,5,8,12,15$, give the two ordinates $\pm\gamma_j$ a combined weight $w_j$, split equally between them. The weighted inner product of $e_m$ and $e_n$ is then $\sum_j w_j\cos(\gamma_j\log(n/m))$. Since $6=2\cdot3$, there are just four distinct absolute nonzero log differences, $\log 2,\log 3,\log(3/2),\log 6$. Set the four cosine sums to zero and the sum of the weights to one. Interval arithmetic verifies that this five-by-five system is invertible and that all five weights are strictly positive. In the order given above, they are approximately $(0.105512,0.122932,0.215694,0.451373,0.104488)$. With the exact solution as weights, the four phase functions are orthonormal. $$
x_u=\frac{e_6+u_1e_1+u_2e_2+u_3e_3}{\sqrt2},
\qquad \langle x_u,x_v\rangle=\frac{1+u\cdot v}{2}.
$$

Within the standard qubit model, we can construct a channel that reproduces the entire table of readings. Map $e_6,e_1,e_2,e_3$, in that order, to the Hilbert–Schmidt orthonormal basis $I/\sqrt2,\sigma_x/\sqrt2,\sigma_y/\sqrt2,\sigma_z/\sqrt2$. The image of $x_u$ is the rank-one projector $P_u=(I+u\cdot\sigma)/2$. Write $P_u=z_u z_u^\dagger$. Using its row and column indices as the two qubit indices gives the vector $z_u\otimes\overline{z_u}$. Discarding the second qubit leaves the state $P_u$. **Two nodes give a Tier 2 state.** Take any two-by-two principal block of $H$ and denote its off-diagonal entry by $h$. Positive definiteness gives $|h|<1$. Dividing the block by its trace $2$ gives a full-rank qubit state $B$ with Bloch vector $(h,0,0)$. Its hyperbolic distance from the center $I_2/2$ satisfies $\cosh d(B,I_2/2)=1/\sqrt{1-h^2}$. Regard the two normalized spectral vectors as unit vectors in the underlying real inner-product space, with the real part of the complex inner product. Their inner product is $h$, so the squared norm of their chord midpoint is $(1+h)/2$. Thus $h$ gives the Tier 2 distance in state space and obeys the same half-angle identity used on the Tier 1 Behrend shell. Interpreting that distance as a physical velocity or clock reading requires a corresponding measurement procedure. [@rem-ch39-axiom-omega-tier2-velocity-and-de-sitter]

The same zero ordinate $\gamma$ can supply the input to two phase encodings. Write $z=2^{i\gamma}$ and $w=3^{i\gamma}$. Recording the constant and the two individual phases gives the unit vector $(1,z,w)/\sqrt3$. Recording their product $zw=6^{i\gamma}$ as well gives $(1,z,w,zw)/2$. Sampling ordinates from a common probability distribution produces a mixed state for each encoding. The mean squared overlap of two independent draws equals the purity $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)$ of the mixture. The following bound compares the purity losses of the two encodings.

::: {.theorem #thm-ch39-phase-ensemble-purity title="Sharp coefficient for the product phase encoding" lean="D5/S3/Quantum/PureState/PhaseProductFidelity.mixed_coefficient_iff"}
Fix an integer $n\ge1$ and a real number $C$. For a vector $u=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$ of complex coordinates, define

$$
A(u)=\frac{|1+\sum_k u_k|^2}{(n+1)^2},\qquad
B(u)=\prod_k\frac{1+\Re u_k}{2}.
$$

Take any finite list of complex rows $v_i$ with real weights $p_i$. Assume every product $p_i p_j$ is nonnegative and every coordinate of a row of nonzero weight lies in the closed unit disk. Put $u_{ij,k}=\overline{v_{ik}}v_{jk}$ and $L(F)=\sum_{i,j}p_i p_j(1-F(u_{ij}))$. The inequality $L(B)\le C L(A)$ holds for every such finite ensemble if and only if $C\ge(n+1)^2/4$.
:::

::: {.proof}
Omit rows of weight zero. For every remaining pair of inputs, each factor in the product lies in $[0,1]$, so one minus the product is at most the sum of the individual losses. Expanding $A$ and using $|\sum_k u_k|\le n$ gives the bound for each pair of inputs. Summing with the nonnegative pair weights preserves it. For sharpness take two equally weighted rows, one consisting of ones and the other consisting of a single unit phase repeated in every coordinate. As that phase approaches one, the loss ratio tends to $(n+1)^2/4$. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

The two-phase encodings above have $n=2$, giving the sharp coefficient $9/4$. For probability weights and unit phases, $L(A)$ and $L(B)$ are each one minus the purity of the corresponding mixture. Sharpness holds over all finite unit-phase ensembles. Any particular choice of zeros and weights inherits the inequality.

If each complex coordinate may range over the disk of radius $R$, the same formulas define $L(A)$ and $L(B)$ as algebraic deficits. The zero-ordinate encoding has unit-modulus phases, so its probability ensembles satisfy the purity bound for $R=1$.

Consider one row $(t,t)$ of weight one, with $t$ real and nonnegative. Both relative coordinates equal $x=t^2$. The two losses are $1-(1+2x)^2/9$ and $1-(1+x)^2/4$. For $x\ne1$ the ratio of the second to the first is $9(3+x)/[16(2+x)]$. Both losses change sign as $t$ passes through one. The choice of disk radius therefore affects the allowable lower coefficient.

::: {.theorem #thm-ch39-phase-pair-loss-floor title="Sharp lower coefficient for two coordinates on a disk" lean="D5/S3/Quantum/PureState/PhasePairLossFloor.mixed_lower_coefficient_iff"}
Use the two-coordinate functions $A,B,L$ above, and fix real numbers $R\ge0$ and $C$. Consider every finite ensemble with real weights satisfying $p_i p_j\ge0$ and with both coordinates of every nonzero-weight row bounded in modulus by $R$. The inequality $C L(A)\le L(B)$ holds for all these ensembles if and only if the following condition holds.

$$
\begin{cases}
C\le 9(3+R^2)/[16(2+R^2)],&0\le R\le1,\\
C=3/4,&R>1.
\end{cases}
$$

:::

::: {.proof}
For a pair of nonzero-weight rows, let $u_1,u_2$ denote the relative coordinates and put $x=(\Re u_1+\Re u_2)/2$. When $y=R^2\le1$ we have $x\le y$. At the endpoint coefficient $c=9(3+y)/[16(2+y)]$, direct expansion gives

$$
(1-B)-c(1-A)=
\frac{(1-x)(y-x)}{4(2+y)}
+\frac{(\Re u_1-\Re u_2)^2}{16}
+\frac{c(\Im u_1+\Im u_2)^2}{9}.
$$

All three terms are nonnegative. The relative coordinates lie in the unit disk, so $A\le1$ and every smaller coefficient also works. For relative coordinates of arbitrary modulus, the same expansion at $c=3/4$ gives $(1-x)^2/12$ as the first term, with the two remaining squares still nonnegative. Summing with weights $p_i p_j$ gives the ensemble inequality. In the single-row example above, $t=R$ attains the endpoint ratio when $R<1$. At $R=1$, taking $t$ to one from below forces $C\le3/4$. For $R>1$ we can also approach from above, where the change of sign forces $C\ge3/4$. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

**Do the three low-energy readings determine the matching scale?
