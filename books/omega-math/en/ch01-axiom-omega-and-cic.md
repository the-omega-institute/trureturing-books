# Chapter 1 Bare CIC and the Mother Axiom AXIOM-Ω

> *"Do not alter the verifier's kernel, and never inject unchecked dogma with the `axiom` keyword; write the Mother Axiom as a computable typeclass and build a pure integer carrier that inhabits it without Choice or quotients—consistency and executability arrive together."*

When rebuilding mathematics from zero axioms, the first question is not how to write numbers, but **what logical floor we are standing on**. If our proof engine silently opens with ZFC set theory and the real continuum, then claiming to reject actual infinity or transcendentals in prose changes nothing beneath the hood: Choice and quotient axioms are already active in the kernel's dependency graph. This chapter lays the floor bare in four steps. First, we inspect the bare Calculus of Inductive Constructions (CIC) kernel of Lean 4 and see why it natively lacks actual infinity and Choice. Second, we replace static infinite sets with an explicit observer rational precision contract $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{self}} = 1/M$. Third, we state the three Mother Laws of `AXIOM-Ω`. Fourth, a schematic interface shows how to pass operations and their individual certificates to the kernel.
“Zero-axiom kernel verification” refers to the empty axiom record in the strict Omega backend. The official version reached by a source link has its own frozen axiom record. For example, the ray left-cancellation result in Chapter 3 has no axioms in the strict backend and depends on `propext` in the official backend.

## 1.1 The Bare CIC Kernel and the Three Hidden Classical Axioms {#sec-ch01-bare-cic}

Split a modern proof assistant such as Lean 4 into its **underlying verification kernel** and its **classical analysis library**, and you meet two completely different philosophies of mathematics.

Before any classical real-analysis module is imported, Lean 4's bare kernel is the **Calculus of Inductive Constructions (CIC)** with universe levels. Its entire ontology consists of three primitives: dependent function types ($\Pi$-types, unifying functions and universal quantification), a stratified hierarchy of universes preventing self-membership paradoxes, and finite inductive definitions (`inductive`). Checking a proof or evaluating an expression uses only four deterministic reduction rules: function application ($\beta$), definition unfolding ($\delta$), inductive eliminator reduction ($\iota$), and local binding expansion ($\zeta$).

Inside this bare kernel there is no Cantorian "completed infinite bag" pre-populated with $\aleph_0$ elements. The natural numbers `Nat` are generated by two finite constructors, `zero` and `succ`. Every concrete natural number is a finite syntax tree, and every structurally recursive function on `Nat` terminates in finitely many inductive steps. The kernel knows nothing of $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\infty$, $\pi$, or $e$; it does not even hard-wire classical excluded middle or Choice into its reduction engine.

Where, then, do the three standard axioms of classical formalization enter? They arrive through three seemingly convenient classical shortcuts:

1. **Non-constructive Choice (`Classical.choice`)**: extracts a data element from a mere proof that a type is nonempty without giving an algorithm to construct it. Once a definition touches `Classical.choice`, no executable machine code can be extracted from it; `noncomputable` spreads through downstream definitions and kernel reflection stalls.
2. **Quotient soundness (`Quot.sound`)**: collapses an equivalence relation into a new type so that equivalent representations become propositionally equal even when computation has no canonical normal form. Defining real numbers as equivalence classes of rational Cauchy sequences pulls both `Quot.sound` and `Classical.choice` straight into the foundation.
3. **Propositional extensionality (`propext`)**: identifies logically equivalent propositions. Though gentler computationally than `Classical.choice`, it remains an external axiom outside bare CIC reduction.

One subtle trap deserves early warning: even without importing `Mathlib`, standard-library types that bundle proof fields inside data—such as a proof-carrying rational type `Rat`—can quietly pull in `propext`, `Quot.sound`, or `Classical.choice` during arithmetic operations. In the zero-axiom libraries `OmegaInit` and `OmegaMath`, we therefore start inside a bare `prelude` environment, building inductive naturals, signed integers, and exact fraction types carrying an explicit zero-denominator `undefined` branch (`Omega.Ratio` and `Omega.IntRatio`) or canonically normalized pure-data tuples. Everything rests on **constructive decidable equality (`DecidableEq`)**: for any two values $a$ and $b$, a terminating decision procedure yields either a proof of $a = b$ or a refutation of $a = b$.

::: {.definition #def-ch01-zero-axiom-core title="Zero-Axiom Bare CIC Core and Decidable Equality"}
A formal module belongs to the **zero-axiom bare CIC core** if the transitive axiom closure of every definition and theorem in it is empty—depending neither on `propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound`, nor on any custom `axiom`—and every type check and equality verification proceeds solely by dependent typing and $\beta\delta\iota\zeta$-reduction. A type $T$ carries **constructive decidable equality** (`DecidableEq`) if there is a computable procedure that, given any $a, b : T$, returns either a proof term witnessing $a = b$ or a refutation term witnessing $(a = b) \to \mathrm{False}$.
:::

When each ray has a unique canonical representative, comparing canonical integer tuples decides whether they represent the same ray. The underlying library can also keep unreduced representatives and compare them by ray equivalence. See [Chapter 2](ch02-homogeneous-projective-line.md) and [Chapter 3](ch03-canonical-ternion-manifold.md).

## 1.2 The Observer Rational Precision Contract and On-Demand Horizons {#sec-ch01-precision-contract}

Banishing Cantor's static actual infinity immediately raises a sharp question: **if we refuse to pack infinitely many numbers into a completed set, how do we handle arbitrarily fine measurement precision, arbitrarily large primes, or arbitrarily long evolutions?**

The answer is disarmingly concrete and matches both laboratory instruments and digital hardware: **replace static actual infinity with an explicit rational precision contract $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{self}} = 1/M \in \mathbb{Q}_+$, and replace completed infinite containers with an on-demand finite horizon.**

No physical observer, detector, or finite-memory computer ever resolves infinitely many decimal digits in a single reading. Every active comparison carries a positive integer resolution parameter $M \in \mathbb{N}_+$, defining a rational precision floor $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{self}} = 1/M > 0$. Within that active horizon, every coordinate, rational reading, and candidate state lives inside a finite reduced rational grid of height at most $M$. Because the horizon is finite, searches terminate, extrema are attained, and equality decisions always halt.

Nothing about this freezes the universe onto one rigid lattice. Whenever composing two states produces larger numerators or denominators—say multiplying two rational circle points with denominators $M_1$ and $M_2$ into a denominator bounded by $M_1 M_2$—or whenever an observer demands sharper resolution, the horizon expands lazily on demand from $M$ to a larger finite integer $M'$. At every actual step, $M'$ is a concrete finite natural number: we retain the inductive rule for constructing larger finite horizons (potential infinity), while never positing a completed infinite container holding all horizons at once.

::: {.definition #def-ch01-observer-precision title="Observer Rational Precision Contract and Finite Height Horizon (MODEL)"}
Let $M \in \mathbb{N}_+$ be a positive integer. The positive rational number

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{self}} \;=\; \frac{1}{M} \;\in\; \mathbb{Q}_+
$$

is the observer's **rational precision contract** for the current comparison task. For a coprime homogeneous projective pair $[P : Q]$ with $\gcd(\lvert P\rvert, \lvert Q\rvert) = 1$, define its height by $H([P : Q]) := \max(\lvert P\rvert, \lvert Q\rvert)$, and define the **finite projective horizon** at resolution $M$ by

$$
\mathbb{QP}^1_{\le M} \;:=\; \bigl\{\,[P : Q] \in \mathbb{QP}^1 \;:\; \max(\lvert P\rvert, \lvert Q\rvert) \le M\,\bigr\}.
$$

Whenever algebraic composition exceeds height $M$, the contract raises the bound lazily to a concrete finite integer $M'$, without invoking uncountable sets or completed actual infinity.
:::

::: {.example #ex-ch01-finite-horizon-budget title="An Arithmetic Walk Through a Finite Precision Budget (NUM)"}
Take resolution $M = 12$, corresponding to the precision contract $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{self}} = \frac{1}{12}$. Any two distinct reduced fractions $a/b \neq c/d$ with denominators $b, d \le 12$ are separated by at least $\lvert ad - bc\rvert / (bd) \ge \frac{1}{12 \times 11} = \frac{1}{132}$: the entire horizon has a strictly positive rational gap, with zero ambiguity about arbitrarily close distinct states. Now compose two rational circle points lying inside the tiny horizon $M = 3$: $[1 : 2]$ (squared norm $1^2 + 2^2 = 5$) and $[1 : 3]$ (squared norm $1^2 + 3^2 = 10$). Their unreduced product momentarily reaches height $5$, yet cancelling the common factor $5$ drops it right back to the height-$1$ anchor $[1 : 1]$. Composing $[1 : 2]$ with $[2 : 5]$ (squared norms $5$ and $29$) yields $[9 : 8]$, raising the active horizon on demand to $M' = 9$. Every single transition moves purely among finite integers.
:::

From mathematical logic, finite horizons offer a second deep safeguard. Over the unbounded integer ring $(\mathbb{Z}, +, \times)$, arbitrary existentially quantified Diophantine equations are undecidable by the negative solution to Hilbert's tenth problem (Yu. Matiyasevich, 1970, `ARGUED`). Inside any bounded finite horizon $M$, every boundedly quantified arithmetic claim is constructively decidable in finitely many steps. The observer precision contract is simultaneously a statement of physical honesty and a natural boundary of algorithmic decidability.

## 1.3 The Three Mother Laws of AXIOM-Ω {#sec-ch01-mother-laws}

With bare CIC underfoot and finite precision horizons in place, we can pose the central geometric question of the entire book: **if we strip away the two artificial scaffolds of textbook physics—a privileged external origin $\mathbf{1}$ and flat Cartesian linear addition $+$—what primitive relational structure remains?**

Exactly two primitives remain on a nonempty state carrier $\mathcal{M}$. The first is an **origin-free ternary comparison transport** $[-, -, -] : \mathcal{M} \times \mathcal{M} \times \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{M}$: using $y$ as a temporary local bridge, it reads the relation of $x$ against $y$ and transfers that exact relation onto $z$, producing $[x, y, z]$. The second is a **rational symmetric fidelity readout** $\kappa : \mathcal{M} \times \mathcal{M} \to \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$: it reports the mutual alignment of any pair $x, y$ without appealing to external coordinates or real radian rulers.

These two primitives obey three inseparable laws, jointly called the **Mother Axiom `AXIOM-Ω`**:

::: {.definition #def-ch01-axiom-omega title="The Three Mother Laws of AXIOM-Ω (MODEL)"}
Let $\mathcal{M}$ be a nonempty type equipped with a ternary comparison transport $[-, -, -] : \mathcal{M} \times \mathcal{M} \times \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{M}$ and a symmetric fidelity readout $\kappa : \mathcal{M} \times \mathcal{M} \to \mathbb{Q}$. The triple $(\mathcal{M}, [-, -, -], \kappa)$ satisfies the Mother Axiom **`AXIOM-Ω`** when it obeys three laws:

1. **Law I (Origin-Free Cancellation and Ternary Associativity)**: for all $x, y, z, u, v \in \mathcal{M}$,

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   [x, x, z] &= z, \qquad [x, z, z] = x, \\
   \bigl[[x, y, z],\, u,\, v\bigr] &= \bigl[x,\, y,\, [z, u, v]\bigr].
   \end{aligned}
   $$

2. **Law II (Bilateral Invariance, Extensional Separation, Zero-Reaching, and Rational Gram Positivity)**: for all $x, y, u, v \in \mathcal{M}$, $\kappa$ is symmetric ($\kappa(x, y) = \kappa(y, x)$), separates points ($\kappa(x, y) = 1 \iff x = y$), is nonnegative ($\kappa(x, y) \ge 0$), reaches complete orthogonal mismatch ($\kappa = 0$, or values arbitrarily close to $0$), and is invariant under ternary transport on either side:

   $$
   \kappa\bigl([x, u, v],\, [y, u, v]\bigr) \;=\; \kappa\bigl([u, v, x],\, [u, v, y]\bigr) \;=\; \kappa(x, y);
   $$

   moreover, for every finite sample $x_1, \dots, x_n \in \mathcal{M}$ and rational coefficients $c_1, \dots, c_n \in \mathbb{Q}$, the finite Gram matrix $\mathbf{G}_n = \bigl[\kappa(x_i, x_j)\bigr]_{i,j=1}^n$ is positive semidefinite ($\mathbf{G}_n \succeq 0$):

   $$
   \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} c_i c_j\,\kappa(x_i, x_j) \;\ge\; 0.
   $$

3. **Law III (Minimal Noncommutativity Self-Lock)**: ternary transport is order-sensitive in its outer slots,

   $$
   \exists\, x, y, z \in \mathcal{M},\qquad [x, y, z] \;\neq\; [z, y, x],
   $$

   and the carrier locks onto the minimal nontrivial degrees of freedom and minimal feature rank compatible with Laws I and II.
:::

Pause to notice how sparse these laws are. Not a single $\pi$, $e$, $\varphi$, real number $\mathbb{R}$, linear sum $+$, hand-typed dimension "$3$", or privileged identity $\mathbf{1}$ enters the statement. Small Gram matrices from Law II constrain the fidelity, and the identities in Law I recover a group at each basepoint (`ARGUED`).

- **The upper bound $\kappa \le 1$ comes for free**: apply Law II's rational Gram positivity with $n = 2$, sample $(x, y)$, and weights $(c_1, c_2) = (1, -1)$. Using $\kappa(x, x) = \kappa(y, y) = 1$ and symmetry gives

  $$
  1^2\cdot 1 + (-1)^2\cdot 1 + 2(1)(-1)\kappa(x, y) \;=\; 2 - 2\kappa(x, y) \;\ge\; 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \kappa(x, y) \;\le\; 1,
  $$

  so nonnegativity gives $\kappa(x, y) \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$.

  Now add an arbitrary probe point $z$ and write $d = \kappa(x,z)-\kappa(y,z)$. For the sample $(x,y,z)$ and rational weights $(1,-1,-d)$, normalization and symmetry give the Gram inequality $2-2\kappa(x,y)-d^2 \ge 0$. If $\kappa(x,y)=1$, then $x$ and $y$ have the same reading against every $z$. Conversely, if their rows agree, taking $z=y$ gives $\kappa(x,y)=\kappa(y,y)=1$. This argument uses only normalization, symmetry, and rational Gram positivity. Inferring equality of the underlying points from unit fidelity requires precisely that the row map $x\mapsto\kappa(x,\cdot)$ be injective. This is the role of extensional separation in Law II.

  How far apart can two probe readings be? Fix the mutual reading of $x$ and $y$ at $3/5$, and let a third point $z$ read $4/5$ against $x$ and zero against $y$. For rational weights $u,v,w$ on the three points in that order, the Gram form is $(v+3u/5)^2+(w+4u/5)^2$. These readings are therefore attainable on three points. The identity $3^2+4^2=5^2$ suggests testing the mutual reading and the difference between the probe readings in a single sum of squares.

::: {.theorem #thm-ch01-three-reading-contrast title="Exact range of a three-point Gram reading contrast" lean="D5/S3/Quantum/AURICOmega/ThreeReadingContrast.exists_nonnegative_readings_iff"}
Fix $r,d\in\mathbb Q$. There exist nonnegative rationals $a,b$ such that $a-b=d$ and

$$
Q(u,v,w)=u^2+v^2+w^2+2(ruv+auw+bvw)\ge 0
$$

for all $u,v,w\in\mathbb Q$ if and only if $r^2+d^2\le 1$.
:::

::: {.proof}
The test $Q(1,-1,0)\ge0$ gives $r\le1$. If $d=a-b\ge0$, then
$1-r^2-d^2=Q(1,-r,-d)+2(1-r)bd\ge0$. If $d<0$, interchange the first two points. Conversely, suppose $r^2+d^2\le1$. For $d\ge0$, choose $a=d,b=0$. The form becomes

$$
Q(u,v,w)=(v+ru)^2+(w+du)^2+(1-r^2-d^2)u^2.
$$

For $d<0$, interchange the first two points and take $a=0,b=-d$. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

This proof uses Mathlib’s rational numbers and their order. Its Lean dependencies include `propext`, `Classical.choice`, and `Quot.sound`. Law II also requires $r\ge0$. The criterion describes the readings on three sampled points. Extending those data to a comparison space requires the global transport laws as well.

- **Any point serves temporarily as an origin, recovering a group in three lines**: pick any temporary reference point $o \in \mathcal{M}$, and define a binary product $x \star_o z := [x, o, z]$ and local inverse $x^{-1_o} := [o, x, o]$. Associativity follows straight from Law I: $(x \star_o y) \star_o z = [[x, o, y], o, z] = [x, o, [y, o, z]] = x \star_o (y \star_o z)$. Left and right identity are $[o, o, z] = z$ and $[x, o, o] = x$, and the left and right inverses take two rewrites each:

  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  x \star_o x^{-1_o} &= \bigl[x, o, [o, x, o]\bigr] = \bigl[[x, o, o], x, o\bigr] = [x, x, o] = o, \\
  x^{-1_o} \star_o x &= \bigl[[o, x, o], o, x\bigr] = \bigl[o, x, [o, o, x]\bigr] = [o, x, x] = o.
  \end{aligned}
  $$

  The original ternary transport recovers identically as $x \star_o y^{-1_o} \star_o z = [x, y, z]$, and shifting basepoint $o \mapsto o'$ via $x \mapsto [x, o, o']$ is a bijection (with inverse $x \mapsto [x, o', o]$) preserving both transport and fidelity. Every point stands on equal footing; an observer plants a local reference wherever it stands, computes, and lets the bridge cancel away without trace.

**Identifying points with the same row.** Keep normalization and the other reading conditions of Law II while dropping extensional separation. Can we perform ternary transport on the resulting classes? Write $x\sim y$ when their rows agree. In the group $G$ based at $o$, let $N=\{n\in G:n\sim o\}$. Left translations are invertible and preserve the reading, so $x\sim y$ exactly when $x^{-1}y\in N$. For $n,m\in N$, left translation gives $nm\sim n\sim o$ and $n^{-1}\sim o$, so $N$ is a subgroup. When right translations also preserve the reading, multiplying first on the left by $g$ and then on the right by $g^{-1}$ gives $gng^{-1}\sim o$, so $N$ is normal.

The classes are then the elements of the quotient group $G/N$, where $[xN,yN,zN]=(xy^{-1}z)N$. With left invariance alone, normality is still both necessary and sufficient for transport to descend. Indeed, if transport is independent of representatives, fixing its middle slot at $o$ gives a well-defined multiplication, so we can multiply both sides of $n\sim o$ on the left by $g$ and on the right by $g^{-1}$. Row equality makes the quotient reading independent of the first representative, and symmetry does the same for the second. Computing on representatives carries each available translation invariance to the quotient. The Gram form of a finite sample of classes is the Gram form of its representatives, and the set of reading values stays the same, preserving zero-reaching. Unit fidelity on the quotient holds exactly between equal classes.

To check whether swapping the outer slots can still change the result, compare $xy$ with $yx$ in the quotient. Fixing the middle slot at the identity gives group multiplication, and in an abelian group every ternary transport is symmetric in its outer slots. Noncommutativity therefore survives exactly when some commutator $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$ lies outside $N$, equivalently when $[G,G]\nsubseteq N$.

Both outcomes occur on the same finite group. Take $G=S_3\times\{+1,-1\}$ with transport $[x,y,z]=xy^{-1}z$. One reading is $1$ when the permutation components agree and $0$ otherwise. Another uses the same rule with permutation parity. Both readings group points by the fibers of a homomorphism, and both left and right translations preserve the grouping relation. If $r_i$ records the permutation or parity of the sample point $x_i$, the Gram form is the sum of squares $\sum_r(\sum_{i:\,r_i=r}c_i)^2$. Both readings identify $(e,+1)$ with $(e,-1)$ and give zero between the identity and a transposition.

The permutation reading gives quotient $S_3$, where the order of $(12)$ and $(23)$ still changes the product. The parity reading gives the abelian group of order two, where both orders give the even class. Thus passing to reading classes restores extensional separation, and which commutators survive determines whether the reading can still distinguish the order of transport. Minimality still requires a specified class of models.

## 1.4 Why a Computable Typeclass class OmegaSpace Rather Than an Unchecked axiom {#sec-ch01-typeclass-not-axiom}

When formalizing `AXIOM-Ω` in a proof assistant, the most tempting shortcut is also the deadliest: typing `axiom M : Type`, `axiom triad : M → M → M → M`, and `axiom kappa_refl : ∀ x, kappa x x = 1`. **This monograph bans unchecked `axiom` declarations from the very first line.**

Two decisive failures explain why.

First, **a raw `axiom` provides zero guarantee of consistency and explodes to `False` at the slightest boundary slip**. The Lean kernel accepts every user-declared `axiom` without question. If someone writes `axiom kappa_refl : ∀ x, kappa x x = 1` over raw quadruples while forgetting to exclude the zero quadruple $z_0 = (0, 0, 0, 0)$ where the ratio function returns `undefined` or $0$, substituting $x = z_0$ yields `0 = 1` and hence `False` in three lines—collapsing the entire logic. Every independent kernel auditor rejects non-standard custom axioms on sight.

Second, **a raw `axiom` freezes $\iota$-reduction and severs the Curry–Howard unity of proof and computation**. Because an `axiom` carries no computation rule, any term mentioning it sticks as an irreducible neutral symbol: kernel reflection cannot simplify it, and no native executable code can run it.

A structure can collect the operations and their individual certificates, so that each theorem depends explicitly on the certificates it takes as input. The schematic `class OmegaSpace` below illustrates Law I and some of the reading conditions in Law II.

```lean
class OmegaSpace (M : Type) where
  triad       : M → M → M → M
  kappa       : M → M → QRat
  idemp_left  : ∀ x z, triad x x z = z
  idemp_right : ∀ x z, triad x z z = x
  assoc_triad : ∀ x y z u v, triad (triad x y z) u v = triad x y (triad z u v)
  kappa_refl  : ∀ x, kappa x x = QRat.one
  kappa_symm  : ∀ x y, kappa x y = kappa y x
  kappa_inv_r : ∀ x y u v, kappa (triad x u v) (triad y u v) = kappa x y
  kappa_inv_l : ∀ x y u v, kappa (triad u v x) (triad u v y) = kappa x y
```

This interface guarantees exactly the fields it lists. Take the eight-element quaternion group $Q_8 = \{\pm 1,\pm\mathbf{i},\pm\mathbf{j},\pm\mathbf{k}\}$ with transfer $[x,y,z]=xy^{-1}z$ and constant reading $\kappa(x,y)=1$. The group identities give cancellation and associativity. The constant reading meets the remaining fields and even gives the nonnegative Gram form $(\sum_i c_i)^2 \ge 0$, while $\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}\neq\mathbf{j}\mathbf{i}$. Yet $\kappa(1,-1)=1$ and the reading cannot approach zero. A full model needs separate evidence for separation, nonnegativity, Gram positivity, zero-reaching, and noncommutativity, together with a specified class of structures for the minimality claim.

The zero-axiom library verifies integer quaternion laws using nonzero representatives and ray equivalence. Chapter 3 then derives strict cancellation and reading properties by hand for canonical representatives and gives the additional assumptions for the real-valued rank-five result. See [Chapter 3](ch03-canonical-ternion-manifold.md).

With the logical foundation and model laws in place, start with a commutative carrier in one dimension. The homogeneous integer projective line $\mathbb{QP}^1$ crosses the antipode while keeping every reading finite.
