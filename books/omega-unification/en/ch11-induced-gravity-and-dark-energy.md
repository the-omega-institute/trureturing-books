# Chapter 11 Induced Gravity, Four-Layer Heat-Kernel Cancellation, and Dark-Energy Sensitivity {#sec-ch11-induced-gravity-and-dark-energy}

Chapter 10 ended with a sharp cliffhanger: even if one grants the simplicial Regge action as a geometric scaffold, geometry alone never tells you the magnitude of Newton's constant $G_N$ or why the dimensionless cosmological constant sits on the razor's edge $\Lambda G \sim 10^{-122}$. Sakharov famously proposed an enticing way out: **perhaps neither $G_N$ nor $\Lambda$ is a bare geometric input at all, because one-loop quantum fluctuations of all matter fields in a curved background automatically induce both.**

The idea is beautiful—so let us test it ruthlessly by feeding the exact three-generation gauge-crossing heavy spectrum from Volume II into a one-loop proper-time heat kernel and letting arithmetic pronounce the verdict. This chapter tightens the calipers step by step: first measuring the vast gap left by the visible heavy spectrum alone alongside its geometric twin in calibrated Regge volume models; next computing the exact minimal count of hidden real singlets needed to close both scales and exposing their extreme ultraviolet fragility; then constructing the four-layer $(-1, 3, -3, 1)$ finite positive vacuum cancellation, watching it hit $10^{-122}$ to four significant digits before slamming into the tree-level Higgs potential of electroweak symmetry breaking; and finally presenting an honest balance sheet for Question 4.

## 11.1 Proper-Time Induced Gravity and the Visible Heavy-Spectrum Gap {#sec-ch11-visible-spectrum-gap}

To eliminate any ambiguity about conventions, we begin by fixing the exact one-loop proper-time prescription and the four-layer finite positive vacuum stencil.

::: {.definition #def-ou-ch11-four-layer-vacuum title="One-Loop Proper-Time Induced Gravity, Newton Calibration, and the Four-Layer Positive Vacuum Stencil"}
Let $E_j(z) := \int_1^\infty e^{-zt} t^{-j}\,dt$ ($j = 1, 2, 3$) denote the exponential integrals, $M_P = 1.22089\times 10^{19}\,\mathrm{GeV}$ the Planck mass, $x := Q^2/M_P^2$ the dimensionless proper-time UV cutoff, and $z_k := m_k^2/M_P^2$ the dimensionless squared masses. Under the minimally coupled one-loop prescription with zero bare Newton and bare vacuum counterterms, define the flat vacuum energy density $\rho$, normalized inverse Newton constant $K$, and dimensionless horizon curvature $C$ by

$$
\rho = -\frac{Q^4}{32\pi^2}\sum_k g_k E_3\!\left(\frac{z_k}{x}\right), \qquad K := \frac{G^{-1}}{M_P^2} = \frac{x}{2\pi}\sum_k \alpha_k E_2\!\left(\frac{z_k}{x}\right), \qquad C := \Lambda G = \frac{8\pi\rho/M_P^4}{K^2}.
$$

Here $g_k$ is the bosonic-minus-fermionic polarization count, and the minimal-coupling Newton weights $\alpha_k$ equal $1/6, 1/6, 1/3, -2/3, -1/2$ for a real scalar, Weyl fermion, Dirac fermion, massless gauge-plus-ghost vector, and massive Proca vector, respectively. Newton calibration is $K = 1$, and the target curvature is $c = 10^{-122}$.

Given a light seesaw scale $\mu = v^2/M$ ($v = 246.22\,\mathrm{GeV}$, $M$ a heavy spectral mass) and a positive integer polarization multiplicity $D$, the **four-layer positive vacuum stencil** assigns net polarization weights $D(-1, 3, -3, 1)$ at the equally spaced squared masses $(0, \mu^2, 2\mu^2, 3\mu^2)$. Up to an overall scale, this weight vector uniquely annihilates the $0$th, $1$st, and $2$nd mass-squared moments; its heat trace is $-D(1 - e^{-at})^3$ ($a := \mu^2/M_P^2$), yielding a finite, strictly positive vacuum density at every $Q > 0$:

$$
V_{\mathrm{4L}} := \frac{8\pi\rho_{\mathrm{4L}}}{M_P^4} = \frac{D a^2}{4\pi}\,I\!\left(\frac{a}{x}\right), \qquad I(y) := \int_y^\infty \frac{(1 - e^{-t})^3}{t^3}\,dt, \qquad I(0) = \frac{9\ln 3 - 12\ln 2}{2}.
$$

For fixed signed weights $p_i$ summing to one, let $W=\sum_i p_iw(x_i)=c$ and $K=\sum_i p_ik(x_i)=1$. Write $D=x\,d/dx$, $T=\sum_i|p_i|$, $L=\sum_i|p_i|x_i$, and $J=\sum_i|p_i|x_i^2$. Summation by parts over the visible polarization prefixes gives $a x^2\le Dw(x)\le b x^2$, where $a=62/(4\pi)$ and $b=958/(4\pi)$ suffice. The Newton coefficients give $|k(x)|,|Dk(x)|\le Bx$ with $B=32$. These estimates hold on the positive cutoff axis. Independent shifts of $\log x_i$ have a worst-direction derivative $S_W=\sum_i|p_i|Dw(x_i)$ per unit maximum shift. Thus
$$
 S_W\ \ge\ aJ\ \ge\ a\max\{1/(B^2 T),\,\min_i x_i/B,\,2c/b\}.
$$
The three terms follow from $1\le BL$ and $L^2\le TJ$, from $J\ge(\min_i x_i)L$, and from $w(x)\le bx^2/2$. A single coordinate can attain at least $S_W/n$ in an $n$-node layout. At $K=1$, the corresponding curvature derivative is $S_C=\sum_i|p_i||Dw(x_i)-2cDk(x_i)|$. With $r=\max\{\min_i x_i,1/(BT)\}$, it satisfies $S_C\ge (a-2cB/r)J$.

For the finite drift, use $x_i(t)=x_i e^{-\operatorname{sgn}(p_i)t}$. Then $W(t)\le c-aJ(1-e^{-2t})/2$ and $|K(t)-1|\le BL(e^t-1)$. If $aJ\ge8c$, taking $t=2c/(aJ)$ makes $W(t)<0$. If $4Bc/(ar)<1$, $K$ also stays positive throughout the drift, so the curvature changes sign. For example, $T\le10^6$ gives a flip by $t<4.2\times10^{-114}$ and keeps $|K-1|<8.4\times10^{-114}$. These are bounds in $\log x=2\log(Q/M_P)$. If upward moves beyond the Planck cutoff are excluded, move only the positive-weight nodes downward. Their quadratic sum $J_+$ is at least $aJ/(2b)$ by the matching equation for $W$. If $aJ_+\ge8c$, moving these nodes by $t_+=2c/(aJ_+)$ makes $W<0$. The Newton response stays positive along this path whenever $BL(1-e^{-t_+})<1$.

For layouts constrained only by the two matching equations and weight normalization, a positive derivative bound can coexist with arbitrarily large distances to a negative reading. At small $x$, the massless layer gives $k(x)\sim x/(12\pi)$ and $w(x)/k(x)\sim93x$. The positive-mass gap makes the omitted terms exponentially small. For $c=10^{-122}$, the equation $w(x_*)/k(x_*)=c$ has a simple solution with $c/186<x_*<2c/93$. Take two nodes $x,y$ and set $p=(1-k(y))/(k(x)-k(y))$. The weights $(p,1-p)$ give $K=1$. For every sufficiently small $y>0$, the condition $W=c$ has an exact solution $x=x(y)\to x_*$ by the implicit function theorem. The negative weight tends to the finite value $1-1/k(x_*)$. On any fixed box of independent logarithmic shifts, its vacuum contribution tends uniformly to zero, while the positive contribution has a strictly positive minimum. This family has a finite common variation bound of order $1/c$. A budget of that size can therefore allow arbitrarily large flip radii.

For layouts also stationary in the common scale, the vacuum derivative along that scale vanishes, so $aJ_+\le bJ_-$, where $J_-$ is the negative-weight quadratic sum. Lowering positive nodes and raising negative ones then gives $W(t)\le(bJ_+e^{-2t}-aJ_-e^{2t})/2<0$ whenever $t>\tfrac12\log(b/a)$. With a Planck ceiling, lowering only the positive nodes gives $W<0$ for $t>\log(b/a)$. In both cases $K(t)$ is analytic and starts at $1$, so any open interval of such times contains a point with negative curvature.

For these fixed spectra even the unconditional curvature sensitivity is strictly positive. Let $s$ be the unique positive zero of $H(x)=Dw(x)-2cDk(x)$. The positive-mass terms give $s>x_*$. The sign reduces to an exponentially small remainder at $x_*$ using $(j-1)E_j(u)+uE_{j-1}(u)=e^{-u}$. A charged row of mass below $57\kappa$ dominates the negative Proca contributions, whose masses exceed $96\kappa$. With $q=w(s)/k(s)>c$, the estimates at small cutoff and on the rest of the positive axis give $|w(x)-qk(x)|\le50|H(x)|$. Summing against the signed weights yields $S_C\ge(q-c)/50>0$. This last bound can be exponentially smaller than $c$. [Full derivation and spectrum checks](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-experiments/blob/d59dc6b6a7e2877bc0181d62646a02180e34a09a/experiments/ch39-omega-research/replay/ch39-independent-cutoff-sensitivity.md)

Whenever we audit whether three real response vectors—such as the vacuum value, Newton constant, and cutoff or Higgs slope, or the sensitivity vectors across three candidate zeros—are linearly independent or degenerate, the governing geometric invariant is the $3\times 3$ Gram determinant. Its exact expansion in any real inner-product space is frozen in the Lean 4 kernel:

::: {.theorem #thm-ou-ch11-three-anchor-gram title="Exact Three-Anchor Gram Determinant Identity" lean="D5/S3/QuantumContext/ThreeAnchorGram.three_anchor_gram_det_formula"}
Let $E$ be any real inner-product space (`[NormedAddCommGroup E]`, `[InnerProductSpace ℝ E]`), and let $u, v, w \in E$. Writing $G(u, v, w)$ for the $3\times 3$ Gram matrix of pairwise real inner products (`threeAnchorGram u v w`), its determinant satisfies

$$
\det G(u, v, w) = \|u\|^2\|v\|^2\|w\|^2 + 2\langle u, v\rangle\langle u, w\rangle\langle v, w\rangle - \|u\|^2\langle v, w\rangle^2 - \|v\|^2\langle u, w\rangle^2 - \|w\|^2\langle u, v\rangle^2.
$$

:::

::: proof
Set $a = \|u\|^2 = \langle u, u\rangle$, $b = \|v\|^2 = \langle v, v\rangle$, $c = \|w\|^2 = \langle w, w\rangle$, and $x = \langle u, v\rangle$, $y = \langle u, w\rangle$, $z = \langle v, w\rangle$. By symmetry of the real inner product, expanding the $3\times 3$ symmetric Gram determinant along its first row yields $a(bc - z^2) - x(xc - yz) + y(xz - by) = abc + 2xyz - az^2 - by^2 - cx^2$, which is the stated formula. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

## 11.3 Four-Layer $(-1, 3, -3, 1)$ Positive Vacuum Cancellation and the Cost of EWSB {#sec-ch11-four-layer-ewsb}

The 311-singlet match is pathologically fragile because it relies on accidental cancellation of $O(Q^4)$ divergent terms at one razor-thin cutoff. Can one instead cancel all divergent vacuum moments across **every** cutoff, leaving behind a finite, strictly positive vacuum energy?

### 11.3.1 Symmetric-Origin Mass-by-Mass Grading, Four-Layer Positivity, and Joint Zero Hits {#sec-ch11-four-layer-hits}

With both minimally coupled real scalars and gauge-singlet **Dirac fermions**, how many vacuum-density readings certify cancellation at every mass? Use the zero bare vacuum term of [@def-ou-ch11-four-layer-vacuum]. Put $u=1/x$, order the distinct squared masses $z_i$ increasingly, and let $g_i$ be the net polarization at each. A change of integration variable gives

$$
F(u):=-32\pi^2\rho/M_P^4=\int_u^\infty H(t)\,\frac{dt}{t^3},
\qquad H(t)=\sum_i g_i e^{-z_i t}.
$$

**Hand derivation.** For a finite spectrum with at least one nonzero $g_i$, let $s$ count sign changes after zero coefficients are removed. Since the exponents $-z_i$ strictly decrease, $H$ has at most $s$ distinct real zeros. Suppose $F$ vanishes at $r\ge1$ distinct positive inverse cutoffs. The integral of $H(t)/t^3$ vanishes between successive readings and on the tail after the last one. The weight is positive, so continuity gives a zero of $H$ inside each of these disjoint intervals. Thus $r\le s$. This turns the open-interval test for mass-by-mass cancellation into a finite test. The frozen zero bound applies to $H$. [Sign variations of an exponential sum](lean:D5/S3/Analytic/ExponentialSum/DescartesRule.zero_bound)

Regard the positive and negative polarizations as two towers with positive weights. If the reference tower has exactly $d\ge1$ distinct mass levels, all massive, exact vacuum cancellation at any $2d+1$ distinct positive inverse cutoffs forces equality layer by layer. A massless reference level reduces the count to $2d$. The comparison spectrum can have infinitely many levels if its proper-time integral is finite at the smallest inverse cutoff. Each positive heat trace decreases with $t$, so finiteness of the integral makes the trace finite to the right of the first cutoff. Dominated convergence gives continuity there. Their points of equality meet the nonuniform sampling criterion of [Section 5.1](ch05-kinetic-trace-and-uv-ceilings.md#sec-ou-ch05-s01).

Why does one fewer reading leave an ambiguity? Call this smaller count $m$ and fix the $m$ inverse cutoffs $u_j$. Interleave the reference levels with new ones to obtain $m+1$ nodes, ending with a new level. Start with a new level in the all-massive case and with the reference level $0$ in the massless case. Each node $z$ gives a column of readings $B(u_j,z)=\int_{u_j}^\infty e^{-zt}t^{-3}\,dt$. Any $m$ columns form an invertible matrix, since a nonzero combination of at most $m$ layers vanishing at $m$ cutoffs would exceed its sign-change bound. All these ordered minors have the same sign by continuity as the ordered nodes vary. The signed minors obtained by deleting each column in turn therefore give an alternating null vector. Choose its negative entries to lie on the reference levels. Adding a sufficiently small positive multiple to the reference weights gives a different positive tower with identical readings, for any prescribed positive real reference weights.

**Derivatives at a single cutoff (hand derivation).** If the two positive spectra have the same known total weight and all squared masses lie in $[0,Z]$ with $Z>0$, the readings can be taken at a single inverse cutoff $u_0>0$. Suppose the reference occupies $d\ge1$ levels, of which $e$ are at the interval endpoints. Starting with $F(u_0)$, the consecutive derivatives through order $2d-e-1$ give exactly enough data to decide mass-by-mass cancellation. Since $-u^3F'(u)$ is the heat trace, the derivatives give successive moments in squared mass weighted by $e^{-u_0z}$, while the function value retains a proper-time tail integral. Multiplying the observation functions by $e^{u_0z}$ lets repeated applications of Rolle's theorem bound their zeros. A linear combination of the observations can then give a nonnegative function with double zeros precisely at the interior reference levels and simple zeros at the occupied endpoints. Its integral is zero for each spectrum, so the comparison spectrum can only occupy the same levels. The moment equations then fix the individual weights. With one fewer reading, the alternating-minor construction above yields a counterexample with the same total weight. [Uniqueness at the boundary of a moment space](https://arxiv.org/html/1306.4872#S2) · [Derivation for the proper-time kernel](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/1824#issuecomment-7238099277)

For example, set $u_0=1$ and $Z=2$ and place unit weight at each of $z=1/2$ and $3/2$. Ordered minors for the total weight, $F$, $F'$ and $F''$ give a way to transfer some weight from both levels to $z=0,1,2$ while preserving all four data exactly. The fourth vacuum reading $F'''(1)$ then rises by about $0.00643933$. A single cutoff can thus distinguish these two spectra when sufficiently many exact derivatives are available. [Reproducing the two-level example](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/1824#issuecomment-7238099277)

**Vacuum and heat trace at different cutoffs (hand derivation, `ARGUED`).** Take two positive spectra with the same known positive total weight, zero bare vacuum term and squared masses in $[0,Z]$, where $0<Z<\infty$. Can a vacuum reading $F(u)$ at $u>0$ and a heat trace $H(v)$ at a different $v>0$ identify a single reference layer? Normalize the total weight to one and set $x=e^{-vz}$, $a=e^{-vZ}$ and $f(x)=B(u,-\log(x)/v)$. The heat trace gives the mean of $x$ and the vacuum reading gives the mean of $f(x)$. When $f$ is strictly convex, equality in Jensen's inequality identifies a single layer. The sharp condition for identifying **every** single layer is

$$
v\le R_u(Z),\qquad R_u(z)=\frac{I_1(z)}{I_2(z)},\qquad I_j(z)=\int_u^\infty t^{-j}e^{-tz}\,dt .
$$

Indeed, $v^2x^2 f''(x)=I_2(z)(R_u(z)-v)$. For $z>0$, $R_u$ is the mean proper time under the density proportional to $t^{-2}e^{-tz}$ on $[u,\infty)$, and its derivative is minus the variance. It decreases strictly from infinity as $z\downarrow0$ to $R_u(Z)>u$. At the threshold the graph is still strictly convex in the interior. Above it the graph is first concave, then convex as $x$ increases. Choose a center in each region and the same sufficiently small step $h$ at both. The difference $f(x-h)+f(x+h)-2f(x)$ has opposite signs at the two centers. At some intermediate center the two states $(x-h,x+h)$ and the coincident states $(x,x)$ give identical readings.

For a specified reference coordinate $s\in(a,1)$, evaluate the gap above the tangent, $D_s(y)=f(y)-f(s)-f'(s)(y-s)$, at the two endpoints. The layer is uniquely identified exactly when $D_s(a)D_s(1)\ge0$. Since the curvature changes sign at most once, the graph lies on one side of the tangent whenever the endpoint gaps have the same sign or one of them vanishes. It can touch again only at one endpoint. Matching the readings confines the weight to the contact points, and the mean $s$ excludes any weight at that extra endpoint. If the gaps have opposite signs, join each endpoint to a point just across $s$. The two chord heights at $s$ lie on opposite sides of $f(s)$. Moving the chord endpoints continuously gives a two-layer positive spectrum with both readings exactly matched. A reference at either endpoint is identified by the mean alone. [Endpoint test and integer two-state example](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/1689#issuecomment-7239914242)

With measurement error, let $\varepsilon$ be the larger absolute discrepancy in the two normalized readings and measure $W_1$ in squared mass. For fixed $u,v,Z$ with $v<R_u(Z)$, the distance from a comparison spectrum to a single reference layer is $O(\varepsilon^{1/2})$ uniformly over the reference layer. At $v=R_u(Z)$ the uniform bound is $O(\varepsilon^{1/3})$. At the threshold each fixed interior layer still has a square-root bound. At either endpoint the heat trace alone gives a linear bound.

The rates come from the gap above the tangent. Below the threshold, $f''$ has a positive lower bound throughout the interior. At the threshold, $f''(a)=0$ and the positive proper-time variance gives $f'''(a)>0$, so $f''(x)\ge c(x-a)$ for some $c>0$. Integration along the segment bounds $D_s(x)$ below by a constant times $|x-s|^2$ or $|x-s|^3$, respectively. Since $f'$ is bounded, the two reading errors control the spectrum's average tangent gap. Jensen's inequality and $|x-s|\ge va|z-z_*|$, where $z_*$ is the reference squared mass, then give the transport bounds. Splitting a fixed interior layer symmetrically in $x$ keeps the heat trace exact and gives a quadratic vacuum error. At the threshold, split the reference $s=a+h$ equally between $a$ and $a+2h$. The heat trace is still exact, the vacuum error is cubic, and the distance is of order $h$. The two splits show that the uniform exponents are sharp. [Uniform bound and proper-time integral check](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/1689#issuecomment-7240850180)

Above the threshold, the square-root bound holds whenever the reference passes the endpoint test. For fixed $u,v,Z$ the bound is uniform over identifiable layers, including a layer whose tangent also touches a band endpoint. Let $c$ be the inflection point. Its tangent gaps at the two endpoints have opposite signs, so the identifiable interior layers stay a positive distance from $c$. For such a layer $s$, let $\sigma=\operatorname{sgn}f''(s)$ and $Y=\max\{\sigma(x-s),0\}$. On the side where $Y>0$, the curvature keeps its sign and its magnitude has a uniform positive lower bound $m$. Here $f''(a)<0$, and $I_1(z)\to\infty$ with $I_2(z)\le1/u$, so $f''(x)\to+\infty$ as $x\uparrow1$. Thus $\sigma D_s(x)\ge mY^2/2$, and $\sigma D_s(x)\ge0$ on the other side. The heat trace controls the difference between displacements on the two sides because $\mathbb E|x-s|=2\mathbb E Y-\sigma(\mathbb E x-s)$. Averaging the tangent gap and using $f'(s)\le1/(uva)$ gives $\mathbb E Y^2\le 2(1+1/(uva))\varepsilon/m$. It follows that

$$
vaW_1\le 2\sqrt{\frac{2(1+1/(uva))}{m}\,\varepsilon}+\varepsilon .
$$

An equal-weight split of an interior layer shows that the exponent $1/2$ is still sharp. If the tangent also touches an endpoint $e$, a split placing weight $h/(|e-s|+h)$ at $e$ and the rest at $s+\sigma h$ preserves the heat trace exactly. The tangent gap vanishes at the endpoint, so the vacuum error is quadratic in $h$. Both the endpoint weight and the transport distance in squared mass are of order $h$. [Hand derivation and interval check for endpoint contact](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/1689#issuecomment-7241164027)

When the tangent touches the heavy endpoint $a$, allowing error in both readings gives an exact local constant at the contact layer. Write $p=f'(s)$ and $k=f''(s)>0$, and let $\omega_s(\varepsilon)$ be the supremum of the squared-mass transport distance over positive comparison spectra with reading error at most $\varepsilon$. Then

$$
\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\frac{\omega_s(\varepsilon)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
=\left(\frac{1}{vs}+\frac{Z-z_*}{s-a}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2(1+p)}{k}} .
$$

Near the reference layer, the quadratic tangent gap bounds the mean absolute displacement in the heat coordinate by $\sqrt{2(1+p)\varepsilon/k}$ at leading order. The gap rises linearly from zero near the heavy endpoint, so moving the weight in that neighborhood to $a$ costs only $O(\varepsilon)$ in transport distance. The heat trace bounds that endpoint weight by the mean displacement divided by $s-a$ at leading order. The two terms in parentheses convert the local displacement and the endpoint weight to squared-mass distance. A two-layer spectrum at $a$ and $s+h$ attains the leading constant by making the heat-trace error $-\varepsilon$ and the vacuum error $+\varepsilon$. The mean tangent gap then uses the full allowance $(1+p)\varepsilon$. [Hand derivation of the sharp constant, uniform threshold limit, and interval check](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/1689#issuecomment-7241652592)

Just above the threshold, a band of interior layers next to the heavy endpoint loses identifiability. The endpoint itself is still identified by the mean. Fix $u,Z$ and let $v_0=R_u(Z)$, $\delta=v-v_0>0$, and $V=-R'_u(Z)>0$, the proper-time variance at squared mass $Z$. For sufficiently small $\delta$, the identifiable layers are exactly $[0,z_c]\cup\{Z\}$, where

$$
Z-z_c=\frac{3}{2V}\delta+O(\delta^2).
$$

At the boundary $z_c$, the tangent also touches the heavy endpoint $a$. For $d=Z-z_*$, the gap there is $D_s(a)=B(u,Z)-B(u,Z-d)+I_2(Z-d)(1-e^{-vd})/v$. Dividing by $I_2(Z)d^2$ and extending to $d=0$ gives $-\delta/2+Vd/3+O(\delta d+d^2)$. Its zero gives the stated expansion. Since $\partial_s D_s(a)=(s-a)f''(s)$, the heavy-end gap first falls and then rises, with this single interior zero. The light-end gap $D_s(1)$ first rises and then falls to zero. At the threshold $D_a(1)$ is strictly positive, so just above it the light-end gap is still positive for every interior reference.

In this near-threshold range, the supremum of $W_1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ over all identifiable single reference layers and comparison probability spectra with positive reading error is $K_c$. Every such ratio is strictly below $K_c$. Fix the contact layer $s_c=e^{-vz_c}$ and let $\omega_{s_c}(\eta)$ be the supremum of the squared-mass transport distance $W_1$ to that layer over comparison spectra with normalized reading error $\varepsilon\le\eta$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_c&=\lim_{\eta\downarrow0}\frac{\omega_{s_c}(\eta)}{\sqrt{\eta}}\\
&=\left(\frac{1}{vs_c}+\frac{Z-z_c}{s_c-a}\right)
\sqrt{\frac{2(1+f'(s_c))}{f''(s_c)}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Place two layers at $a$ and $s_c+h$ and choose their weights so that the normalized heat trace and vacuum reading differ from their reference values by $-\varepsilon$ and $+\varepsilon$, respectively. The ratios for these comparison spectra tend to $K_c$ as $h\downarrow0$. When the heat trace falls below its reference value, the heavy-side displacement uses weight that would otherwise be available on the light side. Keeping the light-side weight in the Cauchy–Schwarz bound offsets the increase caused by the heat-trace error. As $\delta\downarrow0$, $K_c=\Theta(\delta^{-1/2})$. [Threshold expansion](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/1689#issuecomment-7241441212) · [Sharp constant at the fixed contact layer](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/1689#issuecomment-7241652592) · [Hand derivation and replay for the sharp bound at all errors](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-books/issues/1689#issuecomment-7241841089)

**Sharp bound for fixed integer multiplicities (`ARGUED`).** The reference and comparison spectra may have any positive integer multiplicities. Order the positive reference masses. Let $h$ of these levels have at least two states. They divide the single-state levels into runs of lengths $\ell_0,\ldots,\ell_h$, allowing empty runs. The exact number of readings needed to identify the reference at any prescribed distinct positive inverse cutoffs is $r+1$. Set $\delta=1$ when the reference has a massless level and $\delta=0$ otherwise.

$$
r=
\begin{cases}
2h+\ell_0+2\sum_{j=1}^h\lfloor\ell_j/2\rfloor,&\delta=0,\\
1+2h+2\sum_{j=0}^h\lfloor\ell_j/2\rfloor,&\delta=1.
\end{cases}
$$

When each of the $d$ reference levels has a single state, the count is $d+1$, falling to $d$ when there is a massless level and $d$ is even. When each positive reference level has at least two states, the integer spectra attain the real-weight bound above.

Let $C(z)$ count comparison states minus reference states up to squared mass $z$. Write $B(u,z)=\int_z^\infty A(u,w)\,dw$, where $A(u,w)=\int_u^\infty e^{-wt}t^{-2}\,dt$. Applying Tonelli's theorem to each spectrum gives the reading difference $\int_u^\infty t^{-2}L(t)\,dt$, with $L(t)=\int_0^\infty e^{-zt}C(z)\,dz$. Finiteness at the smallest inverse cutoff makes both transforms of the nonnegative cumulative counts finite and continuous to its right. Every interval between readings and the tail beyond the last reading supply zeros of $L$. If $C$ is nonzero and changes sign $s$ times, take any $s+1$ distinct zeros of $L$ and form a nonzero linear combination of their exponentials that vanishes at the $s$ sign changes. The exponential sum has at most $s$ zeros counted with multiplicity, so all these roots are simple. Choose its sign to match $C$ wherever $C$ is nonzero. The product then has positive integral, although the chosen zeros of $L$ make that integral zero.

A transition from positive to negative uses either a reference level with several states or at least two single-state levels from the same run. Separate transitions use disjoint reference levels. Starting positive gives at most $m=h+\sum_{j=0}^h\lfloor\ell_j/2\rfloor$ downward transitions from the positive masses, hence at most $2m$ changes of sign. Starting negative also consumes the earliest reference level. If the remaining levels allow $m'$ downward transitions, there are at most $1+2m'$ sign changes. A massless level can never contribute to a downward transition, so the massless case gives at most $1+2m$ changes. In the all-positive case, removing the first level lowers the budget by one unless the leading single-state run has odd length. Together these cases give $s\le r$, so $r+1$ readings force the spectra to agree.

To match any prescribed $r$ readings, add one state at squared mass $X$ and move some of the reference states. In the all-positive case, select the whole leading single-state run. Select an even number from each other run, leaving out at most its last level. In the massless case select an even number from every run and give the entire massless level one adjustable mass. Each selected single-state level and the massless level contribute one displacement parameter. At each positive level of multiplicity $w\ge2$, place one state at $z+a-(w-1)\sqrt v$ and the remaining $w-1$ at $z+a+\sqrt v$. The parameters shift the mean by $a$ and split the mass when $v>0$. The first-order terms in $\sqrt v$ cancel, giving derivative $w(w-1)B_{zz}(u,z)/2$ at $v=0$. Extending by the tangent to $v<0$ gives a continuously differentiable map. Extend the shift of the massless level by the tangent with $B_z(u,0)=-1/u$.

Order the $r$ parameter columns of the Jacobian $J$ by mass. Each displacement column is a positive multiple of $-A(u,z)$. Each split column follows its displacement column and is a positive multiple of $-\partial_z A(u,z)$. The ordered minors used here are nonzero. A dependence of $n$ columns would give at least $n$ distinct zeros of an exponential polynomial with $n$ coefficients, by integrating over the intervals between the cutoffs and over the tail. Factoring out one exponential, differentiating to eliminate its polynomial factor, and applying Rolle's theorem proves the bound of $n-1$ by induction. An ordered minor of $A$ columns of size $n$ has sign $(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}$. Scaling all masses up gives this sign from the term pairing the rows and columns in reverse order, whose exponential decays slowest. Taking positively oriented divided differences and letting the adjacent mass pairs coalesce preserves the sign for minors with derivative columns immediately following their parent columns.

With $b_j=B(u_j,X)$, the inverse function theorem gives parameters $-J^{-1}b+o(\lVert b\rVert)$ as $X\to\infty$. The first reading dominates the others asymptotically. The first entry in row $j$ of $J^{-1}$ has sign $(-1)^{r+j+1}$ for a split parameter or the massless displacement, by the corresponding ordered minors. Every split parameter has the same parity as $r$, since an even number of singleton columns follows it and the later repeated levels each use two columns. The massless displacement is first and $r$ is odd in that case. These entries of $J^{-1}$ are therefore negative, so the split parameters and the massless displacement are positive for large $X$. The resulting spectrum has allowed masses and agrees with the reference at the given $r$ cutoffs.

A single massless level illustrates the ambiguity. At $u=1$, one massless state gives $B(1,0)=1/2$. Two states of the same positive mass give $2B(1,z)$, which decreases continuously and strictly from $1$ to $0$ as $z$ grows. Exactly one $z>0$ matches the first reading, and any second distinct positive inverse cutoff distinguishes the towers.

**Local stability for real weights (`ARGUED`).** Suppose both positive spectra are supported on the known squared-mass interval $[0,Z]$ with $0<Z<\infty$, and fix a reference spectrum with $d$ occupied levels, $e$ of them at the endpoints. Write $k=2d-e$. Any $k+1$ distinct positive inverse cutoffs suffice. If the total weight is known and shared, any $k$ suffice. At these cutoffs let $\delta$ be the largest absolute discrepancy in the $B(u,z)$ readings. For small $\delta$, the total weight and the weights assigned to the nearest reference levels change by at most $C\delta$. In the squared-mass coordinate, the probability-normalized $W_1$ distance is at most $C\sqrt{\delta}$ if the reference occupies an interior level. If it occupies only endpoints the bound is $C\delta$. The constant can depend on the cutoffs, the interval and the fixed reference.

To see this, order the cutoffs and subtract consecutive kernels. The resulting rows integrate $e^{-tz}$ over disjoint ordered intervals with weight $t^{-3}$. With known total weight, the constant row corresponds to $t=0$. Interpolation matrices for values and consecutive derivatives are nonsingular. By multilinearity, their determinants integrate confluent exponential determinants of a fixed nonzero sign, using the zero bound with multiplicity for distinct exponentials. Only a first derivative is needed at $z=0$, where $B_z(u,0)=-1/u$. In the span of the available rows choose $q$ that vanishes twice at the interior reference levels and once at the occupied endpoints, and is positive at one point outside the reference support. The interpolation determinant makes these the exact zeros. Thus $q$ is positive elsewhere and bounds from below the squared distance to each interior nearest level and the distance to each endpoint nearest level, up to a positive constant. Its integral against the comparison spectrum is $O(\delta)$. Interpolating each layer indicator on the reference support, with derivative zero at interior levels, bounds its difference from the nearest-layer indicator by a multiple of $q$. This controls the layer weights. Moving each comparison mass to its nearest reference level, then adjusting these weights, gives the transport bounds.

The exponents are sharp. Splitting an interior layer equally to $z-h$ and $z+h$ moves probability a distance of order $h$ and changes the readings by order $h^2$. Moving a single endpoint layer inward by $h$ gives distance and reading changes of order $h$. For the sharp reading counts, insert one new mass between each adjacent reference pair and on each side with room. With one fewer reading, the ordered evaluation matrix on the $k+1$ alternating old and new levels has $k$ rows, including the constant when available. Its signed maximal minors alternate. Orient them positively at the new levels and add a small multiple to the reference weights. This gives a distinct positive spectrum with identical readings. The linear endpoint power refers to distance in squared mass. Moving a massless atom to squared mass $h$ has distance $\sqrt h$ in mass units.

**Local stability with a mass bound (`ARGUED`).** Fix an integer reference spectrum and require the squared masses of both spectra to lie in $[0,Z]$ for some $Z>0$. At the $r+1$ inverse cutoffs needed for exact identification, let $\delta$ be the largest error in the normalized readings. For sufficiently small $\delta$, the spectra have the same total number of states, which can be matched in order of mass. If a reference level with $0<z<Z$ has at least two states, the sum of the squared-mass displacements in absolute value is at most $C\sqrt\delta$. In all other cases it is at most $C\delta$. Constants can depend on the reference, the mass bound, and the sample locations.

At a fixed cutoff every state contributes at least $B(u,Z)>0$, so a bounded reading bounds the number of states. For each state count, ordered spectra form a compact set that includes coincident masses. Exact uniqueness then forces the number of states near each reference level to equal its multiplicity as the errors tend to zero. Let $\eta$ be the largest absolute displacement. At each positive reference level $z_i$, let $a_i,b_i$ be the sum and sum of squares of its displacements. At zero use just their nonnegative sum $a_0$. The first-order reading terms are $-A(u,z_i)a_i$, and the positive-mass second-order terms are $-\partial_z A(u,z_i)b_i/2$, with remainder $O(\eta\sum b_i)+o(a_0)$. At a single-state level and at the upper endpoint $Z$ one has $b_i\le\eta|a_i|$. Independence of the $A$ columns at the positive masses lets us absorb those small terms and bound $\sum_{z_i>0}|a_i|\le C(\delta+a_0+\sum_* b_i)$, where the star restricts the sum to repeated positive levels in the interior. This directly gives the linear estimate when both remaining terms are absent.

If there is a massless level or a repeated interior level, choose a real combination $g(z)=\sum_j c_j A(u_j,z)$ that vanishes simply at every positive reference mass. Between consecutive repeated interior levels, insert an extra simple root if the number of intervening reference levels is even. If the reference has both a massless level and a repeated interior level, insert a root before the first such interior level when the count of positive levels preceding it is even. The number of roots needed is bounded by the number of positive reference levels plus the number of even-length singleton runs between repeated levels, counting an even leading run as well when there is a massless level. The formula for $r$ bounds that number by $r$. Use one more sampled cutoff than the number of specified roots. The ordered and confluent minors above make the prescribed roots simple and rule out any others. Orient $g$ so $-g'(z_i)>0$ at all starred levels and $-g(0)>0$ when there is a massless level. If the massless level is the only level needing this estimate, choose the sign at zero.

Taking the same combination of the reading errors cancels the first-order terms at positive masses and reads $a_0$ and the starred second moments with strictly positive coefficients. The estimate for the displacement sums absorbs the remaining terms. This gives $a_0+\sum_* b_i\le C\delta$ and hence $\sum_{z_i>0}|a_i|\le C'\delta$.
