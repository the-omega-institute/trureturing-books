# Chapter 4 The golden word and returns

[Chapter 1](ch01-golden-deficit.md) added the Zeckendorf digits of two natural numbers and studied the deficit left behind by the carries. This chapter takes another view: listing the lowest Zeckendorf digit of every natural number in turn gives an infinite word over two letters. It is the golden word generated by a substitution rule ([@prop-golden-word-zeckendorf]), and it has exactly $n+1$ distinct blocks of each length $n$ ([@thm-factor-complexity]). The last two sections turn to the returns of the golden rotation and to one use of the deficit on prime factorizations.

The chapter uses the following terms.

Word
: A finite or infinite sequence of letters. The length of a finite word $u$ is $|u|$; letters are counted from position $0$.

Factor
: A finite word made of consecutive letters of an infinite word, starting at some position. The only factor of length $0$ is the empty word.

$\mathrm{zeck}(i)$
: The Fibonacci indices used by the Zeckendorf representation of the natural number $i$, in decreasing order: `wdigits i` in Lean (mathlib's `Nat.zeckendorf i`). Indices follow mathlib's convention $F_0 = 0$, $F_1 = F_2 = 1$, $F_3 = 2$: every index is at least $2$, and no two are adjacent. For example $12 = F_6 + F_4 + F_2$, so $\mathrm{zeck}(12) = [6, 4, 2]$. For existence and uniqueness see [@thm-zeckendorf-exists] and [@thm-zeckendorf-unique] in [Appendix A](appendix-a-zeckendorf.md).

::: {.definition #def-golden-word title="The golden word" lean="D5/S1/Words/GoldenWord.goldenWord"}
Take two letters $\mathtt{a}$ and $\mathtt{b}$, standing for `true` and `false` in Lean. The substitution $\sigma$ is

$$
\sigma(\mathtt{a}) = \mathtt{ab}, \qquad \sigma(\mathtt{b}) = \mathtt{a},
$$

applied to a finite word letter by letter, with the results concatenated in order. Let

$$
w_0 = \mathtt{a}, \qquad w_{Q+1} = \sigma(w_Q) \quad (Q \in \mathbb{N}).
$$

The golden word is the infinite word $g = g(0)\,g(1)\,g(2) \cdots$ in which $g(i)$ is letter $i$ of $w_i$.
:::

The definition reads along the diagonal: letter $i$ is taken from level $i$. This always works, because $|w_Q| = F_{Q+2}$ (`fibWord_length` in trureturing) and $i < F_{i+2}$. The first levels are:

| $Q$ | $w_Q$ | length $F_{Q+2}$ |
| ---: | :--- | ---: |
| 0 | $\mathtt{a}$ | 1 |
| 1 | $\mathtt{ab}$ | 2 |
| 2 | $\mathtt{aba}$ | 3 |
| 3 | $\mathtt{abaab}$ | 5 |
| 4 | $\mathtt{abaababa}$ | 8 |
| 5 | $\mathtt{abaababaabaab}$ | 13 |
| 6 | $\mathtt{abaababaabaababaababa}$ | 21 |

Each level is a prefix of the next: $w_0 = \mathtt{a}$ is a prefix of $w_1 = \mathtt{ab}$, and if $u$ is a prefix of $u'$ then $\sigma(u)$ is a prefix of $\sigma(u')$. So all levels are prefixes of one infinite word, and $g(i)$ can be read from any level longer than $i$ (`goldenWord_eq_fibWord_get` in trureturing); in this sense $g$ is the limit of the $w_Q$. For instance, the first 21 letters of $g$ are $w_6$. The Lean definition takes the diagonal directly and needs no notion of limit.

::: {.proposition #prop-golden-word-zeckendorf title="The golden word and the lowest Zeckendorf digit" lean="D5/S1/Words/GoldenWord.goldenWord_char_zeckendorf"}
For every natural number $i$,

$$
g(i) = \mathtt{a} \iff 2 \notin \mathrm{zeck}(i).
$$

In words: $g(i) = \mathtt{a}$ exactly when the Zeckendorf representation of $i$ does not use the weight $F_2 = 1$.
:::

The table lists $0 \le i \le 20$. The letters $g(i)$ were computed from the substitution, and $\mathrm{zeck}(i)$ by the greedy algorithm (repeatedly take the largest Fibonacci number not exceeding what is left). Both agree with the proposition for all $i < 3000$; this is a finite check, not a proof.

| 1 | $[2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ | 12 | $[6, 4, 2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ |

| 4 | $[4, 2]$ | $\mathtt{b}$ | 15 | $[7, 3]$ | $\mathtt{a}$ |

::: proof
The Lean proof does not compare position by position; it shows that both sides obey the same recursion. On one side, induction on $Q$ gives $w_{Q+2} = w_{Q+1}\,w_Q$. On the other, for $0 \le j < F_{Q+2}$ the list $\mathrm{zeck}(F_{Q+3} + j)$ is $\mathrm{zeck}(j)$ with the index $Q+3$ put in front (`wdigits_fib_add` in trureturing); since $Q + 3 \ne 2$, positions $F_{Q+3} + j$ and $j$ get the same verdict. So the word of length $F_{Q+2}$ written down by the criterion $2 \notin \mathrm{zeck}(i)$ obeys the same recursion and also starts with $\mathtt{a}$ and $\mathtt{ab}$, hence equals $w_Q$ at every level. Reading letter $i$ at level $i$ gives the proposition. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

[@prop-golden-word-zeckendorf] describes single letters; the next theorem is about consecutive letters. The factor of length $n$ at position $i$ is $g(i)\,g(i+1) \cdots g(i+n-1)$.

::: {.theorem #thm-factor-complexity title="Exactly $n+1$ factors of length $n$" lean="D5/S1/Words/GoldenFactorComplexity.golden_factor_complexity"}
For every natural number $n$, the golden word has exactly $n + 1$ distinct factors of length $n$:

$$
\#\bigl\{\, g(i)\,g(i+1) \cdots g(i+n-1) \;:\; i \in \mathbb{N} \,\bigr\} = n + 1 .
$$
:::

For $n = 0$ the set contains only the empty word. The table lists the factors for $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$, with the position of the first occurrence in parentheses. They were found in the prefix $w_{25}$, of length $F_{27} = 196418$. Such a computation only shows that these factors occur; it cannot show that no other factor appears further out, which is what the theorem says.

| $n$ | factors of length $n$ (first occurrence) | count |
| ---: | :--- | ---: |
| 1 | $\mathtt{a}$ (0), $\mathtt{b}$ (1) | 2 |
| 2 | $\mathtt{aa}$ (2), $\mathtt{ab}$ (0), $\mathtt{ba}$ (1) | 3 |
| 3 | $\mathtt{aab}$ (2), $\mathtt{aba}$ (0), $\mathtt{baa}$ (1), $\mathtt{bab}$ (4) | 4 |
| 4 | $\mathtt{aaba}$ (2), $\mathtt{abaa}$ (0), $\mathtt{abab}$ (3), $\mathtt{baab}$ (1), $\mathtt{baba}$ (4) | 5 |

For example, $\mathtt{bb}$ never occurs: every level $w_{Q+1} = \sigma(w_Q)$ is a concatenation of the blocks $\mathtt{ab}$ and $\mathtt{a}$, every $\mathtt{b}$ ends a block $\mathtt{ab}$, and the next block always begins with $\mathtt{a}$.

$n + 1$ is very few: an infinite word that has at most $n$ factors of length $n$ for some $n$ is periodic from some position on[^sturm]. Among words that are not eventually periodic, the complexity in [@thm-factor-complexity] is therefore the smallest possible; words whose factor complexity is exactly $n+1$ are called Sturmian words.

::: proof
The Lean proof follows the classical route for Sturmian words. It first writes the golden word as a difference of floors: the number of $\mathtt{a}$'s among the $m$ letters starting at position $i$ is

$$
\left\lfloor \frac{i+m+1}{\varphi} \right\rfloor - \left\lfloor \frac{i+1}{\varphi} \right\rfloor
$$

(`goldenWindowTrueCount_eq_floor` in trureturing, derived from [@prop-golden-word-zeckendorf] through the Beatty sequence). Write $\{x\}$ for the fractional part and $x_i = \{(i+1)/\varphi\}$ for the phase; since $1/\varphi = \varphi - 1$, this is the $p(i)$ of [@thm-golden-phase]. For $1 \le m \le n$, the count above is $\lfloor m/\varphi \rfloor$, plus $1$ when $x_i \ge 1 - \{m/\varphi\}$. These $n$ cut points $1 - \{m/\varphi\}$ are distinct (because $1/\varphi$ is irrational) and divide $[0, 1)$ into $n + 1$ pieces. The factor of length $n$ is recovered from the counts by taking successive differences, so it depends only on the piece that contains $x_i$; hence there are at most $n + 1$ factors. Conversely, the phases $x_i$ are dense in $[0, 1)$, so every piece contains one, and phases in different pieces give different factors; hence there are at least $n + 1$. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

[^sturm]: M. Morse, G. A. Hedlund, Symbolic dynamics, *Amer. J. Math.* 60 (1938), 815–866; for Sturmian words see their Symbolic dynamics II. Sturmian trajectories, *Amer. J. Math.* 62 (1940), 1–42. This is a result from the literature, not formalized in this book: the cited Lean declaration only counts the factors of the golden word and does not contain this periodicity theorem. The other direction is elementary: an eventually periodic word has only boundedly many distinct factors, while $n + 1$ is unbounded, so the golden word is not eventually periodic.

## Returns {#sec-returns}

In the proof of [@thm-factor-complexity], the golden word is governed by the phase $x_i$, and $x_{i+1}$ is $x_i$ turned by $1/\varphi$ around a circle of circumference $1$. Since $\varphi = 1 + 1/\varphi$, turning by $1/\varphi$ and turning by $\varphi$ are the same rotation. When the phase comes back into the same piece, the same factor reappears; how soon factors recur depends on how soon this rotation comes back close to where it started.

A finite cyclic window is restored exactly after one full loop (trureturing calls this a revival; see the `CyclicWindowRevival` module). The golden rotation is never restored exactly: after $q$ steps its distance from the start is the distance $\norm{q\varphi}$ from $q\varphi$ to the nearest integer, which is not $0$ for any $q \ge 1$. Along Fibonacci times, [`fibonacci_golden_residual`](lean:D5/S1/Scale/FibonacciErrorRatio.fibonacci_golden_residual) gives the exact residual

$$
F_n\varphi - F_{n+1} = -\left(-\frac{1}{\varphi}\right)^{n},
$$

so for $n \ge 2$ the integer nearest to $F_n\varphi$ is $F_{n+1}$, and $\norm{F_n\varphi} = \varphi^{-n}$. The error shrinks like $\varphi^{-n}$ while the waiting time $F_n$ grows on the order of $\varphi^{n}$; their product $F_n\,|F_n\varphi - F_{n+1}|$ is called the revival score.

::: {.theorem #thm-revival-score title="The limit of the revival score" lean="D5/S3/ObserverMemory/GoldenRevivalScore.golden_fibonacci_revival_score_tendsto"}
Let $F_n$ be the Fibonacci numbers ($F_0 = 0$, $F_1 = 1$) and $\varphi = (1+\sqrt5)/2$. Then

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} F_n \left| F_n\varphi - F_{n+1} \right| = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}.
$$
:::

::: proof
By the residual formula above, $|F_n\varphi - F_{n+1}| = \varphi^{-n}$. Binet's formula $F_n = (\varphi^n - \psi^n)/\sqrt5$ (`Real.coe_fib_eq` in mathlib), with $\psi = -1/\varphi$, then gives the exact closed form

$$
F_n \left| F_n\varphi - F_{n+1} \right| = \frac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt5}\,\varphi^{-n} = \frac{1 - (-\psi^2)^n}{\sqrt5}.
$$

Since $\psi^2 = 1/\varphi^2 < 1$, the correction $(-\psi^2)^n$ tends to $0$. The Lean proof establishes exactly this closed form and then passes to the limit. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

Fibonacci times give one limit. Which other finite limits become possible if we allow all return times? For a fixed real quadratic irrational, the literature gives a complete answer. We first state the general theorem of Dhanda and Haynes, then translate it into the nearest-integer distance used in this chapter[^dhanda-haynes].

::: {.theorem #thm-quadratic-accumulation title="Accumulation points of normalized approximations to a real quadratic irrational" lean="D5/S3/Arith/QuadraticFields/QuadraticAccumulationClassification.derivedSet_eq_signed_norm_values"}
Let $D\ge2$ be a squarefree integer, $A,B\in\mathbb Q$ with $B\ne0$, $\alpha=A+B\sqrt D$, and $K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)$. Put

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Lambda&=\mathbb Z+\alpha\mathbb Z,\\
\Lambda^*&=\{s\in K:\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(s\lambda)\in\mathbb Z
\text{ for all }\lambda\in\Lambda\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Let $\mathcal A_1(\alpha)$ be the set of accumulation points in $\mathbb R$ of

$$
\mathrm{NA}_1(\alpha)=\{|q|(q\alpha-p):q\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\},\ p\in\mathbb Z\}:
$$

every neighborhood of an accumulation point contains an element of this set different from the point itself. Then

$$
\mathcal A_1(\alpha)=
\{\pm2B\sqrt D\,\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(s):s\in\Lambda^*\setminus\{0\}\}.
$$

Here the trace and norm are $\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(x)=x+x'$ and $\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(x)=xx'$, respectively, where the prime denotes conjugation sending $\sqrt D$ to $-\sqrt D$. There is no coprimality condition on $p,q$.
:::

::: {.proof #pf-quadratic-accumulation}
We retain the unit argument of Section 6, Lemmas 18–19, and Section 7, equations (35)–(36), as an explanation of the published proof, then explain the discrete-grid argument Lean uses for the accumulation-to-norm direction. Both establish the same classification; this does not claim that Lean verifies the paper’s unit reduction line by line. Write $\alpha_0^*,\alpha_1^*$ for the basis dual to $1,\alpha$ under the trace pairing. Section 2.3 considers the multiplier ring

$$
\mathcal Z_\Lambda=\{v\in\mathcal O_K:v\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda\},
$$

where $\mathcal O_K$ is the ring of algebraic integers in $K$. Its units preserve $\Lambda^*$ under multiplication. The unit lattice of Lemma 13 in that section supplies, in the quadratic case, positive dominant units $u_k\to\infty$, with conjugates satisfying $|u_k'|=u_k^{-1}$. Lemma 18 states that for each $C>0$ there is a finite set $S_C\subseteq\Lambda^*$ such that every integer pair with $q>0$ and $q|q\alpha-p|\le C$ admits a representation

$$
q\alpha_0^*+p\alpha_1^*=su,
\qquad s\in S_C,\quad u\in\mathcal Z_\Lambda^\times,
$$

with $u$ dominant; here $|u|$ and $|u'|$ are comparable, within fixed positive factors, to $q$ and $q^{-1}$, respectively. This finite reduction comes from dividing a dual-lattice element whose two embeddings are $O(q)$ and $O(q^{-1})$ by such a unit, leaving a bounded lattice set.

For any fixed $s\in\Lambda^*\setminus\{0\}$ and such a unit $u$, set $t=su$ and take the integers

$$
q_u=\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(t),\qquad
p_u=\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(t\alpha).
$$

Expanding the traces gives the one-dimensional formulas of Lemma 19:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_u\alpha-p_u&=(\alpha-\alpha')t'=2B\sqrt D\,t',\\
\gamma_u:=|q_u|\,|t'|
&=|\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(s)|\left|1+\frac{t'}{t}\right|.
\end{aligned}
$$

A unit has norm of absolute value $1$. As $|u|\to\infty$, we have $t'/t\to0$, so $\gamma_u\to|\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(s)|$, while $|q_u|(q_u\alpha-p_u)=\pm2B\sqrt D\,\gamma_u$.

First we show that every accumulation point belongs to the stated norm set. Choose distinct normalized approximation values tending to an accumulation point. They are bounded. Bounded $|q|$ would allow only finitely many corresponding $p$, contradicting distinctness, so we can pass to a subsequence with $|q|\to\infty$. The map $(q,p)\mapsto(-q,-p)$ negates the normalized value, so it suffices to treat positive-denominator sequences and their negatives. Using the finite set in Lemma 18, pass to a further subsequence with a fixed nonzero $s$; the corresponding dominant units satisfy $|u|\to\infty$. The formulas above allow only the limits $\pm2B\sqrt D\,|\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(s)|$, which belong to the right-hand side of the theorem.

Conversely, fix any nonzero $s\in\Lambda^*$ and let $u=u_k\to\infty$. Then $q_{u_k}$ is eventually nonzero, and the absolute values of the normalized approximations tend to $2|B|\sqrt D\,|\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(s)|$. We must ensure a genuine accumulation point of the set, rather than repetition of a single value. The expression above from equation (33) guarantees $\gamma_u\ne|\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(s)|$: equality would force the nonzero real number $t'/t$ to be $-2$, but conjugating $t'=-2t$ would give $t=4t$, contradicting $t\ne0$. On a subsequence with constant sign, set elements different from their limit therefore tend to one of the two signed limits. Symmetry of $\mathrm{NA}_1(\alpha)$ about $0$ gives the other sign as well. This proves the reverse inclusion. Allowing both signs also makes the norm set unchanged when the norm is replaced by its absolute value.

For the converse in Lean, start directly with any genuine accumulation point $x$. Bounded normalized values with bounded $|q|$ allow only finitely many integer pairs $p,q$; a punctured neighborhood can avoid this finite set. We can therefore choose $q_n\ne0,p_n\in\mathbb Z$ with $|q_n|>n$ and $y_n=|q_n|(q_n\alpha-p_n)\to x$. Set $e_n=q_n\alpha-p_n=y_n/|q_n|$, so $e_n\to0$. Write $\delta=2B\sqrt D\ne0$ and take, in $K$,

$$
s_n=\frac{q_n}{2}+\frac{p_n-Aq_n}{2DB}\sqrt D.
$$

Direct calculation gives $\operatorname{Tr}(s_n)=q_n$ and $\operatorname{Tr}(s_n\alpha)=p_n$, hence $s_n\in\Lambda^*$. Its constant coordinate $q_n/2$ is nonzero, so $s_n\ne0$. Its norm satisfies

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname N(s_n)
&=\left(\frac14-\frac{A^2}{4DB^2}\right)q_n^2
 +\frac{A}{2DB^2}q_np_n-\frac{p_n^2}{4DB^2},\\
\delta\operatorname N(s_n)&=q_ne_n-\frac{e_n^2}{\delta}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The three coefficients of this quadratic form are fixed rational numbers. Choose a positive integer $m$ clearing their denominators; then $m\operatorname N(s_n)\in\mathbb Z$. With $\varepsilon_n=\operatorname{sgn}(q_n)\in\{1,-1\}$, the integer sequence

$$
j_n:=m\varepsilon_n\operatorname N(s_n)
=\frac m\delta\left(y_n-\frac{\varepsilon_ne_n^2}{\delta}\right)
\longrightarrow\frac m\delta x.
$$

The integers form a closed discrete subset of the reals, so the limit is an integer and $j_n$ eventually equals it. Any index in this tail gives $x=\varepsilon_n\delta\operatorname N(s_n)$, one of the required signed values. Lean performs these calculations in the coordinate quadratic algebra and transports them through an algebra equivalence preserving traces and norms to the actual subfield $\mathbb Q(\alpha)\subset\mathbb R$. The other inclusion uses the Pell multiplier construction of the next theorem, which also supplies an injective approximation sequence avoiding the limit. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

[^dhanda-haynes]: Kavita Dhanda and Alan Haynes, *Accumulation points of normalized approximations*, *Journal of Number Theory* 268 (2025), 1–38, DOI: [10.1016/j.jnt.2024.09.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnt.2024.09.002). The general statement is Theorem 4; the trace dual is defined in Section 2.3, and the proof uses Section 6, Lemmas 18–19, and Section 7. An accessible [primary version](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.00173v2) is available.

The following theorem gives the norm-to-accumulation inclusion in the complete classification and retains injective sequence data stronger than the set equality.

::: {.theorem #thm-quadratic-norm-inclusion title="Nonzero trace-dual norms give genuine accumulation points" lean="D5/S3/Arith/QuadraticFields/QuadraticAccumulation.signed_norm_values_accumulate"}
Let $D\ge2$ be a squarefree integer, $A,B\in\mathbb Q$ with $B\ne0$, and $\alpha=A+B\sqrt D$. In the actual subfield $K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)\subset\mathbb R$, let $\Lambda^*$ be the field-trace dual of $\mathbb Z+\alpha\mathbb Z$. For every $s\in\Lambda^*\setminus\{0\}$, put $c=2B\sqrt D\,\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(s)$. For each $x\in\{c,-c\}$ there is an injective sequence $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(n)&\in\mathrm{NA}_1(\alpha),& f(n)&\ne x\quad(n\in\mathbb N),\\
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)&=x,& x&\in\mathcal A_1(\alpha).
\end{aligned}
$$

This includes negative $B$, both signs and every nonzero integer denominator, with no coprimality condition.
:::

::: proof
Choose a Pell multiplier of norm $1$ whose positive-root embedding exceeds $1$ and whose multiplication preserves the same trace-dual lattice. Powers of this multiplier acting on a fixed nonzero dual vector give integer numerators and denominators through their trace coordinates. After finitely many initial terms, the normalized approximations equal the target norm value plus a nonzero geometrically decreasing correction. Their values are therefore distinct and avoid the limit. Negating numerator and denominator together supplies the other sign. An algebra isomorphism from the coordinate quadratic algebra to the actual generated field preserves trace and norm and transports the construction to the original lattice in the statement. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

Now specialize to $\alpha=\varphi$. First compare the parameters in the two questions. Let $\mathcal C_\varphi$ be the set of all finite limits of $q_j\norm{q_j\varphi}$ along strictly increasing positive integers $q_j$. Given $x\in\mathcal A_1(\varphi)$, choose distinct set values tending to $x$. Bounded normalized errors allow only finitely many values at any finite collection of denominators, so we can extract a subsequence with strictly increasing $|q_j|$. Negate $q_j,p_j$ together when necessary to make the denominators positive, leaving the absolute errors unchanged. Since eventually $|q_j\varphi-p_j|=O(1/q_j)<1/2$, the integer $p_j$ is eventually the nearest integer. Thus $q_j\norm{q_j\varphi}\to|x|$.

Conversely, suppose positive integers $q_j$ increase strictly and $q_j\norm{q_j\varphi}\to L$. Choose a nearest integer $p_j$ for each $q_j$, and pass to a subsequence on which the error has constant sign. Then $q_j(q_j\varphi-p_j)\to x$ with $|x|=L$. These signed values are distinct: equality of values at positive denominators $q,r$ with corresponding integers $p,s$ would imply

$$
(q^2-r^2)\varphi=qp-rs;
$$

irrationality of $\varphi$ forces $q=r$. Hence $x$ is a genuine accumulation point of the set used in the source, so $x\in\mathcal A_1(\varphi)$. Together, the two directions give $\mathcal C_\varphi=\{|x|:x\in\mathcal A_1(\varphi)\}$.

The trace dual can also be computed by hand. The basis dual to $1,\varphi$ is $(\varphi-1)/\sqrt5,\ 1/\sqrt5$: the trace pairings of the first element with $1,\varphi$ are $1,0$, and those of the second are $0,1$. Thus

$$
\Lambda^*=\frac1{\sqrt5}(\mathbb Z+\varphi\mathbb Z).
$$

Write $z=a-b\varphi$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Z$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(a,b):=\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(z)&=a^2-ab-b^2,\\
\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\sqrt5)&=-5,\\
\operatorname N_{K/\mathbb Q}(z/\sqrt5)&=-Q(a,b)/5.
\end{aligned}
$$

The denominator's norm $-5$ determines the minus sign in the last identity. Taking $A=B=1/2$ and $D=5$ in [@thm-quadratic-accumulation], retaining both signs first and then taking absolute values, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal A_1(\varphi)
&=\left\{\pm\frac{Q(a,b)}{\sqrt5}:(a,b)\in\mathbb Z^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\right\},\\
\mathcal C_\varphi
&=\left\{\frac{|a^2-ab-b^2|}{\sqrt5}:(a,b)\in\mathbb Z^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}\right\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

A nonzero integer pair has nonzero norm, since both $a-b\varphi$ and its conjugate are nonzero. No coprimality condition is imposed here either. These formulas are written deductions from the golden specialization of the general classification theorem and the two-way correspondence above. The nearest-integer conversion and the golden trace-dual calculation themselves are not Lean declarations cited here.

::: {.remark #prob-revival-spectrum title="The literature result and the formalization boundary"}
The Lean verification in [@thm-revival-score] covers only Fibonacci times. The complete set of finite accumulation points for fixed $\varphi$ follows from [@thm-quadratic-accumulation] and the conversion above; this classification should therefore not be listed as a mathematically open problem. [@thm-quadratic-accumulation] now gives the complete signed accumulation-set equality for general quadratic irrationals in Lean; [@thm-quadratic-norm-inclusion] additionally retains injective approximation sequences. `GoldenRevivalScore` and `GoldenHurwitzBound` themselves still do not contain this equality. The specialized nearest-integer conversion and golden trace-dual calculation remain the written deductions above.

The phrases "full revival spectrum" and "global optimality" in `Blueprint/D5/S3/ObserverMemory/GoldenRevivalScore.md` are not precisely defined[^hurwitz]; the corresponding Lean file also explicitly disclaims a full Lagrange–Markov spectrum classification. The accumulation set for a fixed quadratic irrational differs from a spectrum problem in which $\alpha$ varies. The classification theorem for the former does not automatically settle those broader, unformulated statements. The [formalization request](https://github.com/the-omega-institute/trureturing-requests/issues/111) records the formalization of the general quadratic accumulation theorem and the scope of related declarations.
:::

[^hurwitz]: trureturing does not say exactly what "global optimality" means here; what follows is one possible reading. If it is read as "the lower limit of $q\norm{q\varphi}$ over all positive integers $q$ is $1/\sqrt5$", it is a classical result (A. Hurwitz, Ueber die angenäherte Darstellung der Irrationalzahlen durch rationale Brüche, *Math. Ann.* 39 (1891), 279–284). trureturing has frozen the bound needed for one half of it: `golden_hurwitz_bound` in the module `D5/S1/Depth/GoldenHurwitzBound` says that every rational number $r$ with reduced denominator $d$ satisfies $|\varphi - r| > 1/(\sqrt5 d^2 + d)$, from which a paper argument gives a lower limit of at least $1/\sqrt5$; on the other hand, $\norm{F_n\varphi} = |F_n\varphi - F_{n+1}|$ for $n \ge 2$, so [@thm-revival-score] gives at most $1/\sqrt5$. The combined statement about the lower limit is not contained in those cited frozen declarations and is not formalized in this book.

## A use for the deficit {#sec-almost-additive}

[@thm-deficit-three-valued] says that the deficit takes only the values $-1$, $0$ and $1$. This section applies that bound to the exponents of a prime factorization. For a prime $p$ and a positive integer $n$, write $v_p(n)$ for the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorization of $n$.

$\lambda_-(n)$
: For a positive integer $n$, $\lambda_-(n) = \sum_{p \mid n} \beta'(v_p(n)) \log p$, summed over the prime factors of $n$, where $\beta'$ is the contraction-face reading of Chapter 1 ([@def-beta-contraction]) and $\log$ is the natural logarithm. This is `lambdaMinus` in Lean; it is also defined for $n = 0$ (with value $0$), which we do not need.

$\operatorname{rad}(k)$
: The product of the distinct prime factors of a positive integer $k$, with $\operatorname{rad}(1) = 1$. This is `primeRadical` in Lean.

Replacing $\beta'$ by $v \mapsto v$ would turn the sum into $\sum_{p \mid n} v_p(n) \log p = \log n$, which is exactly additive under multiplication. $\beta'$ is not additive, and neither is $\lambda_-$: $\lambda_-(2) = \beta'(1) \log 2 = \psi^2 \log 2 \approx 0.2648$ and $\lambda_-(4) = \beta'(2) \log 2 = \psi^3 \log 2 \approx -0.1636$, so $\lambda_-(4) - 2\lambda_-(2) = (\psi^3 - 2\psi^2) \log 2 = -\log 2$. The next theorem bounds the discrepancy.

::: {.theorem #thm-almost-additive title="Almost additivity of $\lambda_-$" lean="D5/S1/Deficit/AlmostAdditivity.lambdaMinus_almost_additive"}
For all natural numbers $m, n$ with $m \ge 1$ and $n \ge 1$,

$$
\bigl| \lambda_-(mn) - \lambda_-(m) - \lambda_-(n) \bigr| \le \log \operatorname{rad}(\gcd(m, n)).
$$
:::

In particular, when $m$ and $n$ are coprime the right-hand side is $\log 1 = 0$, so $\lambda_-(mn) = \lambda_-(m) + \lambda_-(n)$.

::: proof
Fix a prime $p$ and write $a = v_p(m)$, $b = v_p(n)$, so that $v_p(mn) = a + b$. A prime dividing only one of $m$ and $n$ contributes nothing, because $\beta'(0) = 0$; a common prime $p$ contributes $\bigl(\beta'(a+b) - \beta'(a) - \beta'(b)\bigr) \log p$. By [@thm-deficit-integer], the deficit equals its contraction-face reading $c(a, b) = \beta'(a) + \beta'(b) - \beta'(a+b)$, so

$$
\lambda_-(mn) - \lambda_-(m) - \lambda_-(n) = -\sum_{p \mid \gcd(m, n)} c\bigl(v_p(m), v_p(n)\bigr) \log p .
$$

By [@thm-deficit-three-valued] each term has $|c| \le 1$; by the triangle inequality, the absolute value of the left-hand side is at most $\sum_{p \mid \gcd(m, n)} \log p = \log \operatorname{rad}(\gcd(m, n))$. The Lean proof takes the same steps, and the decisive one is [`deficit_three_valued`](lean:D5/S1/Deficit/DeficitThreeValued.deficit_three_valued). The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

The table lists a few cases computed from the identity above, with the values of $c$ taken from [@ex-small-deficits] in Chapter 1; the error column was also computed directly from the definition of $\lambda_-$. For all pairs with $1 \le m, n < 300$ the direct computation agrees with [@thm-almost-additive], and the error is $0$ whenever $m$ and $n$ are coprime. This is a finite check, not a proof.

| $m$ | $n$ | common primes $p$: $(v_p(m), v_p(n))$, $c$ | error | bound $\log \operatorname{rad}(\gcd(m, n))$ |
| ---: | ---: | :--- | ---: | ---: |
| 2 | 2 | $2$: $(1, 1)$, $+1$ | $-\log 2 \approx -0.693$ | $\log 2 \approx 0.693$ |
| 4 | 4 | $2$: $(2, 2)$, $-1$ | $\log 2 \approx 0.693$ | $\log 2 \approx 0.693$ |
| 2 | 4 | $2$: $(1, 2)$, $0$ | $0$ | $\log 2 \approx 0.693$ |

Here "error" means $\lambda_-(mn) - \lambda_-(m) - \lambda_-(n)$. The first, second and fourth rows attain the bound; in the fifth row the deficits at the two common primes have opposite signs and partly cancel.

$\lambda_-$ places the contraction-face reading on the exponents of primes. Placing the expansion-face reading there instead, and writing the result as an Euler product, leads to a question that is still open in trureturing. For a natural number $v$ let $S(v) = \lfloor (v+1)\varphi \rfloor - 1$ (the $s(v)$ of [@thm-beatty-deficit]), let $\psi = 1 - \varphi$, and

$$
\beta(v) = S(v) - v\psi .
$$

By the two closed forms in the proof of [@thm-beatty-deficit], this is exactly the expansion-face reading $\beta(v)$ of Chapter 1 (we also checked it value by value for $v < 500$); for example $\beta(1) = \varphi^2$ and $\beta(2) = \varphi^3$. For a complex number $s$, define the Euler germ

$$
E(s) = \prod_{p} \sum_{v=0}^{\infty} p^{-s\beta(v)}, \qquad p^{w} = e^{w \log p},
$$

the product running over all primes $p$.

Here $\beta(v)=S(v)-v\psi$ is exactly `o5Beta v` in the source. For a prime $p$, the original local factor is defined by

$$
\operatorname{germLocalFactor}(s,p)
=\sum_{v=0}^{\infty}p^{-s\beta(v)}.
$$

This follows the argument order $s,p$ of `germLocalFactor` in the source and includes the constant term $1$ at $v=0$; no correction factors have been extracted or divided out.

::: {.conjecture #conj-independence title="The independence conjecture O-5"}
There is a function $Z \colon \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ such that:

1. $Z$ is meromorphic on the right half-plane $\{s : \operatorname{Re} s > 0\}$;
2. $Z(s) = E(s)$ for every $s$ with $\operatorname{Re} s > 1/\varphi^2$;
3. for every $s$ with $1/(2\varphi^3) < \operatorname{Re} s < 1/\varphi^2$: if $Z$ is analytic at $s$ and $Z(s) = 0$, then $\operatorname{Re} s = 1/(2\varphi^2)$.
:::

The three constants are $1/\varphi^2 \approx 0.382$, $1/(2\varphi^2) \approx 0.191$ and $1/(2\varphi^3) \approx 0.118$. Meromorphic and analytic are meant in Mathlib's sense[^conv].

Put $u = \varphi^2 s$. Since $\beta(1) = \varphi^2$, the $v = 1$ term of $E(s)$ is $p^{-\varphi^2 s} = p^{-u}$; the band in condition 3 becomes $1/(2\varphi) < \operatorname{Re} u < 1$, and the line becomes $\operatorname{Re} u = 1/2$ (the trureturing Blueprint page GoldenSpectralCoordinate records this change of scale). In this coordinate the line sits where the critical line of the Riemann $\zeta$ function does; this is only a formal analogy, and the trureturing README states plainly that the repository does not establish the Riemann hypothesis. $E$ is also related to $\lambda_-$: expanding the product formally (ignoring convergence), each positive integer $n$ contributes exactly one term $\prod_{p \mid n} p^{-s\beta(v_p(n))}$; by [@lem-two-faces], $\beta(v) = \beta'(v) + \sqrt5\, v$, so this term equals $n^{-\sqrt5 s} e^{-s\lambda_-(n)}$, and formally

$$
E(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^{-\sqrt5 s} e^{-s\lambda_-(n)} .
$$

This is only a formal manipulation; the book does not discuss where it holds.

O-5 is an open problem, not a theorem, and this book cites no Lean declaration for it. trureturing records it as `o5_independence` in `Hearts.lean`, whose proof is `sorry`; the module comment says the declaration records the open claim without asserting that it has been proved. The trureturing README and VISION documents both list O-5 as still having open obligations.

trureturing proves two results about O-5 in the module `GoldenWindowZeroObstruction`, and neither settles it. One says: if some function is analytic on a half-plane $\operatorname{Re} s > r$ (with $0 < r < 1/\varphi^2$), equals $E$ for $\operatorname{Re} s > 1/\varphi^2$, and has a zero inside that half-plane and inside the band of condition 3 whose real part differs from $1/(2\varphi^2)$, then no candidate that satisfies conditions 1 and 2 and, on $\operatorname{Re} s > r$, also a normal-form condition (Mathlib's `MeromorphicNFOn`) satisfies condition 3. The other says: changing the value of a meromorphic candidate at a single point makes it non-analytic there, which evades condition 3 at that point. The Blueprint concludes that the first does not refute the original O-5 statement, and that the second turns a limitation of that statement into a theorem.

For one local factor, the following theorem locates a zero without additional hypotheses.

::: {.theorem #thm-o5-prime-two-local-zero title="A zero of the original local factor at prime $2$" lean="D5/S3/Analytic/GermWindow/GermZeroCertificate.germLocalFactor_two_has_zero_near_candidate"}
Let the complex center be

$$
c=\frac{23815329946211908}{10^{17}}
+\frac{5256712292901926}{10^{15}}\,i.
$$

There exists $z\in\mathbb C$ such that

$$
\|z-c\|<10^{-8},\qquad
\operatorname{germLocalFactor}(z,2)=0.
$$
:::

::: proof
Put $G(s)=\sum_{v=0}^{60}2^{-s\beta(v)}$, written `g 60 s` in the source. Rational enclosures for the real and imaginary parts of all $61$ terms and their derivatives give $\|G(c)\|<4/10^{10}$ and $\operatorname{Re}G'(c)>187/100$ after summation. On the closed square centered at $c$ with half-side $1/(2\cdot10^8)$, the curvature bound $\|G''(s)\|\le400$ controls the first-order Taylor remainder, while the uniform tail bound $\|\operatorname{germLocalFactor}(s,2)-G(s)\|<58/10^{11}$ controls the truncation error. The affine comparison function $G(c)+G'(c)(s-c)$ has a simple zero in the square, and its modulus on the boundary exceeds the sum of these errors. The existing rectangle Rouché reduction in `GermZeroCertificateReduction` therefore gives a zero of the original local factor in that square, which lies inside the stated open ball. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

The theorem states only existence in this exact open ball. Its position also follows directly from elementary rational bounds: the center and radius satisfy

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac15&<\operatorname{Re}c-10^{-8},\\
\operatorname{Re}c+10^{-8}&<\frac14.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $|\operatorname{Re}z-\operatorname{Re}c|\le\|z-c\|<10^{-8}$, every point in the ball has real part in $(1/5,1/4)$. Using $8/5<\varphi<5/3$, $\varphi^2=\varphi+1$ and $\varphi^3=2\varphi+1$ gives $1/(2\varphi^3)<1/8$, $1/(2\varphi^2)<1/5$ and $1/\varphi^2>1/3$. The entire ball therefore lies in the original window of condition 3, to the right of the structural line $\operatorname{Re}s=1/(2\varphi^2)$; this placement is a consequence stated outside the theorem box.

There is indeed cancellation within the local factor, but the theorem asserts neither $E(z)=0$ for the original infinite product defined by `tprod` nor a zero of a continued germ. The ball lies outside the original agreement domain in condition 2. Transferring a local zero to a continuation of the full product still requires a suitable factorization and analytic or meromorphic continuation identities, together with control of the remaining product at that point and any cancellation between zeros and poles. The normal-form and single-point modification limitations above still apply: this theorem does not refute the original O-5 statement with its bare `MeromorphicOn` requirement, and it neither proves nor refutes the Riemann hypothesis.

::: {.remark #rem-o5-continuation-route title="A continuation route for O-5 and its remaining interfaces"}
**Literature result, not formalized in this book.** The multivariable Euler product method of Bhowmik–Essouabri–Lichtin (BEL) offers a route to continuation[^bel-continuation]. For finitely many analytic functions $\ell_j$ and integers $a_j$, the proof of their Lemma 1 allows local factors of the form $1+\sum_j a_jp^{-\ell_j(s)}+R_p(s)$, where $R_p$ is analytic on the target domain and uniformly $O(p^{-2})$ in both the prime $p$ and $s$ in that domain. On $W(\ell;\delta)=\{s:\operatorname{Re}\ell_j(s)>\delta\text{ for all }j\}$ ($\delta>0$), repeated cancellation of low-order terms extracts finitely many integer powers of $\zeta$ functions, leaving a holomorphic, absolutely convergent remainder product. Integer powers keep the extracted factors meromorphic; this is an established literature method whose application to O-5 has not been completed in this book.

The parameter correspondence starts from $\beta(v)=\lfloor(v+1)/\varphi\rfloor+v\varphi\ge\sqrt5\,v-1$. Write $H_\epsilon=\{s:\operatorname{Re}s>\epsilon\}$. Given $0<\epsilon<1/\varphi^2$, choose $M\ge1$ with $\epsilon(\sqrt5(M+1)-1)\ge2$; the truncation interface still to establish is analyticity of the tail on $H_\epsilon$ together with the uniform bound

$$
\left|\sum_{v>M}p^{-s\beta(v)}\right|
\le \frac{p^{-2}}{1-2^{-\epsilon\sqrt5}}.
$$

The paper reasoning behind this estimate bounds each term by $p^{-\epsilon(\sqrt5\,v-1)}$ and sums a geometric series with ratio $p^{-\epsilon\sqrt5}$; analyticity requires a connection to locally uniform convergence. The retained weights are $b_v=\beta(v)$ ($1\le v\le M$), with $\ell_v(s)=b_vs$ and $a_v=1$. Their minimum is $\beta(1)=\varphi^2$, so BEL's domains correspond here to

$$
W(\ell;\epsilon\varphi^2)=H_\epsilon,
\qquad W(\ell;1)=H_{1/\varphi^2}.
$$

These parameters specify a truncation target for the original germ. [@rem-o5-finite-cancellation] retains the complete finite algebraic argument, while [@thm-o5-finite-integer-cancellation] gives both cancellation to arbitrary finite order and its uniform analytic evaluation. Before applying that theorem, one must still establish the analyticity and uniform bound above for the actual infinite tail $R_p(s)=\sum_{v>M}p^{-s\beta(v)}$, and identify it with the original local factor minus the retained terms. One can then take $J=\{1,\ldots,M\}$, $a_v=1$, $b_v=\beta(v)$, $m=\varphi^2$, $A=(1-2^{-\epsilon\sqrt5})^{-1}$, and choose $N$ with $(N+1)\epsilon m\ge2$. The finite theorem allows repeated or rationally dependent weights and controls the denominators introduced by negative correction exponents on this half-plane; grouping equal-weight multi-indices is unnecessary for this step.

One must still establish the corresponding factorization identities involving $\zeta$ functions, prove locally uniform convergence of the remainder product over primes, construct a continuation for **every** $\epsilon>0$, and make normal-form meromorphic representatives compatible on overlapping half-planes to obtain a function on $H_0$; one fixed truncation does not suffice. Condition 2 also requires equality with $E(s)$ at **every point** of the original domain $H_{1/\varphi^2}$, including the values assigned at apparent singularities that are removable. Equality of meromorphic germs does not ensure pointwise equality of arbitrary functions satisfying only `MeromorphicOn`, so the normal-form and single-point modification limitations above remain relevant. These interfaces for the original tail, infinite products and gluing steps have not been formalized in this book. The finite theorem also gives no nonvanishing assertion for local factors or the product, and no zero localization as required by condition 3. O-5 remains open, and condition 3 requires separate study.
:::

::: {.remark #rem-o5-finite-cancellation title="A finite algebraic form of integer Euler cancellation"}
**The published infinite expansion and the complete finite cancellation argument.** Delabarre's §2.2, Lemma 2, gives a published expansion with integer exponents[^delabarre-cancellation]. Relabel its variables by a finite set $J$, with $a_j\in\mathbb Z$ and $S=\sum_{j\in J}|a_j|$. If $S>0$ and every $|Y_j|<1/S$, then

$$
Q(Y):=1+\sum_{j\in J}a_jY_j
=\prod_{\nu\in\mathbb N^J\setminus\{0\}}(1-Y^\nu)^{\gamma(\nu)},
\qquad \gamma(\nu)\in\mathbb Z,
$$

where $Y^\nu=\prod_jY_j^{\nu_j}$, with absolute convergence on the right. Equation (6) gives the exponents explicitly, and Remark 4 notes that their integrality can be proved by induction on total degree. If $S=0$ (including empty $J$), then $Q=1$ and all exponents may be taken to be zero, without using $1/S$. Writing the expansion as $Q\prod_\nu(1-Y^\nu)^{c_\nu}=1$ sets the correction sign convention $c_\nu=-\gamma(\nu)$. The **complete written argument** below retains this cancellation method after clearing denominators and explains the algebraic part of [@thm-o5-finite-integer-cancellation]. The finite polynomial conclusion is not the literal statement of Lemma 2 and claims no new literature method; the published infinite expansion above is not a conclusion of the finite Lean theorem below.

Work in $\mathbb Z[X_j:j\in J]$, write $|\nu|=\sum_j\nu_j$ and $X^\nu=\prod_jX_j^{\nu_j}$, and take any $N\in\mathbb N$. Consider the finite set

$$
E_N=\{\nu\in\mathbb N^J:1\le|\nu|\le N\}
$$

There are integers $c_\nu$ on this set. Put $c_\nu^+=\max(c_\nu,0)$ and $c_\nu^-=\max(-c_\nu,0)$. They give a finite polynomial identity

$$
\begin{aligned}
U&=\prod_{\nu\in E_N}(1-X^\nu)^{c_\nu^+},\\
V&=\prod_{\nu\in E_N}(1-X^\nu)^{c_\nu^-},\\
QU&=V+\sum_{t\in T}h_tX^t,
\qquad |t|\ge N+1\quad(t\in T).
\end{aligned}
$$

Here take $H_{\mathrm{poly}}=QU-V$, $T=\operatorname{supp}(H_{\mathrm{poly}})$ and $h_t=[X^t]H_{\mathrm{poly}}\in\mathbb Z$; every power has a natural-number exponent, and a negative $c_\nu$ places its factor in $V$. In particular, for $N=0$, $E_N$ is empty, $U=V=1$, and the remainder is $Q-1$. Its constant coefficient is zero, but the remainder need not vanish.

For a proof, let $\mathcal F_d$ consist of polynomials whose coefficients in total degrees below $d$ vanish. Degrees add in every term of the finite coefficient convolution, so $\mathcal F_d$ is closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication by any polynomial, and

$$
\mathcal F_r\mathcal F_s\subseteq\mathcal F_{r+s},
\qquad \mathcal F_{d+1}\subseteq\mathcal F_d.
$$

Start with $U=V=1$, so $H=QU-V=Q-1\in\mathcal F_1$, and both $U,V$ have constant coefficient $1$. Suppose we have reached layer $d\ge1$ with $H\in\mathcal F_d$. Since $J$ is finite, so is $L_d=\{\nu:|\nu|=d\}$. Set $r_\nu=[X^\nu]H$ and $h_d=\sum_{\nu\in L_d}r_\nu X^\nu$, and take

$$
\begin{aligned}
A&=\prod_{\nu\in L_d}(1-X^\nu)^{\max(r_\nu,0)},\\
B&=\prod_{\nu\in L_d}(1-X^\nu)^{\max(-r_\nu,0)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The binomial expansion for natural powers gives $(1-X^\nu)^m=1-mX^\nu$ plus terms in $\mathcal F_{2d}$. Choosing two or more nonconstant terms in the finite products also gives degree at least $2d$. Since $2d\ge d+1$,

$$
A-1,\ B-1\in\mathcal F_d,
\qquad A-B+h_d\in\mathcal F_{d+1}.
$$

Update $U,V$ to $UA,VB$. To account for interactions between the old denominator factors in $V$ and the new layer, use the exact identity

$$
\begin{aligned}
QUA-VB
&=HA+V(A-B)\\
&=H+(A-B)+H(A-1)+(V-1)(A-B).
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $H-h_d\in\mathcal F_{d+1}$, so $H+(A-B)\in\mathcal F_{d+1}$, while $H(A-1)\in\mathcal F_{2d}\subseteq\mathcal F_{d+1}$. The old $V$ has constant coefficient $1$, giving $V-1\in\mathcal F_1$ and hence $(V-1)(A-B)\in\mathcal F_{d+1}$. Thus the new $H$ belongs to $\mathcal F_{d+1}$, and the updated $U,V$ still have constant coefficient $1$. This cancels degree $d$ while preserving all cancellations in lower degrees.

Perform the steps $d=1,\ldots,N$, assigning $c_\nu=r_\nu$ on the disjoint layers. This gives the displayed $U,V$ and $H=QU-V\in\mathcal F_{N+1}$. Taking $T$ to be the finite support of the final $H$, with $h_t=[X^t]H$, gives the remainder and its degree bound. Each layer's correction exponents are determined uniquely by its residual coefficients, consistently with the literature sign $c=-\gamma$. For a hand calculation, $Q=1+X$ at $N=1$ gives $c_1=1$ and $H=-X^2$; at $N=2$, taking $c_2=-1$ then gives $U=1-X$, $V=1-X^2$, and $H=0$.

All these choices precede the introduction of analytic weights. For any $z=(z_j)_{j\in J}\in\mathbb C^J$, polynomial evaluation preserves finite sums, products and natural powers, so

$$
Q(z)U(z)=V(z)+\sum_{t\in T}h_tz^t
$$

holds at every $z$, even when some $1-z^\nu=0$; there is no division. After substituting $z_j=p^{-b_js}$, rational relations among weights may make distinct multi-indices coincide in weight, but do not invalidate this identity. This polynomial identity alone contains no uniform analytic estimate, divisibility or nonvanishing assertion. The following theorem separately gives a uniform analytic conclusion under explicit hypotheses. The obligations concerning the original tail, prime products, normal-form gluing and pointwise agreement remain as described in the preceding remark; finite cancellation settles neither condition 2 nor condition 3 of O-5.
:::

::: {.theorem #thm-o5-finite-integer-cancellation title="Finite integer Euler cancellation and uniform analytic evaluation" lean="D5/S3/Analytic/EulerGerm/FiniteIntegerEulerCancellation.finite_integer_euler_cancellation"}
For every finite set $J$, every integer coefficient family $a:J\to\mathbb Z$, and every $N\in\mathbb N$, there exists $c:E_N\to\mathbb Z$ for which all the following conclusions hold. The set $J$ may be empty, $N$ may be $0$, and both $a_j$ and $c(u)$ have arbitrary signs. Write $|u|=\sum_j u_j$ and $E_N=\{u:J\to\mathbb N:1\le|u|\le N\}$. For this $c$, define

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q&=1+\sum_j a_jX_j,\\
U&=\prod_{u\in E_N}(1-X^u)^{\max(c(u),0)},\\
V&=\prod_{u\in E_N}(1-X^u)^{\max(-c(u),0)},\\
H_{\mathrm{poly}}&=QU-V,\qquad T=\operatorname{supp}(H_{\mathrm{poly}}),\\
h_t&=[X^t]H_{\mathrm{poly}}\quad(t\in T).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $T$ is the actual finite polynomial support. Let $d$ be the finite `Data` with index sets $J,E_N,T$ and fields

$$
\begin{aligned}
d.\mathrm{linear}(j)&=a_j,&d.\mathrm{mode}(u,j)&=u_j,\\
d.\mathrm{power}(u)&=c(u),&d.\mathrm{tailMode}(t,j)&=t_j,\\
d.\mathrm{tailCoeff}(t)&=h_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is exactly `tailData a (polynomialMode N) c`; `polynomialMode` converts an ordinary function on the finite set to the same finitely supported multi-index.

1. For every $u:J\to\mathbb N$, if $|u|\le N$, then $[X^u]H_{\mathrm{poly}}=0$.
2. The data $d$ satisfy `Data.Certificate`: for every $z\in\mathbb C^J$,

   $$
   Q(z)U(z)=V(z)+\sum_{t\in T}h_tz^t.
   $$

   This includes zeros of the correcting binomials $1-z^u$; the identity uses only natural powers and involves no division.
3. For every $u\in E_N$, $1\le\sum_jd.\mathrm{mode}(u,j)\le N$.
4. For every $t\in T$, $N+1\le\sum_jd.\mathrm{tailMode}(t,j)$.
5. For every $b:J\to\mathbb R$ and every $m,\epsilon,A\in\mathbb R$ satisfying

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &m>0,\qquad b_j\ge m\quad(j\in J),\\
   &\epsilon>0,\qquad A\ge0,\qquad 2\le(N+1)\epsilon m,
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   there exists $C\in\mathbb R$, $C\ge0$, such that for every family $R:\operatorname{Primes}\to(\mathbb C\to\mathbb C)$, if for all primes $p$,

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\operatorname{AnalyticOnNhd}_{\mathbb C}(R_p,H_\epsilon),\\
   &\|R_p(s)\|\le A/p^2\quad(s\in H_\epsilon),
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   then for all primes $p$,

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   &\operatorname{AnalyticOnNhd}_{\mathbb C}(K_p,H_\epsilon),\\
   &\|K_p(s)-1\|\le C/p^2\quad(s\in H_\epsilon).
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   Here $H_\epsilon=\{s\in\mathbb C:\operatorname{Re}s>\epsilon\}$ and

   $$
   \begin{aligned}
   z_j(p,s)&=\exp(-b_js\log p),\\
   K_p(s)&=\bigl(Q(z(p,s))+R_p(s)\bigr)
   \prod_{u\in E_N}(1-z(p,s)^u)^{c(u)}.
   \end{aligned}
   $$

   The logarithm is the real logarithm of the positive real number $p$. This $K_p$ is exactly `Data.corrected`, with integer powers. `AnalyticOnNhd` means complex analyticity in a neighborhood of each point of the set.

The quantifier order is as follows: $J,a,N$ are given first, and then $c$ is chosen; the same $c$ works for every $b,m,\epsilon,A$. Once those four parameters are fixed and satisfy the hypotheses, $C$ is chosen before the entire perturbation family $R$; it works for all primes and the whole half-plane. Equality is allowed in the cutoff, and the weights need not be distinct or rationally independent.
:::

::: proof
The complete layer argument in [@rem-o5-finite-cancellation] explains the algebraic part. Lean expresses the total-degree filtration through powers of the variable ideal, cancels each layer, and fills unused modes in $E_N$ with zero exponents. Passing between finitely supported and ordinary function multi-indices preserves total degree. Every coefficient in the residual's actual support is nonzero, so conclusion 1 gives conclusion 4; evaluating the polynomial over that support gives the certificate for every complex vector in conclusion 2.

The analytic part reuses `evaluated_certificate_uniform`. Put $q=\exp(-m\epsilon\log2)<1$. On $H_\epsilon$, every correcting monomial has modulus at most $q$, so $1-z^u$ is nonzero and its integer powers are analytic there. Set

$$
\begin{aligned}
U_0&=\prod_{u\in E_N}(1+q)^{\max(c(u),0)},\\
V_0&=\prod_{u\in E_N}(1-q)^{\max(-c(u),0)}>0,\\
D_0&=\sum_{t\in T}|h_t|.
\end{aligned}
$$

The degree gap and $(N+1)\epsilon m\ge2$ bound each tail monomial by $p^{-2}$. On this half-plane, where $V(z)\ne0$ has been established, the certificate gives

$$
K_p(s)-1=\frac{\sum_{t\in T}h_tz^t+R_p(s)U(z)}{V(z)}.
$$

Thus one may take $C=(D_0+AU_0)/V_0$, independently of $R,p,s$. Division is used only for the estimate on this analytic domain; the polynomial certificate for every complex vector makes no claim that division remains valid at binomial zeros. The full proof is checked by the Lean kernel.
:::

This result constructs finite algebraic corrections and gives a per-prime analytic estimate under its hypotheses. It does not establish those hypotheses for the actual infinite $\beta$ tail above, construct an infinite prime product or a global continuation, or assert that $K_p$ or the product is nonzero. The local zero in [@thm-o5-prime-two-local-zero] and the normal-form and single-point modification limitations retain their stated scope. Neither O-5 nor the Riemann hypothesis is proved or refuted by this result.
